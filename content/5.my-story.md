I am Swarnil. I built this platform because nobody built it for me, and the four
years I lost were not lost to a lack of talent. They were lost to not knowing
what to do next.

There is no tidy version of this. I have left the numbers in, including the ones
that are embarrassing, because the numbers are the only part of a story like this
that cannot be inspirational nonsense.

## Always seventy percent {#average}

I was an average student. Not the kind who fails and has a story about it. The
kind who gets seventy percent in everything, every year, and is therefore
invisible. Seventy in class ten. Seventy in class twelve. PCM, because that is
what you take.

Then Kota, because clearing IIT or NIT was the thing you were supposed to want. I
went. I tried. I failed.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-quote" color="neutral"}
Nobody tells you that Kota is a filter, not a school. It is designed so that most
people fail. I did not know that. I thought I had found out something about
myself.
::

## The branch I picked {#branch}

I took admission at LNCT Bhopal, in computer science.

I want to be precise about why, because it is the single most important fact in
this story. It was not passion. It was not aptitude. I had never written a line
of code.

My brother and my guardians told me that in CSE, getting a job is easy and the
market is good. That was the entire basis of a decision that shaped the next ten
years of my life.

::note{icon="i-lucide-info"}
I am not saying that was wrong. It turned out fine. I am saying this is how most
people actually choose, and every curriculum is written as though they chose out
of love. That mismatch is where four years go.
::

## Four years of pretending {#pretending}

I completed college. I studied. I did not enjoy a single subject.

Operating systems. DBMS. Java. Data structures. Computer networks. The exact
subjects I now know are the whole game. I sat through all of them and understood
almost none of it, and nobody in that room ever connected one of them to a job I
might one day do.

::illustration{name="foundations" caption="What those subjects actually are. Not what they felt like at the time."}
::

I passed. Passing and understanding are different things, and the marksheet does
not have a column for it.

## The mask {#mask}

In the final year I found the thing I actually wanted. I wanted to be a YouTuber.
A creator. I started a channel, and for the first time since school there was
something I would stay up for.

And I told nobody.

I got to a thousand subscribers hiding behind a mask, because I did not have the
courage to say out loud, in my family, in my town, that I wanted to work in a
creative field. You can want something very badly and still not be able to say
its name at dinner.

## Rejected in round one {#round-one}

Placements came. I was not really interested in a job. I was interested in the
channel. But I tried anyway, because everybody tries.

Java. Quantitative aptitude. Written English. Reasoning.

I failed badly. Not "close but not selected" badly. I could not clear the **first
written round**. Not once, in four years.

::illustration{name="resume" caption="Nine seconds. That is roughly how long a first-round filter looks at you."}
::

The one exception was Tech Mahindra. I got through. I kept getting through. I
reached the HR round. I was rejected.

I remember exactly how that felt, and the word for it is loser. Not disappointed.
Not unlucky. Loser.

College ended. No job. My family had no plan and neither did I.

## The kitchen table {#kitchen-table}

You know the conversation. Everyone from a town like mine knows the conversation.
You are home, the degree is finished, and someone asks what next.

My father had an answer ready: BTC. Become a UP government teacher. My academic
marks were good enough, a government job is secure, and it is a respectable thing
to be.

It was not a bad plan. It was the only plan, and that is a different thing.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-wallet" color="warning"}
I need to say this part plainly, because most advice is written by people who
never had to. My father could not spend money on this. We did not have it. That
is not a footnote to my options. It **was** my options.
::

I said nothing about YouTube. Obviously.

## My sister said no {#my-sister}

Then my sister stepped in.

She said: my brother is not going to live here in Mahroni. He is going to
Bangalore. He will study there and attend walk-ins.

That is the sentence this entire platform comes from. Somebody in the room
refused to accept the default on my behalf, at a point when I did not have the
standing to refuse it myself. She was earning fifteen thousand rupees a month and
sending me eight of it.

::compare{caption="Not a choice between them. A sequence."}
  :::compare-side{label="What I told myself I was giving up" verdict="neutral"}
  YouTube. The channel. The only thing I had enjoyed in four years.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What I actually decided" verdict="right"}
  Everything goes into getting a job. The passion is not cancelled, it is
  **queued**. Once there is a salary, weekends are for the channel.
  :::
::

Before I left, I shot my first vlog.

## Unskilled, with a degree in it {#unskilled}

I got to Bangalore. Training, and then two or three walk-ins.

They went badly, and this time the reason was not mysterious. I sat in those
rooms and understood something specific and useful: **I was not skilled. I had a
degree in this and I could not do it.**

I tried to fix it by self-study. It did not work, and I know exactly why. I had
never understood programming in college, so there was no foundation for the
self-study to sit on. You cannot revise your way to something you never learned
the first time.

::real-life{title="Why self-study failed for me, and might for you" source="Bangalore, 2018"}
Self-study assumes you can tell the difference between "I do not know this yet"
and "I have misunderstood something upstream". I could not. Every tutorial made
sense while I watched it and evaporated the moment I closed the tab, and I had no
way to find out which of the twenty things I half-knew was the one breaking
everything else.
::

## Learning it properly {#properly}

So I joined a training institute. It cost ₹40,000, which was money we did not
have.

Java. SQL. HTML and CSS. DBMS. OOP. And this time it went in, taught in a way
where I started to actually understand the language rather than memorise the
shape of it.

Two people are responsible for that, and I am naming them because they will never
show up in a search result and they should.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-graduation-cap" title="Shishir Bhatt sir"}
  Taught me Java in a way that finally made the language make sense, rather than
  making me copy it.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-database" title="S. N. Dixit sir"}
  Taught me SQL. I am not writing SQL every day now, and I still use how he
  taught me to think about a query.
  :::
::

Here is what I actually did, and it matters more than the institute did.

- I never bunked a single class. Not one.
- I made my own notes. Not the handouts. Mine.
- I worked out my own way of answering interview questions, and wrote the answers
  down.
- I wrote **my own example programs** to bring into interviews, so that when
  somebody asked about inheritance I had a thing I had built and could explain,
  rather than the textbook's `Animal → Dog`.

::tech{names="java,sql,html,css,oops" labelled}
::

## One in a thousand {#one-in-a-thousand}

I started giving interviews. And one day I got one.

A startup. Web developer. I was told I was the one selected out of approximately a
thousand people. The package was **1.8 LPA**. Thirteen thousand rupees a month.

I was happy, and I want to be honest about why that number made me happy: my
sister was earning fifteen thousand and sending me eight of it. Thirteen thousand
meant I could live on my own.

My sister was not happy. She always thought I deserved better.

::illustration{name="code" caption="What I was hired to do. Six months earlier I could not have done any of it."}
::

## Tell me about yourself {#tell-me}

I kept the job and kept the habit. Weekends: study, walk-ins, interviews.

Three months later: **Accenture. 3.6 LPA, ₹24,000 in hand.** And I got the things
the startup could not give me: an orientation, a batch, friends. I was trained in
Salesforce. I worked on three different projects.

One of those friends was Durgesh, who had come up the same way I did. When he got
his first job I asked him what mattered, and his answer is the single most useful
thing anybody told me in those years.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-message-circle" color="primary"}
**"Tell me about yourself."**

That is what decides whether you get selected. Not the algorithm question. The
first four words, and the two minutes after them.
::

## The number that changed everything {#the-number}

Three years later Durgesh switched jobs and got **21 LPA**. I was on 5.5.

I remember the feeling exactly, and it was not envy. It was: *what am I doing?*
Same start, same walk-ins, same nothing. He had moved and I had not.

So I changed how I prepared. This is what happened when somebody with my
background finally learned to interview properly.

More than one lakh rupees a month, from thirteen thousand. I kept learning
anyway, which turned out to matter.

## Leaving anyway {#leaving}

I was not happy at Cognizant. Micromanagement, and the way employees were
treated. After seven months I decided to leave.

Seven months. I want that on the record, because the advice everybody gives is
"stay two years, it looks bad otherwise".

I cracked **PwC, at 21 LPA**. And then **Twilio, at 32 LPA with stocks**.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-trending-up" color="success"}
₹13,000 a month → ₹32 LPA with stocks. It looks unreal written down. It looked
unreal to me while it was happening.
::

Twilio is where I learned what a product company culture actually is, which is a
different thing from what people tell you it is. I was doing analytics in
Salesforce, in Go-To-Market, for three-plus years.

## The call from London {#london}

Then a call from London. A company was looking for my skillset, in Budapest.
Would I be interested?

I said yes. I cracked it. I am now in Europe, working as a Salesforce engineer.

::illustration{name="versioning" caption="Every branch here started from the same trunk: Java and SQL, learned properly, once."}
::

Here is the part I most want you to take.

**The foundation was the thing.** I started with Java and SQL. I am not writing
much of either now, and how to think about a query, which S. N. Dixit sir taught
me, is what I use in Salesforce every week. The architecture I learned in a
classroom in Bangalore is the architecture under a platform I had never heard of
at the time.

Tools expire. The layer underneath them does not, and in 2026 that is truer than
it has ever been: a machine will write the tool for you, and it will not know
whether the answer is right.

## Why this exists {#why}

I do not know how you landed here.

Job Seekers Guide is for people like me. People with potential who are lost.
People who have the capability, and no guidance, and possibly no money, and a
family conversation waiting for them about a safer plan.

I was not talented. I was not passionate about software. I did not clear a single
written round in college. What I eventually got was a **sequence**: somebody
telling me what to learn, in what order, and how to talk about it. That sequence
cost ₹40,000 at an institute in Bangalore, plus a year I did not need to lose.

That is what this platform is. The sequence, given away.
