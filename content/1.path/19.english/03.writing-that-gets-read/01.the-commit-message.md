Everything you have just learned to read, somebody else has to read from you.
Start with the smallest piece of writing in the job and the one you will do most:
the line you attach to every change you ever make.

## Why this matters in a job

Because a commit message is the only explanation of *why* a change exists. The
code says what it does. Nothing else in the repository says why anybody wanted it
to, and in two years that question is asked constantly and answered by nobody.

::real-life{title="The afternoon I lost to 'fix'" source="A production bug, year two"}
The fee calculation had a strange condition in it: applicants from one particular
programme were charged differently. It looked like a mistake. I removed it. It was
not a mistake, it was a rule from a government scholarship scheme, and I found
that out from an angry email rather than from `git log`, because the commit that
introduced it said "fix". One sentence would have saved a day and an apology.
::

## The shape

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Three parts. Most commits need only the first."}
  :::flow-step{label="A subject line, in the imperative" icon="i-lucide-type" highlight}
  Under about seventy characters. Written as an instruction: *"Count applicants
  rather than applications on the dean's dashboard"*. Read it as completing the
  sentence "this commit will…" and it always reads correctly.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="A blank line" icon="i-lucide-minus"}
  Not optional. Every tool that displays git history treats the first line as a
  title and needs the blank line to know where it ends.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The body: why, not what" icon="i-lucide-align-left"}
  What was happening before, why that was wrong, and anything you decided that a
  reader could not work out from the diff. The diff already shows what changed.
  :::
::

## Watch it happen

Same change, two messages.

::compare{caption="The change is four lines. The message is what somebody finds in two years."}
  :::compare-side{label="What most people write" verdict="wrong"}
  ```text
  fixed count bug
  ```

  In eighteen months somebody sees a `DISTINCT` in the query, wonders whether it
  is needed, finds this message, learns nothing, and either leaves it in out of
  fear or removes it and reintroduces the bug.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What takes ninety more seconds" verdict="right"}
  ```text
  Count applicants rather than applications on the dean's dashboard

  The intake total was reading 2,040 when the registrar's own figure was
  1,602. The dashboard counted rows in Application, and one person can
  hold three applications, so anybody applying to several programmes was
  counted several times.

  Counting distinct applicant_id instead. The two numbers are both
  legitimate and mean different things, so the label now says which one
  this is: "1,602 applicants, 2,040 applications".

  Reported by the registrar's office, 12 March.
  ```
  :::
::

Notice what the good one contains that the diff cannot: the number that was
wrong, the number it should have been, the reason, the fact that *both* counts
are legitimate, and who noticed.

## The rules

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-type" title="Imperative, present tense"}
  "Add", "Fix", "Remove", not "Added" or "Adding". Git's own generated messages
  are imperative, so yours reading the same way keeps the log consistent.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-scissors" title="One change per commit"}
  If your subject line needs an "and", it is two commits. This matters most when
  something goes wrong and somebody needs to undo one of the two.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="Why, not what"}
  The diff is the what. Spending the body restating it is the most common way a
  long commit message is still useless.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-link" title="Name the source"}
  The ticket number, the person who reported it, the date. It costs four words
  and it is how a future reader finds the rest of the story.
  :::
::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Never write a commit message that requires a conversation with you to understand.
"As discussed" is the worst message in software. The person reading it is in a
different year and cannot discuss anything with you.
::

## What confuses people here

That this is bureaucracy. It is the cheapest form of documentation that exists,
because it is attached permanently to the exact lines it explains and it cannot
drift out of date the way a wiki page does.

::side-note{kind="go-deeper"}
There is also a direct interview use. "Show me your GitHub" is a common request,
and a history of clear commit messages on a real project is startlingly rare and
immediately visible. It says more about how you would be to work with than the
code does, because the code could have been generated and the reasoning could
not.
::

## What you can do now

You can write a message that survives you. You will write several thousand of
these, so the habit is worth forming on your first commit rather than your
five-hundredth.

## Your turn

Write the commit message for this change. You have added a check that stops a
staff member shortlisting for a programme outside their own department, because
a lecturer in physics shortlisted eleven computer science applicants by accident
last week.

Subject line and body. Two minutes.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="One version" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  ```text
  Restrict shortlisting to a staff member's own department

  Shortlisting checked that the user was staff and nothing further, so
  any staff account could shortlist for any programme. A lecturer in
  physics shortlisted eleven CSE applicants on 3 April, believing the
  screen was filtered to their own department.

  Adding a department check in Shortlister rather than in the screen,
  because the screen is not where a rule like this can live: the
  endpoint is reachable directly.

  The eleven shortlistings from 3 April are still in the table. Removing
  them is a data fix, not this change, and the registrar is deciding
  whether to keep them.
  ```

  The last paragraph is the one most people would leave out, and it is the most
  valuable: it tells a future reader that the bad data exists and was a decision
  rather than an oversight.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why does the body of a commit message explain why rather than what?
2. **An interviewer asks: "what does good version control practice look like?"**
   Answer with commits rather than with branching strategies.

## The interview question this answers

**"How do you work with git in a team?"**

Most candidates answer with commands or with a branching model they read about.
A better answer starts smaller: small commits that each do one thing, messages
that say why, and never committing something you cannot explain. Then mention
that you write the message before the code sometimes, because if you cannot state
the change in one line you have not decided what you are doing yet. That last
point is unusual enough to be remembered.
