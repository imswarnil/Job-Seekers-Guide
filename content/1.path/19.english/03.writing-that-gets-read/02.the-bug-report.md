Something is wrong and you have to tell somebody. This is the message juniors get
most wrong, and it is wrong in a specific, fixable way: it describes a feeling
instead of an observation.

## Why this matters in a job

Because a bug report is a request for somebody's time, and how much of their time
it costs is decided entirely by you. "It is not working" costs forty minutes of
conversation. The same discovery, written properly, costs ninety seconds of
reading.

You will also receive these. Knowing the shape makes you the person who asks the
right two questions back.

## The three facts

Every bug report is the same three facts in the same order. Everything else is
optional.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Steps, expected, actual. In that order, always."}
  :::flow-step{label="What I did" icon="i-lucide-footprints"}
  The exact steps, in order, that anybody can repeat. Numbered. Including the
  data you used, because the bug is often in the data.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="What I expected" icon="i-lucide-target"}
  The part everybody omits, and the part that tells the reader whether it is a
  bug at all. Half of all reported bugs are misunderstandings, and this line is
  what reveals them.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="What actually happened" icon="i-lucide-alert-circle" highlight}
  The observation, not the interpretation. The exact number on the screen, the
  exact error text, the exact time. Not "it broke".
  :::
::

## Watch it happen

::compare{caption="The same discovery, reported two ways."}
  :::compare-side{label="What a junior sends" verdict="wrong"}
  > Hi, the dashboard is showing wrong numbers for the dean. I think there is
  > some issue with the query or maybe the data. Can you check?

  The reader now has to ask: which number, which dean, wrong compared with what,
  when, and does it happen every time. Five questions, probably spread over an
  afternoon because of time zones.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What takes four more minutes" verdict="right"}
  > **Applicant total on the dean's dashboard is higher than the registrar's
  > figure**
  >
  > **Steps**
  > 1. Log in as a dean account (used dean.cse@university.edu)
  > 2. Open Dashboard → Admissions → 2026 intake
  > 3. Read "Total applicants"
  >
  > **Expected:** 1,602, which is what the registrar's office report gives for
  > the same intake.
  >
  > **Actual:** 2,040. Reproduced three times, 10:15 today.
  >
  > **Note:** 2,040 is exactly the number of rows in `Application` for this
  > intake. 1,602 is the number of distinct `applicant_id` values. So I think
  > the dashboard is counting applications and calling them applicants, but I
  > have not confirmed which one the dean actually wants.
  :::
::

The last paragraph is what separates a good report from a correct one. It shows
your reasoning and it explicitly marks the part you have not verified, which
means nobody builds on your guess as though it were a fact.

## The rules

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-repeat" title="Reproduce it before reporting"}
  Three times. A bug you cannot repeat is a different and much harder kind of
  report, and saying "three times" tells the reader that immediately.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-quote" title="Paste the exact text"}
  The error message, in full, as text rather than as a description of it. Never
  "some error came".
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-split" title="Separate fact from theory"}
  "Actual: 2,040" is a fact. "I think it is counting applications" is a theory.
  Label them, because a theory presented as a fact sends somebody to the wrong
  file.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-user" title="Say who is affected"}
  One dean, every dean, or every user. This decides whether it is looked at now
  or on Thursday, and it is the question a manager asks first.
  :::
::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Never report a bug you have not reproduced yourself. "A user said it is not
working" is a message to be investigated, not a bug report, and passing it on
unchanged is how a team spends a day on something that turned out to be somebody's
expired login.
::

## What confuses people here

That a good report makes you look like you could not solve it yourself. The
opposite: a report with steps, expected, actual and a labelled theory reads as
somebody who investigated properly and stopped at the edge of what they could
confirm. That is exactly what a senior engineer does.

::real-life{title="What actually happened with this one" source="A dashboard, a dean, a Thursday"}
The theory was right and incomplete. The dashboard was counting applications, and
the dean genuinely wanted applications, and the *label* was the bug. The fix was
one word on the screen rather than anything in the query. That outcome was only
possible because the report said "I have not confirmed which one the dean wants"
instead of asserting that the query was broken.
::

## What you can do now

You can write a report that costs the reader ninety seconds, which is a
surprisingly rare and immediately noticed skill.

## Your turn

Write the report for this. You are testing the attendance screen. A student who
attended 18 of 24 classes is shown as 75 per cent, and the rule is that below 75
per cent is flagged. The student is flagged.

Steps, expected, actual, and one labelled theory.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="One version, and the bug underneath" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  > **Student at exactly 75% attendance is flagged as short**
  >
  > **Steps**
  > 1. Open Attendance → 2026 → Subject CS-201
  > 2. Find roll number 2026CSE0184 (attended 18 of 24)
  > 3. Read the percentage and the flag
  >
  > **Expected:** 75.0%, not flagged. The rule is "below 75%".
  >
  > **Actual:** 75% shown, and flagged as short. Reproduced three times.
  >
  > **Theory, unconfirmed:** 18/24 is 75.0 exactly, so the displayed value is
  > right. I suspect the flag is comparing against a stored value with more
  > precision, or is using `<=` where the rule says `<`. Two other students at
  > exactly 75% are also flagged; every student above 75% is not.

  The last line is the most useful thing in the report: by checking two more
  students the reporter has turned a single observation into a pattern, which
  narrows the search from the whole screen to one comparison operator.

  There is a second, nastier possibility worth knowing about now and meeting
  properly in the Java track: if the percentage is a `double`, 18/24 is exactly
  representable but many fractions are not, and a value that displays as 75 can
  be 74.99999999999999 underneath. That is why marks and money use
  `BigDecimal` and not `double`.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why is "what I expected" the line that reveals whether something is a bug at
   all?
2. **An interviewer asks: "a user says the application is slow. What do you do
   next?"**

## The interview question this answers

**"How do you report or investigate a problem?"**

The structure is the answer: reproduce it, establish what should happen, record
what does happen, and separate what I have confirmed from what I suspect. Then
add the thing that makes it sound real: say what you would check to distinguish
between two theories. Interviewers are listening for whether you can stop
guessing and start narrowing.
