The numbers and the reasoning are handled. The same paper has one more section
in it, and this is the one that beat me specifically, though I did not find out
it was beating me until years later.

Every campus drive I sat had the same first round: quantitative aptitude, logical
reasoning, and **written English**. I failed all of them. I assumed the problem
was that I could not code, because that is what I assumed a software job was
about. It took me until Bangalore to work out that I had been eliminated before
anybody looked at a single line of code I had written, partly by a comprehension
passage.

## Why this is track one and not an appendix

::pull-quote
This is not a soft skill sitting beside the job. It is the gate in front of it,
and then it is most of the job once you are through.
::

Look at what a working week actually contains. You read a specification somebody
wrote badly. You read an error message. You read forty thousand lines of code
that a person who has left the company wrote, which is English as much as it is
Java, because the names and the comments are what make it legible. Then you write
a message explaining what you found, to somebody who will read it for eleven
seconds.

::real-life{title="What the rejection actually was" source="Campus, 2018"}
I was rejected in round one, eleven times, and every time I concluded something
about my intelligence. What was true was much narrower and completely fixable: I
had never practised reading a passage under time pressure, and I had never
written anything for a stranger to act on. Nobody had told me those were the
wall, because nobody thinks of them as part of a computer science education.
::

## What this track is not

::compare{caption="Two things that get confused, constantly, and only one of them is being taught here."}
  :::compare-side{label="Accent and fluency" verdict="neutral"}
  How you sound. Whether you say "the" before every noun. Whether your grammar is
  native. This matters far less than you have been told, and no interviewer worth
  working for is marking it.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Clarity" verdict="right"}
  Whether the person reading you knows what you mean, what you want, and what to
  do next. This is entirely learnable, it is what is actually marked, and it is
  what this track is about.
  :::
::

If you are worried about your English, that worry is almost always about the
left-hand column. The left-hand column is not the problem. I have watched a
fluent speaker fail an interview for not answering the question, and I have
watched someone with an accent so thick I had to concentrate get the offer,
because every sentence he said was about the thing being asked.

## The four chapters

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="The gate, then the three places English is the job."}
  :::flow-step{label="The round that filtered me" icon="i-lucide-filter"}
  Comprehension under time pressure, the six grammar rules that are actually
  tested, vocabulary as a technical problem, and verbal reasoning. Taught as an
  exam to be passed, because that is what it is.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Reading like an engineer" icon="i-lucide-book-open"}
  A specification, a documentation page, an error message, and somebody else's
  code. Four kinds of reading, each with a technique, and none of them the kind
  you were taught in school.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Writing that gets read" icon="i-lucide-pen-line" highlight}
  A commit message, a bug report, a status update, an email, and a piece of
  documentation. The highest-leverage chapter on this platform: it is the
  cheapest way for a junior to be visibly good.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Speaking and being understood" icon="i-lucide-mic"}
  Stand-up, explaining a bug to somebody senior, asking for help without
  apologising for four sentences, and "tell me about yourself".
  :::
::

## How the university runs through it

Every example here is the same world as the rest of the course. You will write
the bug report for the applicant count that is wrong on the dean's dashboard, the
commit message for the fix, the email telling the registrar what happened, and
the two-minute explanation of the whole thing to a person who does not know what
a database is.

::note
Two weeks, and it is the only track in this course with no code in it at all. If
you are tempted to skip it, here is the honest trade: skipping costs you nothing
this month and quite possibly the first round of every application you make.
::

::callout{icon="i-lucide-arrow-right"}
Start with the thing that actually eliminated me. Not the interview, not the
code. A passage of text, six questions about it, and not enough time.
::
