You accepted. This is the end of the course and the beginning of the part nobody
writes a syllabus for, and it is where the difference between my career and
Durgesh's was made.

## The first week

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Five days. None of this is about code."}
  :::flow-step{label="Get it running, and write down every step" icon="i-lucide-play"}
  Your setup notes are the most useful thing a new joiner can produce, because
  the existing team has forgotten what is hard. Offer them to the next person
  and you have contributed in week one.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Read the folder tree, then trace one path" icon="i-lucide-route" highlight}
  The method from the English track: run it, read the structure, find the entry
  point, follow one feature all the way to the database and back.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Ask for a small first task" icon="i-lucide-check-square"}
  A bug, a copy change, a test. Shipping something in week one matters far more
  than its size, because it proves the whole path works for you.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Find out who knows what" icon="i-lucide-users"}
  Who owns which part, who is the person everybody asks, who is patient. Thirty
  minutes of conversation saves weeks.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Say what you do not know" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Early, while it is expected. In month one "I have not used Docker" is a fact.
  In month six it is a thing you concealed.
  :::
::

## The four habits

Not talent. These four, and they are the whole of what "good to work with" means.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-eye" title="Be legible"}
  Say what you are doing, where you are, and when something has changed. The
  engineer whose work is invisible until it appears is not trusted with anything
  urgent, because nobody can plan around them.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-clock" title="Ask at forty-five minutes"}
  Agree the number with yourself in advance. Sitting on a problem is not
  perseverance, it is spending the team's money on your discomfort.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-notebook-pen" title="Write things down"}
  The bug and its cause, the setup step that was missing, the reason behind the
  strange condition. In six months you will be the only person who knows, and a
  written note is how that becomes an asset rather than a dependency.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-check-check" title="Finish things"}
  Including the unglamorous last ten per cent: the tests, the error message, the
  note in the README. The reputation for finishing is the most valuable thing a
  junior can build and it is built entirely out of small things.
  :::
::

::pull-quote
There were two of us at the same level and he was the better programmer. He was
given less responsibility, and it took me a year to see why: nobody ever knew
where he was until he was finished. Surprise is the thing a manager is trying to
eliminate.
::

## The three-year question

This is the part I got wrong, and the part that cost the most.

::timeline{label="Two people, the same start"}
  :::timeline-item{title="Both of us, 2019" date="₹13,000 a month" state="done"}
  Same walk-ins, same rejections, same first job.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Year one" date="Both learning fast" state="done"}
  A first job teaches you an enormous amount regardless of where it is.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Years two and three" date="The divergence" state="current"}
  He kept deliberately learning and moved. I got comfortable, stopped pushing,
  and stayed. The work was fine. Nothing was wrong.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="2022" date="21 LPA against 5.5" state="done"}
  Same start, same ability, four times the salary. Not talent. He had understood
  the structure from the orientation track and I had not.
  :::
::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
The dangerous period is months twelve to thirty. The job has stopped being
frightening, you are competent at it, and the learning quietly flattens because
nothing forces it any more. That plateau is comfortable and it is expensive, and
almost nobody notices they are in it.
::

## When to move

Not a rule. Four honest signals, and one non-signal.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-repeat" title="The last six months taught you nothing new"}
  Not "no new framework". No new **problem**. That is the real measure.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-trending-down" title="You are well below market and a raise will not fix it"}
  In a service company the ceiling is the rate card, not your ability. The move
  is the raise, and knowing that is the orientation track paying off.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-users" title="Nobody there is better than you at anything"}
  A genuine reason to go, and an underrated one. You cannot learn from people
  you have already caught up with.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-heart-crack" title="It is affecting you outside work"}
  I left a job after seven months against every piece of conventional advice,
  and it was right. "Stay two years or it looks bad" is advice, not a law, and
  it has never once been raised with me since.
  :::
::

The non-signal: a bad month. Every job has them, and leaving during one usually
just relocates the problem.

## What to learn next

Not another language. You learned to program once; everything after this is a
dialect you can read in an afternoon.

::pros-cons
---
title: What actually compounds after the first job
pros:
  - Depth in one area — databases, distributed systems, front-end performance, security
  - The subjects in this course, properly: OS, networks and DBMS repay a second pass enormously
  - Reading real systems — open-source codebases in the domain you work in
  - Writing, which is the most under-invested skill in this industry
cons:
  - Collecting frameworks feels like progress and is the most common way a year is spent
  - Certifications rarely move a salary on their own, whatever the advertising says
  - Learning "a bit of everything" produces somebody who cannot be given anything hard
---
::

::tip
The single highest-return thing at eighteen months in: go back to the operating
systems, networks and DBMS tracks with a year of real experience behind you.
Everything that was abstract the first time is now attached to something you have
debugged, and it lands completely differently. This is the second pass those
subjects were built for.
::

## Where this course ends

You have a system you built, on a domain you own, with tests that catch you. You
can read code you did not write, explain something out loud to somebody who does
not know it, and write a message a busy person can act on. You can model data,
query it, ship it and secure it. You know what a model can and cannot be trusted
with, and you know when the right answer is not to use one.

That is more than I had in 2019, and I got a job with less.

::pull-quote
I was not talented and I was not passionate about software. What I eventually got
was a sequence: somebody telling me what to learn, in what order, and how to talk
about it. That is all this was.
::

## The last thing

Somebody is where you were two years ago, reading a rejection email and
concluding something about themselves. You now have something they do not have:
you know it was navigation and not ability.

Tell them. A README somebody can follow, an answer on a forum, forty minutes with
a junior who is stuck. It costs almost nothing and it is the entire reason this
course exists, because somebody did it for me and I would not have got here
otherwise.
