One problem, forty-five minutes, and somebody watching you not type. This round
has a reputation for testing algorithms and it mostly tests something else.

## Why this matters in a job

Because the thing being marked is not whether you reach the answer. It is whether
you can be *worked with* on a problem, and that is a performance you can
rehearse.

::pull-quote
Two candidates get the same problem. One solves it silently in thirty minutes.
One talks through their thinking, gets 80% of the way, and says what they would
do next. The second one is hired more often than people believe.
::

## What is actually being marked

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-message-circle-question" title="Do you ask before you code"}
  The problem is deliberately underspecified. Candidates who start typing
  immediately have failed the first thing being tested.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-mic" title="Can I hear you think"}
  Silence is the most common way this round is lost. The interviewer cannot help
  you, cannot give partial credit, and cannot tell whether you are stuck or
  concentrating.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-move-horizontal" title="Do you consider the edges"}
  Empty, one element, duplicates, null, very large. Naming these unprompted is
  worth more than an optimal solution.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-gauge" title="Do you know what it costs"}
  You will be asked the complexity. Getting it wrong about your own code is
  worse than a slow solution.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-repeat" title="Can you take a hint"}
  They will nudge. Hearing it, and changing direction without defensiveness, is
  being assessed as much as the code.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-users" title="Would I want to debug with you at 2am"}
  The whole round, honestly, is this question.
  :::
::

## The five steps

Do these in this order, every time, out loud.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Forty-five minutes. Do not start at step three."}
  :::flow-step{label="1. Repeat it back, and ask three questions" icon="i-lucide-help-circle" highlight}
  *"So: given a list of applications, return the applicants who scored at or
  above a cutoff. Can I check three things? Can an application have no score
  yet? Can one applicant appear more than once? Is exactly the cutoff included?"*

  Two minutes. It is the single highest-value thing in the round and most
  candidates skip it.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="2. Work one example by hand" icon="i-lucide-pencil"}
  Write four rows, including an unmarked one and a duplicate, and say the
  expected output. You now have a test, and you will catch a misunderstanding
  before it costs you twenty minutes.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="3. Say the approach BEFORE coding" icon="i-lucide-route"}
  *"The simple version is a nested loop, which is O(n²). I think one pass with a
  set is O(n). Shall I write that?"*

  This is where they redirect you if you are heading somewhere wrong, and it is
  free.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="4. Write it, narrating" icon="i-lucide-code"}
  *"I am filtering out nulls first, because comparing a null score would throw…
  now deduplicating, because an applicant applying twice should appear once."*
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="5. Test it out loud, then state the complexity" icon="i-lucide-check-check"}
  Walk your example through the code. Then the empty list. Then say
  *"O(n) time, O(n) space for the set."* Volunteering the complexity is better
  than being asked.
  :::
::

## Watch a round go well

**The problem:** *"Given a list of applications, return the names of applicants
who scored at or above a cutoff."*

```text [Step 1, out loud]
"Let me make sure I have it. A list of applications, each with an applicant and
a score, and a cutoff number. I return the names of those at or above it.

Three questions. Can score be null, meaning not marked yet? Can the same
applicant appear twice, if they applied to two programmes? And does 'at or
above' include exactly the cutoff?"
```

The interviewer says: yes, scores can be null; yes, applicants can repeat; and
yes, inclusive. **You have just avoided all three bugs**, and they now know you
would ask the registrar rather than guess.

```text [Step 2]
"So with cutoff 75:
   Anita, 78   → in
   Vikram, 64  → out
   Anita, 76   → in, but she is already there, so once
   Rahul, null → out, not marked
   Priya, 75   → in, exactly the cutoff
 Expected: Anita, Priya."
```

```text [Step 3]
"Straightforward: one pass, filter out nulls, filter on the cutoff, map to the
name, and deduplicate with a LinkedHashSet so the order is stable. O(n) time and
O(n) space. Shall I write that?"
```

```java [Step 4]
public static List<String> shortlist(List<Application> applications, int cutoff) {
  if (applications == null) return List.of();

  Set<String> names = new LinkedHashSet<>();          // dedupe, keep order
  for (Application a : applications) {
    if (a.getScore() == null) continue;               // unmarked
    if (a.getScore() >= cutoff) {                     // inclusive
      names.add(a.getApplicantName());
    }
  }
  return new ArrayList<>(names);
}
```

```text [Step 5]
"Running my example: Anita 78 in, Vikram 64 skipped, Anita 76 already present so
the set keeps one, Rahul null skipped, Priya 75 in because it is >=. Gives Anita
and Priya.

Empty list gives an empty list. Null gives an empty list. O(n) time, O(n) space.

One thing I would ask in a real system: whether 'at or above' is really the rule,
because eleven applicants on exactly 75 will telephone about it."
```

That last sentence is the one that gets remembered.

## When you are stuck

You will be. Everybody is. Being stuck is normal; being stuck *silently* is
what loses the round.

::compare{caption="The same forty seconds of not knowing."}
  :::compare-side{label="Silence" verdict="wrong"}
  You stare at the screen. The interviewer cannot tell whether you are thinking
  or lost. They cannot help, and they start writing something down.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Narrated" verdict="right"}
  *"I am stuck on keeping the order while deduplicating. My instinct is a
  HashSet, but that loses the order. I think there is a LinkedHashSet. If not,
  I would use an ArrayList with a HashSet beside it to check membership. Does
  that sound reasonable?"*

  They will say "LinkedHashSet is right" and you have lost twenty seconds.
  :::
::

::tip
Three sentences to have ready, because they are hard to produce under pressure:

*"Let me think out loud for a moment."*
*"I do not remember the exact method name. The idea is X. Can I write it as
`maybeX()` and move on?"*
*"I am stuck. Here is what I have tried and what I think the obstacle is."*

All three are things a senior engineer says weekly. None of them counts against
you.
::

## How much to prepare, and what

::pros-cons
---
title: Grinding a thousand algorithm problems
pros:
  - For a product company with a hard algorithmic bar, it is the preparation
  - Pattern recognition genuinely improves and it is genuinely learnable
  - It builds speed, which is real under time pressure
cons:
  - For most service companies and startups, the round is far easier than this
  - It teaches memorising solutions rather than reasoning, which interviewers detect
  - It is a large amount of time not spent building the project that gets you the interview
---
::

::note
The honest allocation for somebody in this market. Build and finish the one real
project, because that is what gets you *into* the room. Then about fifty
problems: arrays, strings, hash maps, two pointers, sorting, and basic recursion.
That covers the overwhelming majority of entry-level rounds. Trees, graphs and
dynamic programming matter for the top-tier product companies and can wait until
you are aiming at one.
::

## What you can do now

You can structure forty-five minutes so that you are never silent and never
guessing at the requirement, which is most of what the round measures.

## Your turn

Do this with a real problem, timed, out loud, recorded. Any problem: find
duplicates, reverse a string, first non-repeating character.

Then listen back and count two things: **how many seconds of silence**, and
**how many clarifying questions you asked before coding**.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What to aim for, and the uncomfortable part" icon="i-lucide-target"}
  **Silence:** no single gap longer than about ten seconds. You will find your
  first recording has thirty-second gaps you did not notice at the time, because
  concentration makes time disappear.

  **Questions before coding:** at least two. Most first recordings have zero.

  **The uncomfortable part:** listening back is horrible and it is the exercise.
  You cannot fix a habit you cannot hear. Three recordings is usually enough to
  change it permanently, and it is the highest-return hour in interview
  preparation, exactly like the "tell me about yourself" recording in the English
  track.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. What are the five steps, and which one do most candidates skip?
2. **You do not remember a method name in the middle of a round.** What do you
   say?

## The interview question this answers

Every coding round. And one more that is asked explicitly: **"how do you approach
a problem you have not seen before?"** The five steps are the answer. Clarify,
work an example by hand, state the approach and its cost before writing,
implement while narrating, then test against your own example and state the
complexity.

Giving that as an answer, and then visibly doing it in the next round, is
consistency an interviewer notices.
