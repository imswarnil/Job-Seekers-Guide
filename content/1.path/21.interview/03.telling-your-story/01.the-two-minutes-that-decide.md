No right answer, no compiler, no marking scheme. Durgesh told me in 2019 that
these two questions decide more than the technical round, I did not believe him,
and it took me three years and about forty interviews to act on it.

## Why this matters in a job

Because both are asked in essentially every interview, both are entirely
rehearsable, and almost nobody rehearses them. That combination makes this the
highest-return hour in the whole course.

## Question one: tell me about yourself

Not a biography. They have your CV. The question is: **why are you in this room,
and what should I ask you about?**

That second half is the part nobody exploits. You are being handed ninety seconds
of uninterrupted control over what the rest of the interview is about.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Four sentences. The fourth is the one people leave out."}
  :::flow-step{label="Where you are now" icon="i-lucide-map-pin"}
  One line. Not your school, not your percentage, not where you were born.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The decision you made" icon="i-lucide-git-fork"}
  What you found out, and what you did about it. This is where honesty beats
  polish, and it is the sentence that makes you a person rather than a document.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The evidence" icon="i-lucide-hammer" highlight}
  The thing you built. Specific, and live. The rest of the interview will come
  from this sentence, so choose what is in it deliberately.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Why this role" icon="i-lucide-target"}
  Connect the evidence to the advert. One line. Almost nobody does it, and it is
  the difference between "a candidate" and "a candidate for this job".
  :::
::

::compare{caption="Same person, same CV, same year."}
  :::compare-side{label="What I said for three years" verdict="wrong"}
  "My name is Swarnil. I am from Mahroni, a small town in UP. I did my schooling
  there, then went to Kota for two years to prepare for IIT. After that I did
  B.Tech in computer science from LNCT Bhopal where I scored 71 percent. I am
  passionate about technology and a quick learner and a good team player.
  Currently I am looking for an opportunity where I can utilise my skills and
  grow with the organisation."

  Ninety seconds and not one sentence that could not have been said by any of
  the other four thousand applicants. No door opened.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What works" verdict="right"}
  "I finished computer science at LNCT in 2026, and I came out of it able to
  pass exams and not able to build anything.

  So I spent the year after that learning Java and SQL properly, from the ground
  up rather than from tutorials.

  What I built with it is an admissions system for a university: applications,
  shortlisting by score, fee tracking and results. It is live, about six thousand
  lines, and the part I am most pleased with is that it handles the cases that
  actually break these systems, like an applicant applying to three programmes
  and being counted three times.

  I am applying for the backend role because the part I enjoyed most was the data
  model, and I would rather spend my time on what is true than on what it looks
  like."
  :::
::

Count the doors in the second: Java, SQL, the admissions system, shortlisting,
the counting bug, the data model. Six follow-ups, all of which you want.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
**Never open on your weakest point.** "I do not have much experience, but…" is
the most common opening from a fresher and it is a gift you did not have to give.
They know it is your first job. It is on the CV and it is why the role is open.
::

## Question two: walk me through your project

Two minutes, and the mistake is universal: candidates start with the technology.

::compare{caption="The same project, two openings."}
  :::compare-side{label="Starts with the stack" verdict="wrong"}
  "So I used Spring Boot for the backend and React for the frontend, with
  PostgreSQL, and I deployed it on a VPS with Docker and nginx…"

  The listener has no idea what the thing *does*, so none of that means
  anything yet. And they now have to remember a list.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Starts with the problem" verdict="right"}
  "A university runs admissions on a shared spreadsheet. Six people edit it, and
  last year two of them allotted the same seat on the same afternoon.

  So I built the system that replaces it: applications, shortlisting, fees and
  results, with the seat allotment done in a single conditional update so the
  database refuses the second one rather than the code hoping.

  It is Java and Spring on PostgreSQL, React on the front, and it is live.

  The hardest part was that an applicant can apply to three programmes, so
  counting 'applicants' and counting 'applications' are different numbers and the
  dean's dashboard was reporting one and calling it the other."
  :::
::

::pull-quote
Problem, then what you built, then the stack, then the hard part. Four sentences,
in that order, and the fourth is the one that gets you the job.
::

## The hard part is the whole point

An interviewer is listening for evidence that you met something real. "The
hardest part was learning React" is the answer of somebody who followed a
tutorial. "The hardest part was that an applicant can apply three times" is the
answer of somebody who built a system and hit a genuine domain problem.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-search" title="Choose it deliberately"}
  Pick the hard part you can talk about for ten minutes and would enjoy being
  questioned on. You are choosing the next ten minutes of the interview.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-scale" title="Include what you decided against"}
  "I considered a nightly recalculation and chose to do it live, because the
  registrar edits during the day." Showing a rejected alternative is the
  clearest signal of engineering judgement there is.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle" title="Name one thing you would change"}
  "The fee module reads the current fee rather than storing what was charged, so
  a fee revision would rewrite history. I would fix that first." Honest
  self-criticism about a specific technical decision is rare and lands well.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="Do not claim the whole thing was hard"}
  "All of it was challenging" says you cannot distinguish, which is the skill
  being tested.
  :::
::

## When you do not know

You will be asked something you cannot answer. How you handle it is being
measured more than the gap itself.

::compare{caption="Three responses to 'how does the JVM decide which objects to collect?'"}
  :::compare-side{label="Bluffing" verdict="wrong"}
  "It uses an algorithm that checks which objects are old and removes them."

  Vague, partly wrong, and the next question will find the edge of it. A
  detected bluff costs more than the unanswered question ever would.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Honest and useful" verdict="right"}
  "I know it is based on reachability rather than reference counting: an object
  that cannot be reached from a root can be collected. I do not know the details
  of the generational collectors beyond that young objects are collected more
  often. I would read about G1 before working on anything performance-sensitive."

  What you know, where the edge is, and what you would do. That is exactly how a
  senior engineer answers.
  :::
::

::tip
The sentence to have ready: *"I do not know. Here is what I do know, and here is
how I would find out."* It is a strong answer, not a weak one, and every
interviewer has seen enough bluffing to value it.
::

## The behavioural questions

Four, in almost every HR round. Each wants a specific story, not a principle.

| Asked | What they are checking | Have ready |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Tell me about a difficult problem" | Do you persist and reason | The debugging story, with the method |
| "A time you disagreed with someone" | Do you argue to win | A story where you found out what they were actually worried about |
| "Your biggest weakness" | Self-awareness, not a disguised boast | A real one, plus what you do about it |
| "Why should we hire you" | Fit for **this** role | Evidence connected to their advert |

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
"My weakness is that I work too hard" and "I am a perfectionist" are heard four
hundred times a year and both read as evasion. A real answer:
*"I sit on problems too long before asking. I have a forty-five minute rule now:
if I have made no progress, I write down what I have tried and ask."* That is a
weakness, a consequence, and a fix, and it is unfakeable.
::

## What you can do now

You have the shape of both answers and you know which sentence in each carries
the weight. What you do not have is the answer itself, which only exists after
it has been said out loud several times.

## Your turn

Write both. Then record both. Then listen to one.

1. **Tell me about yourself.** Four sentences, ninety seconds.
2. **Walk me through your project.** Problem, built, stack, hard part. Two
   minutes.

Say each five times. Record the fifth.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What to listen for, and the version for somebody mid-course" icon="i-lucide-ear"}
  **Listen for three things.** Did you hedge — "I sort of built a thing"? Did you
  go in order, or jump back to add something? Did you stop, or keep adding detail
  into silence? The last one is nerves and it reads as uncertainty.

  **If you have not finished the project yet**, this is still a legitimate
  answer and it is better than an adjective:

  "I graduated in mechanical engineering last year and found during my final
  project that the part I liked was the data, not the machines. So I have spent
  five months learning Java and SQL properly. Right now I am about halfway
  through an admissions system for a university: applications and shortlisting
  work end to end, and I am on the fee module. I am applying for this role
  because it is backend work on a real system, and I would rather learn the rest
  of it in a team than alone."

  Honest about where you are, specific about what exists, and it opens the same
  doors. The only thing that would make it weak is if the system did not exist.
  :::
::

::ai-prompt{title="Pressure-test your own answer" model="Any"}

[paste it].

Do not rewrite it. Tell me which sentences carry no information, which claims I
would be asked to prove, and then ask me the five follow-up questions an
interviewer would ask after hearing this.

  #why
  The follow-up questions are the real output. If they are questions you cannot
  answer well, your opening has opened the wrong doors, and that is fixable in
  ten minutes before an interview rather than discovered during one.
::

## Check yourself

1. What are the four sentences, and which one does almost everybody leave out?
2. **Say your project walkthrough out loud and time it.** Over three minutes
   means cut the stack sentence.

## The interview question this answers

**"Tell me about yourself"** and **"walk me through a project"** — the first two
questions of essentially every interview, in that order, and between them they
set what the whole conversation is about.

Get both right and you have spent your first four minutes choosing the next
forty. That is the only free advantage available to a candidate, and it costs one
hour and a recording you will not enjoy.
