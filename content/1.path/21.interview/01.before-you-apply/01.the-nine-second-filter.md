Somebody who has never seen your system is about to decide whether to pay you.
Before any of that, they decide whether to read about you at all, and that
decision takes about nine seconds.

## Why this matters in a job

Because every other skill in this course is worth nothing until somebody reads
the CV, and because a CV is the one artefact in the process where you have
unlimited attempts and complete control.

::real-life{title="What I sent for four years" source="Campus, 2015 to 2018"}
Two pages. Objective statement at the top: "seeking a challenging position in a
reputed organisation where I can utilise my skills." Then my class X and XII
percentages, a list of eleven technologies I had touched once, three academic
projects with one line each, and hobbies. Not one sentence that described
something I had built. I sent that to about forty companies and cleared no
written round, and I blamed the written round.
::

## What actually happens

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Two passes. The first one is not reading."}
  :::flow-step{label="A scan, six to ten seconds" icon="i-lucide-scan" highlight}
  Eyes go to the top third, then to job titles or project names, then to the
  technology words. They are looking for a reason to continue, and the default
  is not to.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="A read, ninety seconds, only if the scan passed" icon="i-lucide-book-open"}
  Now they read your bullets properly and form a question to ask you. What they
  read here decides what the interview is about.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Possibly a parser before either" icon="i-lucide-file-search"}
  Large employers run a keyword filter first. This is why a PDF with a
  two-column layout or your details in a header can score zero: the parser
  cannot read it.
  :::
::

## The six things they look for

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-hammer" title="Did they build something real"}
  One system, described specifically, with a link. This is the single strongest
  signal available to a fresher and most CVs do not have it.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-code" title="The technologies, in context"}
  Not a list of eleven. Named inside what you built, so the reader can tell
  whether you used it or read about it.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-link" title="Links that work"}
  GitHub, and a live URL. A link is checkable, which is exactly why it carries
  weight that a claim does not.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-check" title="Evidence of finishing"}
  Deployed, tested, used by somebody. Half the CVs in the pile describe things
  that were never completed, and the reader knows it.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-scan-text" title="Readable in one column"}
  Plain, parseable, one page. Design is not being assessed, and a clever layout
  is a risk with no upside.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-map-pin" title="The basics, instantly"}
  Name, phone, email, city, GitHub. In the body, not in a header, where a parser
  cannot see them.
  :::
::

## Bullets: the only rule that matters

Every bullet answers **what did you do, how, and what was the result**. A bullet
with no result is a job description rather than an achievement.

::compare{caption="The same work, two bullets."}
  :::compare-side{label="What most freshers write" verdict="wrong"}
  - Developed a university management system using Java and SQL
  - Worked on database design and backend development
  - Implemented various features for admissions and fee management

  Three bullets, no numbers, no outcome, and nothing an interviewer can ask a
  second question about. "Various features" is the sound of somebody with
  nothing specific to say.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="The same work, written properly" verdict="right"}
  - Built a university admissions system in Java and PostgreSQL handling 40,000
    applications: shortlisting, fee tracking and result publication. Live at
    university-demo.in
  - Cut the shortlisting report from 14 seconds to 0.3 by replacing a nested
    loop with a hash lookup and adding a composite index
  - Wrote 60 JUnit tests covering the boundary cases; caught a bug where an
    applicant applying to three programmes was counted three times

  Every bullet has a number, a mechanism and a result. Each one invites a
  question you would enjoy being asked.
  :::
::

::pull-quote
An interviewer chooses their first question from your CV. Every bullet is a door
you are inviting them to open, so write only doors you want opened.
::

## The structure, for somebody with no experience

```text [One page, in this order]
NAME
Bhopal · +91 XXXXX XXXXX · you@email.com · github.com/you · yoursite.in

SUMMARY                                              ← two lines, not an objective
Backend-focused developer. Built and deployed a university admissions
system in Java, Spring and PostgreSQL handling 40,000 applications.

PROJECTS                                             ← FIRST. This is your experience.
University Admissions System · Java, Spring Boot, PostgreSQL, React
github.com/you/university-admissions · university-demo.in
  · three or four bullets as above

SKILLS                                               ← grouped, honest
Languages: Java, SQL, JavaScript
Frameworks: Spring Boot, React
Tools: Git, Docker, PostgreSQL, Linux

EDUCATION
B.Tech Computer Science, LNCT Bhopal, 2026 · 71%

EXPERIENCE (if any) · CERTIFICATIONS (only if relevant)
```

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Projects **above** education when you have no work experience. The project is
your evidence; the degree is a line. Putting education first is the default in
every template and it buries the only thing that distinguishes you.
::

## What to remove

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="The objective statement"}
  "Seeking a challenging position…" Every CV has it, it says nothing, and it
  occupies the most valuable space on the page.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="Class X and XII percentages"}
  Nobody has asked me for mine since 2019. They take three lines at the top for
  no information.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="Skill bars and star ratings"}
  Four stars out of five in Java means nothing and invites the question of what
  the fifth star is.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="Hobbies, photo, age, father's name"}
  Space with no signal, and in several markets a photo is actively unhelpful
  because it invites bias the employer is trying to avoid.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="Anything you cannot discuss for ten minutes"}
  A technology on your CV is a promise. Listing Kubernetes because you ran one
  tutorial is a trap you set for yourself.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="Two columns, and a header block"}
  Parsers read left to right and frequently skip headers. A two-column CV can
  arrive as scrambled text, which is a rejection you never find out about.
  :::
::

## The GitHub they will actually open

If you put a link on your CV, somebody opens it, and it is judged in about thirty
seconds.

::compare{caption="Two profiles, same person, same amount of work."}
  :::compare-side{label="What most people have" verdict="wrong"}
  Fourteen repositories. Nine are tutorial follow-alongs named `java-practice`
  and `react-todo`. No READMEs. Commit messages: "update", "fix", "asdf".
  Nothing has been touched in five months.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What gets you a call" verdict="right"}
  Three repositories, pinned. The main one has a README with a screenshot, what
  it does, how to run it, the schema, and one paragraph on a decision you made
  and why. Commit messages are sentences. There are tests.
  :::
::

::tip
Delete or make private the tutorial repositories. A profile with three good
things reads better than one with three good things and eleven abandoned ones,
because the reader averages rather than taking the maximum. This is the cheapest
improvement available to you today.
::

## What you can do now

You can write a CV out of evidence rather than adjectives, and you know what
happens to it in the first nine seconds.

## Your turn

Rewrite three of your own bullets. For each: what did you do, how, and what was
the result, with a number in at least two of the three.

Then hand your CV to somebody for exactly ten seconds, take it back, and ask what
they remember.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Where to find a number when you think you have none" icon="i-lucide-hash"}
  Freshers assume they have no numbers. You have several.

  **Size of the thing.** 40,000 rows, 12 tables, 60 tests, 6,000 lines.
  **Before and after.** 14 seconds to 0.3. Ten manual steps to one command.
  **Scope.** Five modules, three user roles, two integrations.
  **Time.** Built in four months alongside a degree.
  **Use.** Eleven people used it. Forty students tried it. Nine stars.

  If you genuinely have none, that is worth knowing now: it usually means the
  project has not been finished or measured, and both are fixable this month.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. What are the six things a reader looks for in the first pass?
2. **Look at your own CV. Which bullet would you most want to be asked about,
   and which one are you dreading?** Delete the second one.

## The interview question this answers

Not a question: the thing that happens before any question. But it decides two
later ones. "Walk me through your CV" and "tell me about a project you are proud
of" are both answered from what you wrote here, so a CV built from evidence gives
you a prepared answer to both, and a CV built from adjectives leaves you
improvising about something you did not do.
