Ten answers about a subject everybody is anxious about, and that is the last
lesson. This page is not one. It is what to do now.

## Where this leaves you

Say it plainly, because vague encouragement is worth nothing.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-cpu" title="You understand the machine"}
  What a processor does, what an operating system is for, how a request crosses
  a network, where data lives and what an index costs. This is the layer a model
  cannot supervise for you.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-code" title="You can build and defend a system"}
  A language, a database, a browser, types, components, a build, and one real
  system taken from written requirements to a URL a stranger can open.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-clipboard-list" title="You can clear the written round"}
  The shapes, the minutes, and the English. The gate that rejected me for four
  years is now a thing you have a method for.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-messages-square" title="You can hold the rooms"}
  Think out loud in a coding round, defend a decision with its rejected
  alternative, answer a behavioural question with evidence, and read an offer
  letter without being misled by the headline.
  :::
::

That is more than I had in 2019, and I got a job with less.

::pull-quote
I was not talented and I was not passionate about software. What I eventually
got was a sequence: somebody telling me what to learn, in what order, and how to
talk about it. That is all this was.
::

## 1. Fill the gap you know about

There is one. There always is.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="How to find it honestly" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Go back through the tracks and ask of each: **could I explain this to somebody
  who has not read it?** Not recognise it. Explain it, out loud, with an
  example.

  The ones where the answer is no are your list, and it is usually short: three
  or four topics, and they are usually generics, transactions, the event loop,
  or how authentication actually works.

  Do not restudy everything. Restudy those, and prove it by writing the
  explanation down as though for somebody else. If you can write it, you have
  it.
  :::
::

## 2. Apply before you feel ready

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Why now rather than after one more track" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Because "one more track" is infinite, and because interviewing is a skill that
  only improves by interviewing.

  My first four interviews were bad and I learned more from them than from the
  six months of preparation before them. They were also, for exactly that
  reason, worth having early rather than late.

  Apply to more than you think you should. Twenty applications is not a lot.
  Ghosting is normal and it is not information about you. The one that works is
  frequently the one you nearly did not send.
  :::
::

## 3. Keep the project alive

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="The three things that matter" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  1. **It must still be deployed on the day of the interview.** A dead link is
     worse than no link. Check it the morning of.
  2. **Demo accounts in the README, one per role.** A reviewer will not sign up
     and will not email you.
  3. **Commit something occasionally.** A repository whose last commit is from
     eleven months ago tells a story, and it is not the one you want.

  Adding one feature every few weeks keeps it honest and gives you new material
  to talk about. It does not need to be large.
  :::
::

## 4. Decide what you are adding next

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Three reasonable directions, and one bad one" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **Depth on what you have.** Go further into Java and Postgres: concurrency,
  the JVM's memory model, query planning, indexing strategy. The least
  fashionable choice and frequently the highest paid.

  **Systems and infrastructure.** Containers, orchestration, observability, CI
  pipelines. Reliably in demand and reliably under-supplied.

  **The AI layer, properly.** You have the foundation for it now, which almost
  nobody entering this area does. Retrieval, evaluation, and cost control are
  where the actual work is.

  **The bad one:** collecting frameworks. Another JavaScript framework adds
  nothing to what you can do or what you can be asked about. If you find
  yourself choosing a third front-end library instead of going deeper on
  anything, that is avoidance wearing the costume of progress.
  :::
::

## 5. The last thing

Somebody is where you were two years ago, reading a rejection email and
concluding something about themselves. You have something they do not: you know
it was navigation and not ability.

Tell them. A README somebody can follow, an answer on a forum, forty minutes
with a junior who is stuck. It costs almost nothing, and it is the entire reason
this course exists, because somebody did it for me and I would not have got here
otherwise.

## The honest closing note

This course cannot get you a job. It can remove the reasons you were being
rejected that had nothing to do with whether you could do the work, and in my
case that was all of them.

There is luck in this. There is timing, and there is a referral you either had
or did not. None of that is in your control, and pretending otherwise is the
thing I find hardest to forgive in most advice about this.

What is in your control is being ready when the room happens. You are.
