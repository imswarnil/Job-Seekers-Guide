Puzzles were about placing people. Logic questions are about placing ideas: what
must be true given what you have been told, and nothing else. Syllogisms are
the most common of them and the most mechanical, which makes them the best
place to start.

## Why it matters

Syllogisms appear in TCS NQT, Infosys, Wipro, Capgemini, AMCAT-style tests and
almost every bank-style reasoning section, usually one to three questions.
Check the current pattern. They are fast (30–45 seconds with the method) and
they are lost for one reason only: answering from general knowledge instead of
from the statements.

A **syllogism** is a pair (or trio) of statements followed by conclusions. You
decide which conclusions **definitely** follow. "Probably true" and "true in
real life" do not count.

| # | Sub-type | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Two statements, two conclusions | High |

## The four statement types

| Statement | Venn picture | Also means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All A are B | A circle inside B | Some A are B, Some B are A |
| No A are B | A and B separate | No B are A |
| Some A are B | A and B overlap | Some B are A |
| Some A are not B | Part of A outside B | Nothing reversible |

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
"All A are B" does **not** mean "All B are A". All freshers at a walk-in are
job seekers; not every job seeker is a fresher. This one reversal is the most
common wrong answer in the topic.
::

## The rules table

For two statements that share a middle term B:

```text
All A are B   + All B are C    →  All A are C
All A are B   + No B are C     →  No A are C
Some A are B  + All B are C    →  Some A are C
Some A are B  + No B are C     →  Some A are not C
No A are B    + All B are C    →  Some C are not A
All A are B   + Some B are C   →  no definite conclusion
Some A are B  + Some B are C   →  no definite conclusion
```

::tip
Two "some" statements never give a definite conclusion. If you see two
"some"s, jump straight to checking each conclusion against the statements
individually and the either-or case.
::

## The either-or case

When neither conclusion follows alone, check whether together they cover every
possibility. That happens when both conclusions use the **same two terms** and
one is a "some / some not" and the other an "all / no" of the opposite kind.

```text
Pairs that make "either I or II follows":
  Some A are B      /  No A are B
  Some A are not B  /  All A are B
```

## A worked example

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Only one follows" icon="i-lucide-circle-dot"}
  **Statements:** All students are learners. Some learners are coders.

  **Conclusions:** I. Some students are coders. II. Some coders are learners.

  Draw it: students inside learners, coders overlapping learners. The coders
  may or may not touch the students circle; nothing forces it.

  I is **possible, not definite**. II is the second statement reversed, which is
  always allowed for "some". **Only II follows.**
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="A some-not conclusion" icon="i-lucide-circle-slash"}
  **Statements:** Some pens are books. No book is a bag.

  **Conclusions:** I. Some pens are not bags. II. Some bags are pens.

  Rules table: Some A are B + No B are C gives Some A are not C. The pens that
  are books cannot be bags. I follows. II might be true, but nothing forces it.
  **Only I follows.**
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="Either or" icon="i-lucide-git-fork"}
  **Statement:** Some notes are books.

  **Conclusions:** I. All notes are books. II. Some notes are not books.

  Neither is definite. But they share the same terms and between them cover
  every case: either every note is a book, or at least one is not. **Either I
  or II follows.**
  :::
::

## Common mistakes

- **Using real-world knowledge.** If the statement says "All chairs are
  rivers", accept it.
- **Reversing "All".** "All A are B" never gives "All B are A".
- **Calling a possibility definite.** Draw the case where the conclusion is
  false. If you can draw it without breaking a statement, it does not follow.
- **Missing the either-or** when neither conclusion follows.

## How to practise

::steps
### Days one and two
Learn the four types and the rules table. 10 questions a day from IndiaBix
"Syllogism", drawing the Venn diagram for every one.

### Days three and four
10 a day, target **40 seconds**. Draw only when the rules table does not
answer it immediately.

### Then
Three syllogisms in every mixed reasoning set. R.S. Aggarwal's *A Modern
Approach to Verbal and Non-Verbal Reasoning* has a long exercise for volume.
::

## Practice questions

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. All engineers are graduates. All graduates are job seekers. Does 'All engineers are job seekers' follow?"}
  **Yes.** All + All gives All.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. Some freshers are engineers. All engineers are graduates. Does 'Some freshers are graduates' follow?"}
  **Yes.** Some + All gives Some.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. All trainers are teachers. No teacher is a student. Conclusions: I. No trainer is a student. II. Some students are trainers."}
  **Only I follows.** All + No gives No; II contradicts it.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="4. Some forms are files. Some files are folders. Conclusions: I. Some forms are folders. II. No form is a folder."}
  **Either I or II follows.** Two "some"s give nothing definite, and I and II
  are a complementary pair on the same terms.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="5. All desks are tables. Does 'All tables are desks' follow?"}
  **No.** "All" does not reverse. Only "Some tables are desks" follows.
  :::
::
