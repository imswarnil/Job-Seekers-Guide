Statement questions asked what a writer assumed or what follows. Cause and
effect asks something narrower: given two events, did one cause the other, or
did something else cause both, or are they unrelated?

## Why it matters

Cause and effect appears in Infosys, Capgemini, AMCAT-style and bank-style
reasoning sections, and in some service-company papers as part of a verbal
reasoning block. Expect one or two questions when it appears, and check the
current pattern. With the time test below, each takes about 30 seconds.

## What to study

The questions give two statements, A and B, and almost always the same answer
options:

| Option | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| (a) | A is the cause, B is its effect |
| (b) | B is the cause, A is its effect |
| (c) | Both are independent causes (of separate things) |
| (d) | Both are effects of independent causes |
| (e) | Both are effects of some common cause |

Some papers drop one of these or word them differently. Read the options once
at the start of the set; they do not change between questions.

## The rules

::steps
### Put the two events in time order
A cause happens first. If one event could only have happened after the other,
the later one cannot be the cause.

### Ask "because" both ways
"B happened because A happened." Then "A happened because B happened." Only
one of these usually sounds like a direct link.

### If neither sounds direct, look for a third event
Both events might be results of something neither statement mentions: a
storm, a new law, a budget cut.

### If no link at all, it is independent
Two unrelated events. Decide whether they read as causes (actions) or as
effects (results).
::

::tip
An **effect** statement usually describes a result: prices rose, attendance
fell, roads were closed. A **cause** statement usually describes an action or
a change: the government raised a tax, a company froze hiring.
::

## A worked example

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Direct cause" icon="i-lucide-arrow-right"}
  **A.** The college's placement numbers fell sharply this year.
  **B.** Several IT companies froze fresher hiring this year.

  "Placements fell because companies froze hiring" is a direct, believable
  link. The reverse makes no sense. **B is the cause, A the effect: option
  (b).**
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="Common cause" icon="i-lucide-git-merge"}
  **A.** All colleges in the city were closed on Monday.
  **B.** Most offices in the city asked staff to work from home on Monday.

  Colleges closing does not make offices send people home, and offices do not
  close colleges. Both are the kind of thing a heavy-rain warning or a city
  holiday causes. **Both are effects of a common cause: option (e).**
  :::
::

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing (a) because A is printed first.** Order on the page is not order
  in time.
- **Inventing a long chain.** "A caused X which caused Y which caused B" is not
  a cause. The link must be direct.
- **Missing the common cause** because it is not written down. The third event
  is almost never in the statements.
- **Mixing up (c) and (d).** Independent causes are actions; independent
  effects are results.

## How to practise

- **Two days:** 10 questions a day from IndiaBix "Cause and Effect", writing the
  "because" sentence both ways for each.
- **Target:** 30 seconds a question.
- **Then:** two in each mixed logic set. R.S. Aggarwal's *A Modern Approach to
  Verbal and Non-Verbal Reasoning* has a short chapter for more.

## Practice questions

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. A. The metro fare was increased by 20%. B. Many students started taking buses to college."}
  **(a) A is the cause.** Students switched because the fare went up.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. A. Rents in BTM Layout have risen. B. Several new tech parks opened nearby."}
  **(b) B is the cause.** More jobs nearby raise demand for rooms.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. A. The price of onions rose sharply. B. The price of tomatoes rose sharply."}
  **(e) Common cause** is the usual answer: a poor monsoon or transport
  disruption affects both. Neither causes the other.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="4. A. The college library extended its hours. B. A new canteen opened in the hostel."}
  **(c) Independent causes.** Two unrelated decisions, both actions.
  :::
::
