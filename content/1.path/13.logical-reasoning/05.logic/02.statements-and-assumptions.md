Syllogisms had neat statements: "All", "Some", "No". This page takes the same
discipline to ordinary sentences, the kind a notice board or a newspaper would
print, and asks three different questions about them.

## Why it matters

Statement-based questions appear in Infosys, Wipro, Capgemini, AMCAT-style and
bank-style reasoning sections, and some TCS NQT papers, usually two to four
questions across the three types. Check the current pattern. They need no
arithmetic and no drawing, which makes them fast marks for a careful reader and
lost marks for anyone who answers from opinion.

## What to study

| # | Type | The question | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Statement and assumption | What did the writer take for granted? | High |
| 2 | Statement and conclusion | What must be true, given the statement? | High |
| 3 | Statement and argument | Which argument for or against is strong? | Medium |
| 4 | Course of action | Which step logically follows? | Medium |

Three definitions, in plain English:

- An **assumption** is something the writer believed without saying it. If it
  were false, the statement would make no sense.
- A **conclusion** is something that follows from what was said, without
  adding outside facts.
- A **strong argument** is directly related to the question, important, and
  not based on an extreme or doubtful claim.

## The rules

| Type | An option is usually right if | An option is usually wrong if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assumption | The statement would be pointless without it | It is extreme, or it is stated outright (then it is not assumed) |
| Conclusion | It follows from the statement alone | It needs outside knowledge, or goes further than the statement |
| Argument | It is about the actual question, and it names a real effect | It is a slogan, an opinion, a "what if" or a comparison with another country |
| Course of action | It solves or reduces the problem, and it is practical | It is extreme (ban, shut down, punish everyone) or unrelated |

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Words that almost always make an option wrong: **all, only, always, never,
definitely, best, must, no one**. Statements in these questions are modest, and
an assumption or conclusion that is stronger than the statement is not
supported by it.
::

## A worked example

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Assumption" icon="i-lucide-lightbulb"}
  **Statement:** "The college will run free aptitude training for final-year
  students from next month."

  **Assumptions:** I. Final-year students need aptitude training. II. All
  final-year students will attend.

  I is implicit: nobody runs training for people who do not need it. II is
  extreme; the college can run it without expecting everyone. **Only I is
  implicit.**
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="Conclusion" icon="i-lucide-flag"}
  **Statement:** "Of the 400 candidates who sat the written round, 90 were
  called for the technical interview."

  **Conclusions:** I. Most candidates did not reach the technical interview.
  II. The written round was too difficult.

  I follows from the numbers: 310 of 400 did not. II needs a judgement the
  statement never makes; the company may have had 90 seats. **Only I
  follows.**
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="Argument" icon="i-lucide-scale"}
  **Question:** "Should students with attendance below 75% be stopped from
  sitting final exams?"

  **Arguments:** I. Yes. Regular attendance is how most students learn the
  syllabus, and the rule pushes them to attend. II. No. Students are adults and
  should be free to decide for themselves.

  I is about the actual effect of the rule. II is a general opinion that does
  not address learning or exams. **Only I is strong.**
  :::
::

::tip
For assumptions, use the negation test: flip the option into its opposite and
ask whether the statement still makes sense. "Final-year students do **not**
need aptitude training" makes the notice pointless, so the original was
assumed.
::

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing what you agree with.** These questions test logic, not your
  opinion about attendance rules.
- **Picking an option because it is true in real life.** Only the statement
  counts.
- **Calling a stated fact an assumption.** If the statement says it, it is not
  assumed.
- **Treating long arguments as strong.** Length is not strength; relevance is.

## How to practise

- **Week one:** 10 questions a day, alternating the three types, from IndiaBix
  "Statement and Assumption", "Statement and Conclusion" and "Statement and
  Argument". Write one line of reason next to each answer.
- **Week two:** 10 a day mixed, target **45 seconds** each.
- **Reference:** R.S. Aggarwal's *A Modern Approach to Verbal and Non-Verbal
  Reasoning*, the verbal reasoning chapters.

## Practice questions

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. 'Carry two printed copies of your résumé to the walk-in.' Assumption: I. The interviewers may want a paper copy. II. Candidates own a printer."}
  **Only I is implicit.** II is not needed; copies can be printed at a shop.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. 'The canteen will close at 9 pm from Monday.' Conclusion: I. The canteen used to close later. II. Students will stop eating dinner."}
  **Neither follows.** I is likely but not stated (it may have closed earlier).
  II is extreme.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. 'Should colleges make an internship compulsory?' I. Yes, it gives students work experience before placements. II. No, some companies do not pay interns."}
  **Only I is strong.** II is about pay, a separate problem that can be fixed on
  its own. It does not argue that internships fail to help students.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="4. 'Many freshers fail the written round.' Course of action: I. Colleges should add aptitude practice to the final year. II. Companies should stop holding written rounds."}
  **Only I follows.** II is extreme and removes a hiring step rather than
  addressing the problem.
  :::
::
