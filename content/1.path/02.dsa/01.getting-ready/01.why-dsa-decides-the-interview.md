You can now write Java: classes, collections, exceptions, a test or two. The
next wall is not the language. It is the coding round, where somebody hands you
a problem about marks or roll numbers and watches what you do with it. This page
answers one question: how much DSA do you need, for which company, and how do
you get there.

**DSA** means **data structures and algorithms**. A data structure is a way of
arranging data in memory (an array, a map, a tree). An algorithm is a precise
list of steps that solves a problem using it. The subject is about choosing the
arrangement and the steps so the answer comes back fast.

## Why it matters

Almost every technical hiring process for a fresher or a two-to-five-year
developer in India has a coding round, and that round is DSA with a costume on.

| Company type | Examples | The coding round, when I went (check today's) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Service companies | TCS, Infosys, Wipro, Accenture, Cognizant, Capgemini | An online test with aptitude, verbal and a coding section: usually 1–2 problems in 45–60 minutes, easy to medium. Arrays, strings, patterns, basic maths. |
| Mid-size and startups | Product startups in Bangalore, Pune, Hyderabad | A take-home or an online assessment, then one or two live rounds with a problem and follow-up questions on complexity. |
| Product companies | Amazon, Flipkart, Microsoft, Swiggy, PhonePe | An online assessment of 2–4 problems (60–90 minutes), then 2–3 live DSA rounds of 45–60 minutes each, medium to hard. |

Here is my honest version. When I went from walk-in to walk-in in Bangalore in
2018, I failed written rounds I could have cleared with two weeks of arrays and
strings, because I had learned Java syntax and never learned to solve a problem
with it. The walk-in where I was finally selected, the 34th, had a coding
section too, and by then I had practised enough to write a clean loop under a
clock.

DSA came back into my life three years later. I was at Accenture on about
5 LPA and decided to switch. The companies paying 15 LPA and more asked binary
search, hash maps and trees in the first technical round, every time. I learned
it properly then, on evenings and Sundays, and it is the single biggest reason I
cracked five companies and later Twilio. I am writing this track so that you
learn it once, early, instead of twice like I did.

::pull-quote
Service companies test whether you can write a loop that works. Product
companies test whether you can write the right loop, and say what it costs.
::

## What to study

In priority order. The first block is enough for most service-company tests;
each block after that moves you up a tier.

::steps
### Enough for a service-company online test
- Big-O: say the time and space of anything you write.
- Arrays: traversal, max and min, reverse, rotate, prefix sums, Kadane.
- Strings: reverse, palindrome, anagram, character frequency.
- Searching and sorting: linear and binary search, bubble, selection, insertion.

### Enough for a startup or a mid-size product company
- Recursion and simple backtracking.
- Merge sort, quick sort, and what `Arrays.sort` actually does.
- Two pointers, sliding window, and `HashMap` / `HashSet` problems.
- Linked lists, stacks and queues, written by hand.

### Enough for a large product company
- Trees: traversals, BST operations, level order.
- Heaps and `PriorityQueue`, top-k problems.
- Graphs: BFS, DFS, cycles, topological sort, shortest paths.
- Dynamic programming and greedy.
::

That is also the order of this track. Nothing later depends on something you
have not seen yet.

::tip
You do not need to finish the whole track before you start applying. If your
target this quarter is a service company, the first five chapters plus the
patterns chapter are the job. Apply while you carry on.
::

## What a coding round is actually scored on

Interviewers in live rounds are not only checking whether your code passes.
They mark four things, and a correct answer can still lose on the other three.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-message-square" title="Communication"}
  Did you ask what the input looks like before writing? Did you think out loud?
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-lightbulb" title="Problem solving"}
  Did you start with a brute force, then improve it for a reason you could say?
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-code" title="Code"}
  Clear names, no duplicated logic, edge cases handled, it compiles in your head.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-gauge" title="Complexity"}
  Can you state the time and space, and defend them line by line?
  :::
::

Online assessments are scored differently: hidden test cases, and a time limit
per test case (often 1–2 seconds). A correct O(n²) solution on an input of
100,000 fails the big test cases with "Time Limit Exceeded" and scores partial
marks. That is why complexity is the next lesson, not an appendix.

## A worked example

A typical service-company question, as it would appear on screen:

> Given the marks of `n` students, print the second highest distinct mark. If
> there is none, print -1. Constraints: 1 ≤ n ≤ 100,000, 0 ≤ mark ≤ 100.

What a prepared candidate does in the first two minutes, before typing:

::flow{numbered caption="Two minutes of thinking that save twenty of debugging"}
  :::flow-step{label="Read the constraints" icon="i-lucide-ruler"}
  n up to 100,000, so O(n²) is 10 billion steps and too slow. O(n) or O(n log n).
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Find the edge cases" icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
  All marks equal. One student. The top mark repeated.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Pick the approach" icon="i-lucide-lightbulb" highlight}
  One pass, tracking the highest and the second highest distinct values.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Write, then dry run" icon="i-lucide-play"}
  Run `[72, 91, 91, 85]` by hand. Expect 85.
  :::
::

```java [SecondHighest.java]
public class SecondHighest {
  static int secondHighest(int[] marks) {
    int highest = -1, second = -1;
    for (int mark : marks) {
      if (mark > highest) {
        second = highest;
        highest = mark;
      } else if (mark < highest && mark > second) {
        second = mark;
      }
    }
    return second;
  }

  public static void main(String[] args) {
    System.out.println(secondHighest(new int[]{72, 91, 91, 85}));
    System.out.println(secondHighest(new int[]{60, 60, 60}));
    System.out.println(secondHighest(new int[]{45}));
  }
}
```

```text [Output]
85
-1
-1
```

The trap is the repeated 91. A candidate who sorts and takes the second-last
element prints 91, which is wrong, and the test with all marks equal catches
them. The condition `mark < highest` is what makes the answer "distinct".

## Common mistakes

::compare{caption="The two ways people prepare for DSA."}
  :::compare-side{label="Collect problems" verdict="wrong"}
  Solve 400 problems in random order, read the solution when stuck after five
  minutes, and forget most of them in a month. It feels like progress because
  the counter goes up.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Learn patterns" verdict="right"}
  Solve 150–200 problems grouped by pattern, struggle for 20–30 minutes before
  looking, and re-solve the ones you needed help with a week later. You
  recognise the next problem because you know its shape.
  :::
::

- **Starting with dynamic programming** because it sounds impressive. It rests
  on recursion, which rests on arrays. The order matters.
- **Practising only in an IDE.** Autocomplete hides whether you know
  `Map.getOrDefault`. Practise at least some problems in a plain editor.
- **Never saying complexity out loud.** Interviewers ask it after every problem.
  If you have never practised the sentence, it comes out badly.
- **Skipping edge cases.** Empty array, one element, all equal, negatives,
  very large numbers. Most rejected "correct" solutions fail on one of these.

## How to practise

::timeline{label="Ten weeks, two to three hours a day"}
  :::timeline-item{title="Weeks 1–2" state="current"}
  This chapter, arrays and strings. Two easy problems a day.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Weeks 3–4" state="todo"}
  Recursion, searching, sorting. Two to three problems a day, one of them medium.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Weeks 5–6" state="todo"}
  Two pointers, sliding window, hashing, linked lists, stacks and queues.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Weeks 7–8" state="todo"}
  Trees, heaps and graphs. Fewer problems, longer each.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Weeks 9–10" state="todo"}
  Dynamic programming, greedy, and the fifty must-do list against a timer.
  :::
::

- **Daily target:** two problems, one you can do and one that stretches you.
- **Time per problem:** 20 minutes for an easy, 40 for a medium, before you
  look at any hint.
- **Where:** any of the public practice sites (LeetCode, HackerRank,
  GeeksforGeeks, Codeforces for later). The platform matters far less than
  doing the problems by pattern.
- **Keep a log:** a spreadsheet with the problem, the pattern, the date, and
  whether you needed help. Re-solve every "needed help" row after seven days.
