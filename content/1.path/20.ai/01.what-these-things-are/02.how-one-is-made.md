You know what it does. Where it comes from is three separate stages, they cost
wildly different amounts, and knowing which stage produced a behaviour tells you
whether you can change it.

## Why this matters in a job

Because "can we train it on our data?" is a question you will be asked, the
honest answer is usually "you mean one of three different things", and the three
differ by a factor of about a million in cost.

## The three stages

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Each stage starts from the one before. Only the last two are ever available to you."}
  :::flow-step{label="Pretraining — learning language itself" icon="i-lucide-book-open" highlight}
  Predict the next token, over trillions of tokens of text. Months, thousands of
  GPUs, and tens of millions of dollars. This is where the model learns grammar,
  facts, code and reasoning patterns. **Nobody outside a handful of labs does
  this.**
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Instruction tuning — learning to be useful" icon="i-lucide-message-square"}
  The pretrained model completes text; it does not answer questions. Training on
  a much smaller set of instruction-and-response pairs teaches it that a question
  should be followed by an answer rather than by more questions.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Preference tuning — learning what is wanted" icon="i-lucide-thumbs-up"}
  Humans rank pairs of answers, a second model learns to predict those rankings,
  and the first model is tuned toward higher-ranked output. This is RLHF, and it
  is where helpfulness, refusals, tone and hedging come from.
  :::
::

::pull-quote
Pretraining gives it knowledge. Instruction tuning gives it manners. Preference
tuning gives it a personality, and that personality is a business decision made
by whoever paid for the ranking.
::

That last point is worth sitting with, because it explains a lot of otherwise
baffling behaviour. When a model hedges, refuses, or produces a bulleted summary
you did not ask for, that is usually not knowledge and not capability. It is the
preference stage, and it can differ between two models of identical capability.

## What each stage costs

Orders of magnitude, because the exact numbers move every few months.

| Stage | Data | Compute | Realistic cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pretraining a frontier model | Trillions of tokens | Thousands of GPUs, months | Tens of millions of dollars |
| Pretraining a small model | Billions of tokens | Tens of GPUs, weeks | Tens of thousands |
| Instruction tuning | Thousands of examples | A few GPUs, hours | Hundreds |
| **LoRA fine-tune** | Hundreds to thousands | **One GPU, hours** | **Tens of dollars** |
| **Retrieval, no training at all** | Your documents | None | **Near zero** |

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
The bottom two rows are the only ones that will ever be your decision, and the
last one is the right answer far more often than people expect. "Train it on our
data" almost always means "make it able to answer from our data", which is
retrieval, costs nothing, updates instantly when a document changes, and can
cite its source. Fine-tuning does none of those three.
::

## Fine-tuning against retrieval: the decision

::compare{caption="The question is what you are trying to change."}
  :::compare-side{label="Fine-tune when you want different BEHAVIOUR" verdict="neutral"}
  A consistent output format. A house tone. A specialised task it does badly —
  classifying support messages into your twelve categories. Teaching a **shape**,
  not a fact.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Retrieve when you want different KNOWLEDGE" verdict="right"}
  Your prospectus, your fee circulars, your applicant records. Facts, which
  change, and which need to be cited. This is almost always what is actually
  being asked for.
  :::
::

::pull-quote
Fine-tuning teaches a shape. Retrieval supplies facts. Somebody who asks you to
fine-tune a model on their documents is nearly always describing retrieval and
using the other word.
::

## What a LoRA actually is

The one fine-tuning technique worth understanding, because it is what made
tuning affordable.

A full fine-tune updates every weight in the model, which needs as much memory
as training it. **LoRA** freezes the original weights and trains a pair of very
small matrices alongside them, whose product is added to the original. It is a
fraction of a per cent of the parameters.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-hard-drive" title="A few megabytes, not gigabytes"}
  The adapter is tiny, so you can keep dozens and swap them per task rather than
  hosting a separate model each time.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-wallet" title="One GPU, a few hours"}
  Which puts it inside a personal budget. This is the difference between fine-
  tuning being a research activity and an engineering one.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-undo" title="Reversible"}
  Remove the adapter and the original model is untouched. A full fine-tune is a
  new model you now have to store and serve.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle" title="Still needs good data"}
  Five hundred carefully written examples beat fifty thousand scraped ones. Data
  quality is the whole of the work, and it is why most fine-tuning projects fail
  before any training runs.
  :::
::

## Building your own, honestly

People ask this, so here is the straight answer at four levels of ambition.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="From most useful to least, for somebody learning."}
  :::flow-step{label="A tiny one, to understand it" icon="i-lucide-graduation-cap" highlight}
  A few hundred lines, trained on one book, on your laptop or a free notebook
  GPU, in an afternoon. It will produce almost-English nonsense, and you will
  understand tokenisation, embeddings, attention and a training loop in a way no
  amount of reading delivers. **Do this one.** Karpathy's nanoGPT is the standard
  starting point.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="A LoRA on an open model" icon="i-lucide-sliders"}
  Take Llama or Mistral, a few hundred examples of the behaviour you want, and a
  single rented GPU for a few hours. This is a genuinely employable skill and it
  is achievable this month.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Pretraining a small model" icon="i-lucide-cpu"}
  Possible, expensive, and almost never the right call: an open model already
  exists that is better than what you would produce.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Pretraining a frontier model" icon="i-lucide-building"}
  Tens of millions of dollars and a team. Included so the scale is clear.
  :::
::

```bash [Terminal]
# The afternoon version. It trains, it generates, and it is legible.
git clone https://github.com/karpathy/nanoGPT && cd nanoGPT
pip install torch numpy transformers datasets tiktoken

python data/shakespeare_char/prepare.py     # tokenise one book
python train.py config/train_shakespeare_char.py \
  --device=cpu --compile=False --max_iters=2000 --eval_iters=20
python sample.py --out_dir=out-shakespeare-char
```

```text [Output, after a few minutes on a laptop]
KING RICHARD:
And then the sead of my sorrow to the sead,
That we shall not the streath of the state...
```

Nonsense, and it is **grammatical** nonsense with line breaks and character
names, learned from nothing but next-token prediction over one book. That is the
whole mechanism from the previous lesson, visible.

::tip
Do this before you build anything else in this track. Two hours, and afterwards
"it predicts the next token" stops being a sentence you repeat and becomes
something you have watched happen.
::

## What you can do now

You can tell the three stages apart, say what each costs, and answer "can we
train it on our data" with the question that actually needs asking: are you
changing behaviour, or supplying facts?

## Your turn

For each request, say which stage or technique, and roughly what it costs.

1. "Make it answer questions about our prospectus."
2. "Make it always reply in the university's house style, in exactly three
   paragraphs."
3. "Make it know about the fee revision we published yesterday."
4. "Make it classify incoming enquiries into our nine categories."
5. "Make it better at mathematics."

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answers" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **1. Retrieval.** Near zero, updates instantly, and it can cite the section.
  Fine-tuning would bake the prospectus in as a vague statistical impression that
  cannot be cited and is wrong the day it is revised.

  **2. Fine-tuning**, a LoRA, and this is the genuine case for it: a consistent
  shape is exactly what tuning teaches. Try a good system prompt first though,
  because it is free and often enough.

  **3. Retrieval, emphatically.** Anything that changes must never be trained in.
  This is the clearest example of the distinction in the whole list.

  **4. Fine-tuning**, and a strong case: classification into a fixed set is a
  shape, the examples are cheap to produce from existing enquiries, and a small
  tuned model will be faster and far cheaper per call than prompting a large one.

  **5. None of the above.** You cannot meaningfully improve a model's
  mathematics with a few hundred examples; that lives in pretraining. The
  engineering answer is to not ask it to do arithmetic at all: have it write the
  query or the expression and let a database or a calculator evaluate it. Which
  is the architecture from the building chapter, arrived at from a different
  direction.
  :::
::

## The interview question this answers

**"What is fine-tuning, and when would you use it?"**

Name the three stages first so the question has a frame, then the distinction
that matters: tuning teaches a shape, retrieval supplies facts. Then the
practical answer — LoRA rather than a full fine-tune, because it is a few
megabytes, one GPU and reversible.

Then the sentence that gets remembered: most requests to "train it on our data"
are descriptions of retrieval, and saying so early saves a project that would
otherwise spend three months producing something that cannot cite a source and
is out of date the day a document changes.
