Both halves of the paper are shapes you recognise. The last section gives you a
table and five questions, and it is the section where good candidates lose marks
to something that is not mathematics at all.

## Why this matters in a job

This is the dashboard from the build track, in exam form. A table, a chart, and
somebody asking a question it does not quite answer. Every mistake available
here is a mistake available in a real reporting meeting, and the most common one
is answering confidently from the wrong column.

## The five questions, and where the marks go

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. Percentage increase in total applications, 2024 to 2026?" icon="i-lucide-calculator"}
  `(4,000 − 3,000) ÷ 3,000 = 33.33%`

  Trivial, and the only way to lose it is dividing by 4,000. Denominator is the
  **old** value. This is the direction trap from the first lesson, and it is
  here every single time.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. Which programme grew fastest 2024 to 2026?" icon="i-lucide-calculator"}
  Do not calculate four percentages. Scan first.

  ```text
  CSE   1,200 → 1,740   up 540, roughly +45%
  ECE     900 →   800   down. Eliminate immediately.
  Maths   400 →   660   up 260, and 260 on 400 is +65%
  MBA     500 →   800   up 300, and 300 on 500 is +60%
  ```

  **B.Sc Maths.** The trap is picking CSE because 540 is the biggest increase.
  The question says fastest, which is relative, and the smallest programme won.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. How many CSE applicants were shortlisted in 2026?" icon="i-lucide-calculator"}
  `1,740 × 25% = 435`.

  One multiplication. The mark is lost by reading the 2025 column, because the
  percentage column sits on the right and the eye travels along the wrong row.

  **Put your finger on the row.** On a screen, use the cursor. This is not
  advice about mathematics and it is worth more than any of it.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="4. Total shortlisted across all programmes in 2026?" icon="i-lucide-calculator"}
  ```text
  CSE    1,740 × 0.25 = 435
  ECE      800 × 0.30 = 240
  Maths    660 × 0.40 = 264
  MBA      800 × 0.15 = 120
                        1,059
  ```

  Four multiplications and an addition, roughly forty seconds. This is the
  question to **skip on the first pass** and return to, because it costs four
  times what the others do for the same one mark.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="5. The ratio of ECE to MBA applications in 2025?" icon="i-lucide-calculator"}
  `860 : 590`. Divide both by 10 → `86 : 59`, which does not reduce further.

  If the options are given as approximate ratios, `86 ÷ 59 ≈ 1.46`, so look for
  something near `3 : 2`.

  The trap: reading 2026 and answering `800 : 800 = 1 : 1`, which will be one of
  the options precisely because it is the mistake they expect.
  :::
::

## The pattern in all five

::pull-quote
The arithmetic in data interpretation is never the difficulty. Every mark lost
here is lost to reading: wrong row, wrong year, wrong denominator, or a question
about "fastest" answered with "biggest".
::

## Four habits

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-scan-eye" title="Read the headers and the units first"}
  Before any question. Is it thousands or lakhs? Is the percentage column for
  one year or all of them? Twenty seconds here saves two wrong answers.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-pointer" title="Track the row physically"}
  Finger, cursor, or the edge of your answer sheet. The eye drifts one row up
  when moving right across a table, and it does it without telling you.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-divide" title="Estimate before calculating"}
  `1,740 × 25%` is "a bit under 1,800 quartered", so about 435. Now the exact
  answer has a sanity check attached, and a slipped digit becomes visible.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-forward" title="Rank the five by cost"}
  One of the five always costs four times the others. Do the cheap ones first.
  Marks are identical, so effort per mark is the only thing that matters.
  :::
::

## Approximation is the skill

In a sixty-minute paper, exact arithmetic is frequently the wrong choice.

```text [Which programme had the highest shortlist count in 2026?]
Exact:   435, 240, 264, 120   → four multiplications, 40 seconds

Estimate: CSE is 1,740 at a quarter, so mid-400s.
          Nothing else is above 800, and 40% of 660 is under 300.
          CSE wins, comfortably.                    → 8 seconds
```

The question asked which, not how many. Calculating all four to answer "which"
is the most common time-waste in this section.

::tip
Read the question before reading the table, and read what it is actually asking
for. "Which", "how many", "by what percentage" and "what is the ratio" need
completely different amounts of work, and one of them needs almost none.
::

## What you can do now

You can read a data set safely: headers and units first, row tracked physically,
estimate before calculating, cheap questions first, and the difference between
biggest and fastest held firmly in mind.

## Your turn

Using the same table, four minutes:

1. Which programme had the largest absolute fall in applications 2024 to 2026?
2. What percentage of all 2026 applications were for CSE?
3. If MBA shortlisting had been 30% instead of 15%, how many more would have
   been shortlisted in 2026?
4. What was the average number of applications per programme in 2025?
5. ECE fell every year. If it continues falling at the 2025 to 2026 rate, how
   many in 2027?

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answers" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **1.** ECE, `900 → 800`, a fall of 100. The only programme that fell at all.

  **2.** `1,740 ÷ 4,000 = 43.5%`.

  **3.** `800 × 0.15 = 120` more. Doubling the rate doubles the count, so the
  increase equals the original 120. Spotting that saves you a multiplication.

  **4.** `3,400 ÷ 4 = 850`.

  **5.** `860 → 800` is a fall of 60, which is `60 ÷ 860 = 6.98%`. Another 7%
  off 800 is 744.

  Then the part worth more than the answer: **that extrapolation is not
  trustworthy.** Two years is not a trend, and the exam wants 744 while a real
  reporting meeting wants you to say the sample is too small. Know both answers
  and know which room you are in.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why does "which is largest" usually need less work than "how many"?
2. **An interviewer shows you a chart and asks what you notice.**

## The interview question this answers

**"Here is some data. What do you see?"**

Asked in analyst and product rounds, and the weak answer describes the chart
back to them. "CSE is the biggest" is reading, not analysis.

The answer that works names something and then questions it: ECE is falling
while everything else grows, which is the only thing on here that needs
explaining. Then the check: three years is not a trend, and I would want to know
whether the intake capacity changed before reading anything into it.

Noticing the anomaly and then doubting your own reading of it is the whole
skill, and it is the same discipline as labelling a dashboard with its
denominator.
