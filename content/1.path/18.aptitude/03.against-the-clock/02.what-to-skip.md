Every shape in the paper is now something you recognise, and that is still not a
score. This lesson is about the minutes, and it is the one that changed my
result when nothing else had.

## Why this matters in a job

It is the same decision as triage in an incident, or picking what goes in a
release when the date will not move. Knowing which work to abandon is a skill in
its own right, and people who cannot do it are the ones still polishing one
thing while four others burn.

## The thing nobody tells you

I failed this round three times in a row. After the third, I did something I
should have done at the start: I reconstructed the paper from memory and worked
out what had actually happened.

I had attempted 38 of 60 questions and got 31 right. That is an 82% accuracy
rate, which is genuinely good. I had spent nine minutes on two seating
arrangement questions, neither of which I solved, and never reached the last
fourteen questions, six of which were percentage questions I could have done in
forty seconds each.

::pull-quote
I was not rejected for what I did not know. I was rejected for what I chose to
spend the minutes on, and nobody had ever suggested that was a choice.
::

## Three passes

Stop trying to do the paper in order. Do it three times.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Budget roughly 25, 20 and 15 minutes. The first pass is where the score is made."}
  :::flow-step{title="Pass one: everything you can do in under sixty seconds" highlight}
  Read a question. If you recognise the shape and can see the route to the
  answer, do it. If not, mark it and move on **immediately**.

  You will get through 60 to 70% of the paper. Every mark here is cheap.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="Pass two: the ones that need working"}
  The four-multiplication data question. The series that needs a second
  difference. The arrangement that looked solvable.

  These cost two to three minutes each and they are worth exactly the same one
  mark, which is why they are second and not first.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="Pass three: what is left"}
  Whatever remains. By now you know how many minutes you have per question,
  which is information you did not have at the start.

  If a question has defeated you twice, it will defeat you now. Spend the time
  checking pass-one answers instead.
  :::
::

## The fifteen-second rule

On pass one, you have fifteen seconds to decide **whether to answer**, not to
answer.

::compare{caption="The same question, two candidates, both competent."}
  :::compare-side{label="Committing" verdict="wrong"}
  Reads a seating arrangement. It looks solvable. Starts drawing. Two minutes
  in, one contradiction found, decides to try the other branch because the sunk
  effort is painful. Four minutes gone. No answer.

  The cost is not this question. It is the four questions at the end never
  reached.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Triaging" verdict="right"}
  Reads it, sees five constraints and no absolute placement, marks it, moves on
  in eleven seconds. Comes back on pass two with a clear head and twelve minutes
  of easy marks already banked.

  Solves it in three minutes, or does not, and either way nothing was lost.
  :::
::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
The hardest part of this is emotional, not technical. Skipping a question you
believe you could solve feels like giving up, and that feeling is the single
most expensive thing in the room. The marks are identical. A question is worth
one mark whether it took eight seconds or eight minutes.
::

## The arithmetic of negative marking

Many papers deduct 0.25 for a wrong answer. People respond to this with fear,
and the fear costs more than the marking does.

```text [Four options, one mark, minus 0.25 for wrong]
Blind guess:      1 in 4 right
                  Expected value = (0.25 × 1) + (0.75 × −0.25) = +0.0625
                  Slightly positive. Guessing beats leaving blank.

One eliminated:   1 in 3
                  Expected value = (0.33 × 1) + (0.67 × −0.25) = +0.165
                  Clearly worth it.

Two eliminated:   1 in 2
                  Expected value = (0.5 × 1) + (0.5 × −0.25) = +0.375
                  Always answer.
```

::tip
**If you can eliminate even one option, answer.** The expected value is positive
and it stays positive. The only case for leaving a question blank is one where
you have no basis at all for eliminating anything, and even then a blind guess
is marginally better than nothing.

Check the paper's actual marking scheme in the first thirty seconds. Some deduct
0.33, a few deduct nothing, and the strategy changes.
::

## What to skip on sight

Four kinds, and recognising them is worth practising.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-armchair" title="A long arrangement set"}
  Five questions off one puzzle with six or more constraints. Enormous value if
  it resolves, total loss if it does not. **Always pass two**, never pass one.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-calculator" title="Multi-step calculation"}
  Anything needing four or more operations. Same mark, four times the cost.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-shapes" title="A shape you do not recognise"}
  If fifteen seconds have not produced a category, more seconds will not. This
  is the one people fight hardest and it is the clearest signal there is.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-file-text" title="A dense paragraph question"}
  Four lines of prose hiding one equation. Often easy once decoded, and the
  decoding is unpredictable. Pass two.
  :::
::

## The last five minutes

Do not start anything new.

- **Fill in every blank.** Positive expected value, as above. A blank is a
  guaranteed zero.
- **Check that your answers are on the right numbers.** One misaligned row on an
  answer sheet has ended more attempts than any question ever has. If you skipped
  question 14, make sure your answer to 15 is on line 15.
- **Do not revisit an answer you were confident about.** Second-guessing under
  time pressure changes more right answers to wrong than the reverse.

## What you can do now

You can work a paper in three passes, decide in fifteen seconds whether to
attempt, calculate whether a guess is worth making, recognise the four kinds of
question to defer, and use the last five minutes on the things that reliably
gain marks.

## Check yourself

1. Why can a candidate with lower accuracy score higher, and what does that say
   about what the exam measures?
2. **An interviewer asks: "you have four tasks and time for two. How do you
   decide?"**

## The interview question this answers

**"How do you prioritise when you cannot finish everything?"**

The honest answer is this lesson, and using the exam as the example is fine and
often better, because it is specific.

Cheapest first when the value is equal, and say why: four tasks worth the same
where one costs four times as much means doing the other three first is
arithmetic, not preference. Then the part people miss: decide fast and revisit,
rather than deciding perfectly. A two-minute triage that is 80% right beats a
ten-minute one that is 95% right, because the eight minutes came out of the
work.
