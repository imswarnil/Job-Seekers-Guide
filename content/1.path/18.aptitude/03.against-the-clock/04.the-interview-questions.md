Nobody sits you down in a technical interview and asks for the next term in a
series. They ask these instead, and every one is a shape from this track wearing
work clothes.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. The conversion rate went from 4% to 5%. How much did it improve?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  One **percentage point**, which is a **25% relative** improvement.

  Then the judgement, which is what is being marked: which one I report depends
  on what the reader is deciding. "Up 25%" from a base of 4% is true and
  reliably misleads, so I would put both on the screen.

  Asked constantly in product and analytics rounds, and the candidate who only
  gives one number has failed it without knowing.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="2. Estimate how many applications this university gets a year." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  A Fermi question. They do not want the number; they want to watch you
  decompose.

  Say the decomposition out loud: twelve programmes, average intake around 100
  seats, so about 1,200 places. Admissions of this kind typically see three to
  five applications per seat, so roughly 4,000 to 6,000 a year.

  Then the check: that assumes every programme is equally competitive, and in
  practice CSE will be ten applications a seat while some are undersubscribed,
  so the real number is probably at the upper end.

  Stating your assumptions and then attacking them is the entire answer.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="3. The import does 400 rows a second, 1.8 million rows, two-hour window." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  `1,800,000 ÷ 400 = 4,500` seconds, so 75 minutes. It fits with 45 minutes
  spare.

  Then the part that separates you: that rate was probably measured on an empty
  table and will degrade as indexes grow. I would want it measured at the 1.5
  million mark before committing, and I would make the import resumable so a
  failure at minute 70 does not mean starting again.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="4. Two servers process a queue. One takes 6 hours, one takes 3. Together?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Two hours. Rates add: `1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2` of the queue per hour.

  Then the sanity check said aloud, because it is the habit being tested: it
  must be faster than the faster server alone, and two is less than three.

  Then the honest caveat, if they are technical: that assumes the two do not
  contend for the same resource. If both are writing to one database, the
  combined rate is not the sum, and I would measure rather than assume.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="5. Here is a chart. What do you notice?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Do not describe it back to them. Name the anomaly, then doubt your own reading
  of it.

  "Everything is growing except ECE, which has fallen three years running. That
  is the only thing here that needs explaining. Before reading anything into it
  I would want to know whether the intake capacity changed, because a fall in
  applications and a fall in advertised seats look identical on this chart."
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="6. You have four tasks and time for two." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Cheapest first when the value is equal, and say why it is arithmetic rather
  than preference: four tasks worth the same, one costing four times as much,
  means the other three come first.

  Then the part people miss: decide fast and revisit. A two-minute triage that
  is 80% right beats a ten-minute one that is 95% right, because the eight
  minutes came out of the work.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="7. Every time the server crashed, memory was high. Memory is high now." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  This is affirming the consequent, and naming it is the answer.

  All crashes had high memory. That does not make high memory sufficient for a
  crash; it might be present during normal load too. What I would actually want
  is the base rate: how often is memory high **without** a crash. If that is
  most of the time, memory is not the signal.

  A correlation observed only on the failures is the single most common bad
  inference in incident reviews.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="8. Can you do mental arithmetic? What is 17 × 24?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  408, and say how you got there, because the method is the point.

  `17 × 24 = 17 × 25 − 17 = 425 − 17 = 408`. Or `17 × 24 = 17 × 20 + 17 × 4 =
  340 + 68 = 408`.

  Narrating the decomposition matters more than the speed. Somebody who says
  "roughly 400, let me be exact" and then is exact looks far better than
  somebody who is silent for six seconds and then correct.
  :::
::

## What every answer has in common

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-mic" title="The method is said out loud"}
  In an aptitude paper only the answer counts. In a room, the answer counts for
  almost nothing and the route counts for everything, which is an inversion
  worth preparing for.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-search-x" title="The assumption gets attacked"}
  Every answer above ends by naming what would make it wrong. That is the
  difference between arithmetic and judgement, and judgement is what is being
  hired.
  :::
::
