Twenty questions of arithmetic done. The other half of the paper has almost none
in it, and that is precisely why it costs strong candidates marks: the
difficulty moved from calculating to reading, and nobody warned them.

## Why this matters in a job

Genuinely, not as a stretch: a number series is pattern recognition against
incomplete information under time pressure, which is debugging. You have three
data points and you are inferring the rule. The habit this lesson teaches,
checking the obvious differences before inventing something clever, is exactly
the habit that makes somebody good at reading a stack trace.

## The checklist

A series question gives you five or six numbers and asks for the next. There is
a fixed order to check, and following it mechanically beats staring.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Work down the list. Stop at the first rule that fits every gap. Do not keep looking for something more elegant."}
  :::flow-step{title="Differences"}
  Subtract each from the next. Constant? Arithmetic, done in five seconds.

  `4, 11, 18, 25, …` differences are 7, 7, 7 → **32**.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="Differences of the differences" highlight}
  Not constant? Do it again. This catches most of the ones that look hard.

  `2, 5, 11, 20, 32, …` differences 3, 6, 9, 12 → next difference 15 → **47**.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="Ratios"}
  Divide each by the previous. Constant? Geometric.

  `3, 12, 48, 192, …` ratio 4 → **768**.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="Squares and cubes nearby"}
  Is each term close to a square or cube? Numbers ending 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36 or
  1, 8, 27, 64, 125 should make you look.

  `2, 5, 10, 17, 26, …` is `n² + 1` → **37**.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="Two series interleaved"}
  Look at alternate positions. Very common and invisible until you check.

  `3, 20, 6, 17, 9, 14, …` is `3, 6, 9` rising by 3 and `20, 17, 14` falling by
  3 → **12**.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="Each term operates on the last"}
  `× 2 + 1`, `× 3 − 2`, and so on.

  `2, 5, 11, 23, 47, …` is `× 2 + 1` → **95**.
  :::
::

::pull-quote
Stop at the first rule that explains every gap. A series with six terms and a
rule that fits five of them is the wrong rule, and a rule that fits all six is
the answer even if it feels too simple.
::

## The trap

You are looking for the most elegant pattern. The examiner wrote the most
ordinary one.

::compare{caption="`1, 4, 9, 16, 25, …` and the two ways people answer it."}
  :::compare-side{label="Looking for something clever" verdict="wrong"}
  "These are squares, but maybe it is squares of primes offset by, or a
  Fibonacci relationship where…"

  Ninety seconds spent, no answer, and the next four questions unattempted.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="The ordinary answer" verdict="right"}
  Squares. **36**.

  Check it fits every term. It does. Move on in eight seconds.
  :::
::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
This is a time-management failure disguised as a difficulty. The cost of a hard
series question is almost never the mark for that question. It is the three
questions at the end of the paper you never reached.
::

## Letter series

The same checklist with one extra step: convert to numbers first.

Write `A=1` through `Z=26` in the margin at the start of the exam. It takes
twenty seconds and it makes every letter question arithmetic.

```text [C, F, I, L, …]
C=3, F=6, I=9, L=12   → differences of 3   → 15 = O
```

::tip
Also write the position from the **end**: `Z=1, Y=2, X=3`. A surprising number
of questions are counting backwards, and doing that in your head under pressure
is where mistakes happen.
::

## Odd one out

A variant that trips people because they find a reason for every option.

> `16, 25, 36, 48, 64`

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="The method" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Find what **most** of them share, then the one that does not.

  16, 25, 36 and 64 are perfect squares. 48 is not. Answer: **48**.

  The failure mode: noticing 48 is the only multiple of 3, that 25 is the only
  odd one, and that 64 is the only cube, and concluding it is ambiguous. It is
  not. The property shared by four of the five is the property, and the others
  are coincidences of small numbers.

  **The rule that fits four is the rule.** Say it to yourself and move on.
  :::
::

## Syllogisms, in one rule

Worth putting here because it is the same skill: read exactly what is written,
not what is plausible.

> **Statements:** All applicants are students. Some students are hostellers.
> **Conclusion:** Some applicants are hostellers.

Does it follow?

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="No, and why" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  It does not. "Some students are hostellers" tells you nothing about *which*
  students, and the hostellers could be entirely among the students who are not
  applicants.

  Draw it. Two overlapping circles where the overlap avoids the applicant
  circle entirely is consistent with both statements and makes the conclusion
  false. **One consistent drawing where the conclusion fails is enough to reject
  it.**

  The rule for the whole topic: a conclusion follows only if it is true in
  **every** arrangement consistent with the statements. Not the likely
  arrangement. Every one.

  This is where real-world knowledge actively hurts. If the statements say all
  buildings are made of paper, you accept that for the duration of the question.
  :::
::

## What you can do now

You can work down a fixed checklist instead of staring, convert letters to
numbers in advance, stop at the first rule that fits, and reject a syllogism by
finding one arrangement that breaks it.

## Your turn

Fifteen seconds each. If it has not resolved, mark it and move on.

1. `7, 14, 28, 56, …`
2. `3, 7, 15, 31, …`
3. `1, 4, 9, 18, 35, …`
4. `B, E, H, K, …`
5. `5, 11, 23, 47, 95, …`
6. Odd one out: `14, 21, 35, 42, 56`
7. `2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, …`
8. `81, 64, 49, 36, …`
9. `4, 9, 20, 43, 90, …`
10. **Statements:** Some faculty are researchers. All researchers are graduates.
    **Conclusion:** Some faculty are graduates.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answers" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **1.** Ratio 2 → **112**.

  **2.** `× 2 + 1` → **63**.

  **3.** Differences 3, 5, 9, 17: each is `× 2 − 1`. Next difference 33 →
  **68**.

  **4.** `2, 5, 8, 11` rising by 3 → 14 = **N**.

  **5.** `× 2 + 1` → **191**.

  **6.** All are multiples of 7. 14, 21, 35 and 42 are, and so is 56. Look
  again: 14, 21, 35, 42 have digit sums 5, 3, 8, 6 and are all multiples of 7,
  but **35 is the only one not even**. Answer: **35**. This one is deliberately
  nasty and teaches the real lesson, which is that when the first shared
  property covers all five, keep looking for a second.

  **7.** Primes → **17**.

  **8.** `9², 8², 7², 6²` → `5² =` **25**.

  **9.** `× 2 + 1`, `× 2 + 2`, `× 2 + 3`, `× 2 + 4`: `4×2+1=9`, `9×2+2=20`,
  `20×2+3=43`, `43×2+4=90`, so `90×2+5 =` **185**.

  **10.** **It follows.** Some faculty are researchers, and every researcher is
  a graduate, so those faculty are graduates. Contrast with the earlier one: here
  the "some" group is fully contained in the graduates, so there is no
  arrangement where it fails.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why is spending ninety seconds on a series question a worse mistake than
   getting it wrong?
2. **An interviewer asks a puzzle you have not seen.** What is the first thing
   you say?

## The interview question this answers

That second one is real, and the answer is not a technique. It is: **say your
approach out loud before you start.**

"I am going to check the differences first, then the ratios, then whether it is
two series interleaved." If you get stuck, the interviewer knows where you are
and can nudge you. If you stare in silence for ninety seconds, they learn
nothing except that you go quiet under pressure.

The same is true of the coding round, which is why this habit is worth building
here where the stakes are low.
