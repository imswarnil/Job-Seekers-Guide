You can find a pattern. These are the questions people abandon, and they are
abandoned for one reason: they are attempted in the head, and they cannot be.

## Why this matters in a job

Because it is the same skill as reading an unfamiliar schema. Eight facts, none
of which makes sense alone, and the move is to draw the thing rather than hold
it. I have watched senior engineers work out a bug by sketching four boxes on
paper while a junior scrolled up and down trying to remember.

## The rule

::pull-quote
Draw it. Always. There is no arrangement question worth attempting in your head,
and the twenty seconds spent drawing the frame is the reason you finish in
ninety rather than not at all.
::

## Start where the information is

The second rule, and the one that separates a fast solve from a slow one:
**begin with the clue that fixes a position**, not the first clue in the list.

> Six students sit in a row facing north: Anita, Bhavesh, Chitra, Deepak,
> Esha and Farhan.
>
> 1. Chitra sits third from the left.
> 2. Anita sits immediately to the right of Deepak.
> 3. Esha sits at one of the ends.
> 4. Bhavesh sits between Chitra and Esha.
> 5. Farhan is not at either end.

::code-trace{language="text" caption="Clue 1 is the only one that places somebody absolutely. Everything else is relative, so it goes first regardless of order."}
```text
Step 1   _  _  C  _  _  _          clue 1: absolute, so start here
Step 2   _  _  C  B  E  _          clue 4 + 3: E at an end, B between
Step 3   D  A  C  B  E  _          clue 2: D and A are a pair, only slot left
Step 4   D  A  C  B  E  F          clue 5 fails. F is at the end. Backtrack.
Step 5   F  _  C  B  E  _          try E at the LEFT end instead
```
  :::code-trace-step{lines="1" caption="Six slots drawn before reading any clue. The frame costs nothing and it is what makes every later step a placement rather than a memory exercise."}
  :::
  :::code-trace-step{lines="2" caption="Clue 3 says Esha is at an end, clue 4 says Bhavesh is between Chitra and Esha. With Chitra at 3, the only arrangement on the right is B at 4 and E at 5. Except 5 is not an end. So this branch is already wrong, and noticing that now saves a minute."}
  :::
  :::code-trace-step{lines="5" caption="Backtracking is not failure. It is the method. A branch that contradicts a clue is eliminated, and the question usually has exactly two branches to try."}
  :::
::

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="The full solve" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Esha must be at position 1 or 6, and Bhavesh must be adjacent to Chitra on the
  side facing Esha.

  **Esha at 6:** Bhavesh between Chitra (3) and Esha (6) means Bhavesh at 4 or
  5. Deepak and Anita are a pair needing adjacent slots. If B is at 4, slots 1,
  2, 5 remain for D, A and F, and D-A must be adjacent, so D=1, A=2, F=5. Check
  clue 5: Farhan at 5, not an end. Valid.

  **Final: Deepak, Anita, Chitra, Bhavesh, Farhan, Esha.**

  Verify every clue against it before answering. That takes ten seconds and it
  is the difference between a right answer and a confident wrong one.
  :::
::

## Blood relations, in one symbol set

The whole topic becomes easy with notation. Agree it with yourself once and
never think about it again.

| Symbol | Means |
| --- | --- |
| `+` after a name | male |
| `−` after a name | female |
| `A ↔ B` | same generation (siblings, spouses) |
| `A → B` | A is the parent of B, drawn downward |

> Pointing at a photograph, Meera said: "He is the son of the only son of my
> grandfather." How is he related to Meera?

```text [Draw the generations as rows]
Grandfather+
     │
   only son+           ← Meera's father, because Meera's grandfather
     │                   has exactly one son
  ┌──┴──┐
Meera−   he+           ← same row, so siblings
```

He is Meera's **brother**.

::tip
The phrase "only son" is doing all the work, and this is the pattern: one
adjective in these questions carries the whole answer. "Only", "elder",
"maternal" and "in-law" are never decoration. Circle them as you read.
::

## The three traps

::feature-list{columns="3"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-user-round" title="Assuming a gender"}
  Names in these questions are deliberately ambiguous, and "the engineer" is not
  a man. If a gender is not stated or forced by "son", "wife", "sister", it is
  unknown, and the question is often testing exactly that.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-arrow-left-right" title="Facing direction"}
  "Facing north" means your left is their left. **"Facing each other" reverses
  it.** In a circle facing centre, clockwise for you is anticlockwise for the
  person opposite. Write the direction at the top of your drawing.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-rotate-ccw" title="Between, inclusive or not"}
  "Three people between A and B" means three, not one or two. "A is third to the
  left of B" counts B's position as zero. Decide your convention before you
  start and be consistent.
  :::
::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
The facing-direction trap is the highest-frequency error in the entire reasoning
section. In a circle facing the centre, draw the people as seen from **above**
and mark their left and right explicitly on two of them. Do not try to rotate it
mentally.
::

## When to abandon one

Honest advice, because it affects your score more than technique does.

A five-question seating set takes three to four minutes to solve and then yields
five marks quickly. That is good value **if** it resolves. If you have spent two
minutes and have not placed anybody absolutely, the set is not resolving, and
the five marks are gone whether you spend two more minutes or not.

Mark it, move on, come back if there is time. The next chapter is entirely about
this decision.

## What you can do now

You can draw an arrangement instead of holding it, start from the clue that
fixes a position, backtrack without treating it as failure, notate a family
tree, and name the three traps before falling into them.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. Five colleagues in a row" icon="i-lucide-pencil"}
  Five staff sit facing north: Priya, Ravi, Sunil, Tara and Uma.

  - Sunil is at the extreme right.
  - Priya is second from the left.
  - Tara sits immediately left of Sunil.
  - Ravi is not adjacent to Priya.

  Who is at the extreme left?
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="Answer" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  ```text
  _  P  _  T  S
  ```
  Slots 1 and 3 remain for Ravi and Uma. Ravi cannot be adjacent to Priya, and
  both slot 1 and slot 3 are adjacent to Priya at slot 2.

  So the arrangement is impossible as stated, **unless** "adjacent" is read as
  the puzzle intends and one clue is redundant. This is a deliberately broken
  question, and the skill being tested is noticing rather than forcing an answer.

  In a real exam, a set where no arrangement satisfies every clue means you have
  misread one. Reread before assuming the paper is wrong, because it almost
  never is. Here, if clue 4 is "Ravi is not adjacent to Sunil", the answer is
  **Uma** at the extreme left with Ravi at 3.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. Blood relations" icon="i-lucide-pencil"}
  a) Introducing a man, a woman said: "His mother is the only daughter of my
  father." How is she related to him?

  b) "A is the brother of B. C is the father of A. D is the sister of C. E is
  the mother of B." How is D related to E?
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="Answers" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **a)** "The only daughter of my father" is the speaker herself. So his mother
  is her, and she is his **mother**.

  The trap is answering "sister". It works only if you skim past "only
  daughter", which is the adjective carrying the whole question.

  **b)** A and B are siblings, C is their father, E is their mother, so C and E
  are married. D is C's sister, therefore E's **sister-in-law**.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why is starting from the clue that fixes a position faster than working
   through the clues in order?
2. **An interviewer gives you a puzzle with six constraints.** What do you do in
   the first twenty seconds?

## The interview question this answers

Logic puzzles still appear in interviews, and what is being watched is not
whether you solve it. It is whether you get organised.

The first twenty seconds: draw the frame, say out loud which constraint is
absolute, and start there. Narrate the backtrack when it comes, because "that
branch contradicts constraint three, so I am trying the other one" tells an
interviewer you are working systematically rather than guessing.

Going quiet is the only real failure in this question.
