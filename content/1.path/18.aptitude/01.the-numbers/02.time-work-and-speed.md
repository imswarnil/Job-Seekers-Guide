Percentages compare what is already there. This shape is about things happening
over time, and it looks like four different topics until you see that it is one.

## Why this matters in a job

Beyond the exam: this is the arithmetic of throughput. "The import processes 400
rows a second, there are 1.8 million rows, how long is the maintenance window?"
is a time-and-work problem, and getting it wrong in a planning meeting is
noticed.

## The one trick

::pull-quote
Do not work with times. Work with rates: how much of the job gets done in one
unit of time. Rates add. Times do not.
::

That is the entire lesson. Everything below is that sentence applied.

Two people can paint a hall. Ravi takes 6 days alone, Meera takes 3. Together?

::compare{caption="The instinct, and the method."}
  :::compare-side{label="Averaging the times" verdict="wrong"}
  `(6 + 3) ÷ 2 = 4.5 days`

  Silently wrong, and wrong in a way you can catch without any method: two
  people working together must be **faster than the faster one alone**. Meera
  does it in 3 days by herself. An answer of 4.5 cannot be right.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Adding the rates" verdict="right"}
  Ravi does `1/6` of the hall per day. Meera does `1/3`.

  Together: `1/6 + 1/3 = 1/6 + 2/6 = 3/6 = 1/2` of the hall per day.

  Half a hall per day means **2 days**. Faster than Meera alone, as it must be.
  :::
::

::tip
Always sanity-check a combined-work answer against the fastest worker. It must
be smaller. That one check catches every arithmetic slip in this shape and takes
two seconds.
::

## The four faces of the same question

::tabs
  :::tabs-item{label="Work done together" icon="i-lucide-users"}
  Three staff enter exam results. Alone they take 10, 15 and 30 hours.

  ```text
  Rates: 1/10 + 1/15 + 1/30
  Common denominator 30:  3/30 + 2/30 + 1/30 = 6/30 = 1/5
  1/5 of the job per hour → 5 hours
  ```

  Check: faster than the fastest (10 hours). Correct.
  :::
  :::tabs-item{label="Someone leaves partway" icon="i-lucide-user-minus"}
  Ravi and Meera start together. After 1 day Ravi leaves. How long in total?

  ```text
  Day 1 together: 1/2 done, so 1/2 remains
  Meera alone at 1/3 per day: (1/2) ÷ (1/3) = 3/2 = 1.5 days
  Total: 2.5 days
  ```

  The pattern: work out what fraction is done, then divide the remainder by the
  remaining rate.
  :::
  :::tabs-item{label="Pipes and drains" icon="i-lucide-droplet"}
  Identical, with one rate **negative**. A tank fills in 4 hours and leaks empty
  in 6.

  ```text
  1/4 − 1/6 = 3/12 − 2/12 = 1/12 per hour → 12 hours
  ```

  If the drain is faster than the fill, the rate goes negative and the tank
  never fills. The question sometimes wants exactly that answer.
  :::
  :::tabs-item{label="Trains and travel" icon="i-lucide-train-front"}
  Two trains approach each other at 60 and 40 km/h, 200 km apart.

  ```text
  Closing rate: 60 + 40 = 100 km/h
  200 ÷ 100 = 2 hours
  ```

  Same as two people painting. Moving apart? Subtract instead of add. It is one
  idea wearing four costumes.
  :::
::

## The average speed trap

This one is worth its own section because the obvious answer is wrong and almost
everybody gives it.

> You travel from your room in Bangalore to the campus at 30 km/h, and back
> along the same road at 60 km/h. What is your average speed?

Not 45.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Why it is not 45, and what it is" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Because you spend **twice as long** at 30 km/h as you do at 60 km/h. The slow
  half dominates, so the average must be below the midpoint.

  Average speed is always `total distance ÷ total time`, never the average of
  the speeds.

  ```text
  Let the distance be 60 km each way (pick a number both divide into).
  Out:   60 ÷ 30 = 2 hours
  Back:  60 ÷ 60 = 1 hour
  Total: 120 km in 3 hours = 40 km/h
  ```

  For the same distance at two speeds there is a shortcut, the harmonic mean:
  `2ab ÷ (a + b)` = `2 × 30 × 60 ÷ 90` = **40**.

  The rule to carry: **average of speeds is only correct when the times are
  equal. Average speed over equal distances is always lower.**
  :::
::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Every exam uses this. If a question gives you two speeds and asks for an
average, the answer they are hoping you give is the midpoint, and one of the
four options will be exactly that. Seeing your first answer sitting in the
options list is not reassurance.
::

## Units, said once

More marks are lost here than on method.

- km/h to m/s: **× 5/18**. m/s to km/h: **× 18/5**.
- A train passing a pole travels its own length. Passing a 200m platform, it
  travels its length plus 200.

That second one is the entire difficulty of most train questions.

## What you can do now

You can convert any time-and-work, pipes, or relative-speed question into rates,
add them, and sanity-check the answer against the fastest component. And you
will never again average two speeds.

## Your turn

1. A fee report takes one clerk 12 hours and another 6. Together?
2. Three staff take 20, 30 and 60 hours. Together?
3. A tank fills in 5 hours, drains in 8. Time to fill?
4. Two buses leave the same campus in opposite directions at 45 and 55 km/h. How
   far apart after 3 hours?
5. A student cycles to campus at 10 km/h and walks back at 5 km/h. Average
   speed?
6. A 150m train passes a 250m platform in 20 seconds. Its speed in km/h?

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answers" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **1.** `1/12 + 1/6 = 1/12 + 2/12 = 3/12 = 1/4` → **4 hours**. Under 6, so it
  passes the check.

  **2.** `1/20 + 1/30 + 1/60 = 3/60 + 2/60 + 1/60 = 6/60 = 1/10` → **10 hours**.

  **3.** `1/5 − 1/8 = 8/40 − 5/40 = 3/40` → **40/3 = 13.33 hours**.

  **4.** Moving apart, so add: `100 km/h × 3 = 300 km`.

  **5.** `2 × 10 × 5 ÷ 15 = 100/15 = 6.67 km/h`. Not 7.5.

  **6.** Total distance is `150 + 250 = 400m` in 20 seconds, so `20 m/s`. Then
  `20 × 18/5 = 72 km/h`. Forgetting to add the train's own length gives 45 and
  it will be one of the options.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why must two people working together always finish faster than the quicker
   one alone, and how does that catch arithmetic errors?
2. **An interviewer asks: "the import runs at 400 rows a second and there are
   1.8 million rows. We have a two-hour window. Does it fit?"**

## The interview question this answers

That second one is a real planning question and it is this lesson exactly.
`1,800,000 ÷ 400 = 4,500` seconds, which is 75 minutes, so yes with 45 minutes
of headroom.

Then the part that separates you: say what would change the answer. That rate
was measured on an empty table, and it will degrade as indexes grow. I would
want the rate measured at the 1.5 million mark before committing to the window,
and I would make the import resumable so a failure at minute 70 does not mean
starting again.
