Four weeks, an hour a day, and the round stops being the thing that ends your
applications. This is the first shape, and it is underneath more questions than
any other.

## Why this matters in a job

Not the exam. The actual job.

Every dashboard you built in the last track is a percentage with a denominator.
"Fee collection is at 82%", "attendance below 75%", "conversion from shortlisted
to accepted is 77%". An engineer who is shaky on percentage change writes
reports that are confidently wrong, and I have shipped one.

## The only two formulas

Everything in this lesson is one of these.

::flow{numbered caption="Learn which one the question is asking for. That decision is the whole difficulty."}
  :::flow-step{title="Percentage of a number"}
  `part = whole × rate ÷ 100`

  Of 412 applications, 23% were shortlisted: `412 × 23 ÷ 100 = 94.76`, so 94 or
  95 depending on how the university rounds.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="Percentage change" highlight}
  `change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100`

  **Divided by the old value.** This is the single most common mistake, and it
  is the reason the next section exists.
  :::
::

## The direction trap

Read these two sentences. They describe different numbers and people treat them
as the same.

::compare{caption="Applications went from 400 to 500."}
  :::compare-side{label="The increase" verdict="neutral"}
  `(500 − 400) ÷ 400 = 25%`

  An increase of **25%**, because the starting point was 400.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Now go back down" verdict="neutral"}
  `(400 − 500) ÷ 500 = −20%`

  A decrease of **20%**, because now the starting point is 500.
  :::
::

Up 25%, back down 20%, same two numbers. If you find that surprising, that
feeling is worth keeping, because examiners rely on it.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
**A 25% rise followed by a 25% fall does not return you to where you started.**
100 → 125 → 93.75. You are down 6.25%, and the reason is that the fall was
calculated on a bigger number than the rise was.
::

## Successive change, the fast way

This is the shape that appears in almost every paper, and doing it in two steps
costs you thirty seconds you do not have.

::pull-quote
Turn every percentage into a multiplier and multiply them together. A 20%
increase is × 1.2. A 20% decrease is × 0.8. Nothing else is needed.
::

A programme's fee rises 10% one year and 20% the next. Total rise?

```text [The slow way, and the fast way]
Slow:  45,000 × 1.10 = 49,500
       49,500 × 1.20 = 59,400
       (59,400 − 45,000) ÷ 45,000 = 32%

Fast:  1.10 × 1.20 = 1.32  →  32%
```

The fast way needs no starting number at all, which is the point: the question
usually does not give you one.

::tip
Two successive changes of `a%` and `b%` come to `a + b + (ab ÷ 100)` percent.
For 10 and 20: `10 + 20 + 2 = 32`. For a 25% rise then a 25% fall:
`25 − 25 − 6.25 = −6.25`. Worth memorising, because it turns a calculation into
a recall.
::

## Ratios are one multiplier

A ratio question looks harder than it is, because people try to work with both
numbers.

> The ratio of students to faculty in the Computer Science department is 22:3.
> There are 66 faculty. How many students?

::code-trace{language="text" caption="Find the multiplier, then multiply. Two steps, every time."}
```text
22 : 3  is the pattern
66 faculty, and the pattern says 3
66 ÷ 3 = 22   ← the multiplier
22 × 22 = 484 students
```
  :::code-trace-step{lines="1" caption="The ratio is a shape, not a quantity. It says 'for every 22 students there are 3 faculty' and nothing about size."}
  :::
  :::code-trace-step{lines="3" caption="The multiplier is the real number divided by its position in the ratio. This is the only calculation in the question."}
  :::
  :::code-trace-step{lines="4" caption="Apply it to the other side. Any ratio problem, any number of parts, is these two steps."}
  :::
::

For three parts it is identical. Departments split seats 5:3:2 over 300 seats:
the parts total 10, so the multiplier is 30, and the seats are 150, 90 and 60.

## The one that catches everybody

> In a university, 60% of applicants are from the state and 40% from outside.
> 30% of state applicants and 45% of outside applicants are shortlisted. What
> percentage of **shortlisted** applicants are from outside the state?

Almost everybody answers 45%. It is wrong, and the reason is worth understanding
because it is the same error as a dashboard with no denominator.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="The method, and why 45% is wrong" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  45% is the percentage of **outside applicants** who were shortlisted. The
  question asks for the percentage of **shortlisted people** who are from
  outside. Different denominators, different question.

  Assume 100 applicants, because the answer is a percentage and the actual size
  cannot matter.

  ```text
  State:    60 applicants × 30% = 18 shortlisted
  Outside:  40 applicants × 45% = 18 shortlisted
  Total shortlisted             = 36

  From outside: 18 ÷ 36 = 50%
  ```

  **Assume 100.** That single habit turns most percentage word problems into
  arithmetic you can do in your head, and it is legitimate because a percentage
  answer cannot depend on the total.
  :::
::

## What you can do now

You can calculate percentage change in the right direction, chain successive
changes with a single multiplication, solve any ratio with one multiplier, and
spot a question that has swapped the denominator on you.

## Your turn

Sixty seconds each, on paper.

1. A programme had 120 seats and now has 96. What is the percentage decrease?
2. A fee falls 15% and then rises 15%. Where does it end up?
3. Students to faculty is 18:1. There are 54 faculty. How many students?
4. Of 400 applications, 35% are shortlisted and 40% of those are accepted. How
   many are accepted?
5. Attendance rose from 68% to 76%. By how many percent did it rise, and why is
   "8%" wrong?

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answers" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **1.** `(96 − 120) ÷ 120 = −20%`. A 20% decrease. Dividing by 96 gives 25% and
  is the classic error.

  **2.** `0.85 × 1.15 = 0.9775`, so 97.75% of the original: **down 2.25%**. Not
  back where it started, for the same reason as the 25% example.

  **3.** Multiplier is `54 ÷ 1 = 54`, so `18 × 54 = 972` students.

  **4.** `400 × 0.35 = 140` shortlisted, `140 × 0.40 = 56` accepted. Or in one
  step: `400 × 0.35 × 0.40 = 56`.

  **5.** It rose by **8 percentage points**, which is a rise of
  `8 ÷ 68 = 11.8%`. "8%" confuses a percentage point with a percentage, and this
  distinction appears in real reports constantly. If a dashboard says attendance
  "rose 8%" when it went from 68 to 76, it is wrong, and now you are the person
  who notices.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why does a 30% rise followed by a 30% fall leave you below where you started?
2. **An interviewer asks: "the conversion rate went from 4% to 5%. Is that a 1%
   improvement or a 25% improvement?"**

## The interview question this answers

That second question is asked in analytics and product interviews and it is
testing exactly one thing: whether you distinguish a **percentage point** from a
**percentage**.

The answer: it is a rise of one percentage point, which is a 25% relative
improvement, and which of those two to report depends on what the reader is
deciding. Then the sentence that finishes it: I would put both on the screen,
because "up 25%" alone from a base of 4% is technically true and reliably
misleads.
