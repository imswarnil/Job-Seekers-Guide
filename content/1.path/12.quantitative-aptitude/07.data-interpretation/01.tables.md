Progressions were the last formula topic. Data interpretation (DI) is different
in kind: one set of numbers, four or five questions about it, and the
arithmetic is the percentages and ratios you already know. This page is the
most common form, the table.

## Why it matters

DI is a fixed part of most written rounds. TCS NQT, Infosys, Wipro, Accenture,
Cognizant, Capgemini and AMCAT-style papers usually include at least one DI
set, often three to five questions on one table or chart. Check the current
pattern, but plan for a set in every paper.

A DI set is either your best five minutes or your worst. Every question uses
the same data, so once you have read the table properly, each question is fast.
Read it badly and you lose all five.

## What to study

| # | Question type on a table | How often |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Percentage change between two years | Every set |
| 2 | "Which is the highest / fastest-growing?" | Very common |
| 3 | Percentage of a total | Very common |
| 4 | Ratio between two cells | Common |
| 5 | Average across a row or column | Common |
| 6 | Combining two columns (count × rate) | Common, and slow |

## The four habits

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-scan-eye" title="Headers and units first"}
  Before any question. Thousands or lakhs? Is the percentage column for one
  year or all of them? Twenty seconds here saves two wrong answers.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-pointer" title="Track the row physically"}
  Finger, pen, or the cursor. The eye drifts one row up when it moves right
  across a table, and it does not tell you.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-divide" title="Estimate before calculating"}
  If the question asks "which", you rarely need the exact number. An estimate
  that separates the options is enough.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-forward" title="Cheapest questions first"}
  One question in a set usually costs four times the others. Same mark. Do it
  last.
  :::
::

## The formulas you use here

```text
Percentage change   = (new − old) ÷ old × 100
Share of total      = part ÷ total × 100
Average             = sum ÷ count
Ratio a : b         → divide both by their HCF
Fastest growth      = biggest percentage change, not biggest absolute change
```

::tip
Learn the fraction-percentage table cold: 1/2 = 50, 1/3 = 33.3, 1/4 = 25,
1/5 = 20, 1/6 = 16.7, 1/7 ≈ 14.3, 1/8 = 12.5, 1/9 ≈ 11.1, 1/11 ≈ 9.1,
1/12 ≈ 8.3. Half of DI approximation is spotting that 260 on 400 is "about
two thirds".
::

## A worked example

Applications received by a college, by programme:

| Programme | 2024 | 2025 | 2026 | % shortlisted (2026 only) |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| B.Tech CSE | 1,200 | 1,450 | 1,740 | 25% |
| B.Tech ECE | 900 | 860 | 800 | 30% |
| B.Sc Maths | 400 | 500 | 660 | 40% |
| MBA | 500 | 590 | 800 | 15% |
| **Total** | **3,000** | **3,400** | **4,000** | |

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. Percentage increase in total applications, 2024 to 2026?"}
  `(4,000 − 3,000) ÷ 3,000 = 33.33%`. The only way to lose it is dividing by
  4,000 (which gives 25%, and 25% will be an option).
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. Which programme grew fastest, 2024 to 2026?"}
  Scan, do not calculate four percentages.

  ```text
  CSE    1,200 → 1,740   +540 on 1,200, about +45%
  ECE      900 →   800   fell. Eliminate.
  Maths    400 →   660   +260 on 400 = +65%
  MBA      500 →   800   +300 on 500 = +60%
  ```

  **B.Sc Maths.** The trap is CSE, because 540 is the biggest increase.
  "Fastest" is relative.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. How many CSE applicants were shortlisted in 2026?"}
  `1,740 × 25% = 435`. The mark is lost by using the 2025 figure because the
  eye slid along the wrong row.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="4. Total shortlisted across all programmes in 2026?"}
  ```text
  CSE   1,740 × 0.25 = 435
  ECE     800 × 0.30 = 240
  Maths   660 × 0.40 = 264
  MBA     800 × 0.15 = 120
  Total              1,059
  ```

  Four multiplications and an addition, about 40 seconds. This is the one to do
  last.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="5. Ratio of ECE to MBA applications in 2025?"}
  `860 : 590 = 86 : 59`. The trap is reading 2026 and answering `1 : 1`.
  :::
::

## Common mistakes

- **Wrong denominator** in percentage change. Always the old value.
- **Biggest versus fastest.** Absolute versus relative.
- **Wrong year or row.** Put a finger on it.
- **Applying a one-year column to every year.** The % column above is 2026
  only; questions will tempt you to use it for 2025.
- **Calculating exactly for a "which" question.**

## Practice questions

Using the same table:

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. Which programme had the largest absolute fall, 2024 to 2026?"}
  **ECE**, down 100. The only programme that fell.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. What percentage of 2026 applications were for CSE?"}
  **43.5%.** `1,740 ÷ 4,000`.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. If MBA shortlisting had been 30% instead of 15%, how many more shortlisted in 2026?"}
  **120.** Doubling the rate doubles the count, so the increase equals the
  original 120.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="4. Average applications per programme in 2025?"}
  **850.** `3,400 ÷ 4`.
  :::
::
