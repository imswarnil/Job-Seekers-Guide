You know how the paper is built and how to spend the minutes. The fastest
minutes in it are number questions, because most of them are one rule applied
once. This page covers what those rules are and which ones actually get asked.

## Why it matters

Every company's quant section has two to four questions that are pure number
system: "which of these is divisible by 11", "the unit digit of 7 to the power
95", "how many zeros at the end of 100 factorial". TCS NQT and AMCAT-style tests
ask them most. They take fifteen to thirty seconds when you know the rule and
several minutes when you try to calculate.

More important: divisibility and remainders sit underneath HCF and LCM,
simplification and half of data interpretation. Weakness here leaks into
everything.

## What to study

In priority order:

- [ ] **Divisibility rules** for 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10 and 11
- [ ] **Unit digit** of a large power (cyclicity)
- [ ] **Remainders**: of a product, of a sum, of a power
- [ ] **Number of factors** of a number
- [ ] **Trailing zeros** in a factorial
- [ ] **Types of numbers**: natural, whole, integer, rational, prime, composite, co-prime
- [ ] Sums: first n natural numbers, first n odd numbers, squares

## The kinds of numbers

| Word | Means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural | Counting numbers | 1, 2, 3 … |
| Whole | Natural numbers and 0 | 0, 1, 2 … |
| Integer | Whole numbers and their negatives | -3, 0, 7 |
| Prime | Exactly two factors, 1 and itself | 2, 3, 5, 7, 11. 2 is the only even prime |
| Composite | More than two factors | 4, 6, 9, 15 |
| Co-prime | Two numbers with HCF 1 | 8 and 15 |

::tip
**1 is neither prime nor composite.** It appears as an option in "how many
primes between 1 and 20" questions because people count it. There are 8 primes
below 20: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19.
::

## The divisibility rules

| Divisible by | Rule | Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | Last digit even | 3,578 yes |
| 3 | Sum of digits divisible by 3 | 4,587: 4+5+8+7 = 24, yes |
| 4 | Last two digits divisible by 4 | 2,728: 28, yes |
| 5 | Last digit 0 or 5 | |
| 6 | Divisible by both 2 and 3 | |
| 8 | Last three digits divisible by 8 | 94,352: 352 = 8 x 44, yes |
| 9 | Sum of digits divisible by 9 | 8,712: 8+7+1+2 = 18, yes |
| 10 | Last digit 0 | |
| 11 | Alternating sum of digits is 0 or a multiple of 11 | 1,331: 1-3+3-1 = 0, yes |

For a composite divisor, split it into co-prime parts. Divisible by 12 means
divisible by 3 and by 4 (not 2 and 6, because 2 and 6 are not co-prime).

## Unit digits and cyclicity

The last digit of a power repeats in a cycle of at most four.

```text [Unit digit cycles]
2: 2, 4, 8, 6        (cycle of 4)
3: 3, 9, 7, 1        (cycle of 4)
4: 4, 6              (cycle of 2)
7: 7, 9, 3, 1        (cycle of 4)
8: 8, 4, 2, 6        (cycle of 4)
9: 9, 1              (cycle of 2)
0, 1, 5, 6: always themselves

Method: divide the power by 4, take the remainder r.
  r = 1, 2, 3  ->  the 1st, 2nd, 3rd digit in the cycle
  r = 0        ->  the 4th (last) digit in the cycle
```

## Other rules you need

```text [Factors, zeros, remainders, sums]
Number of factors:  write N = a^p x b^q x c^r (prime factors)
                    factors = (p+1)(q+1)(r+1)

Trailing zeros in n!:  n/5 + n/25 + n/125 + ...   (whole parts only)

Remainder of a product:  multiply the remainders, then take the remainder again
Remainder of a sum:      add the remainders, then take the remainder again

1 + 2 + ... + n          = n(n+1)/2
1 + 3 + 5 + ... (n terms) = n^2
1^2 + 2^2 + ... + n^2    = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6
```

## A worked example

::steps
### Is 7,26,352 divisible by 11?
Alternating sum from the right: 2 - 5 + 3 - 6 + 2 - 7 = -11. That is a
multiple of 11, so **yes**. Ten seconds. Long division would take a minute and
invites a slip.

### What is the unit digit of 7 to the power 95?
The cycle of 7 is 7, 9, 3, 1. 95 divided by 4 leaves 3. The third digit in the
cycle is **3**.

### How many zeros are at the end of 100 factorial?
100/5 = 20, 100/25 = 4, 100/125 is less than 1 so stop. 20 + 4 = **24**.
Every zero needs a 2 and a 5, there are far more 2s than 5s, so you only count
the 5s.

### How many factors does 360 have?
360 = 2^3 x 3^2 x 5^1. Factors = (3+1)(2+1)(1+1) = 4 x 3 x 2 = **24**.
::

## Common mistakes

- **Counting 1 as prime.** It is not.
- **Using 2 and 6 to test for 12.** The two parts must be co-prime: 3 and 4.
- **Unit digits with remainder 0.** When the power divides exactly by 4, you
  take the **last** digit of the cycle, not the first. 2 to the power 8 ends
  in 6, not 2.
- **Trailing zeros: stopping at n/5.** 100! has 24 zeros, not 20. The 25, 50,
  75 and 100 each carry a second 5.
- **Dividing a huge number when the question only wants the remainder.** Work
  with remainders all the way through.

## How to practise

- **Ten questions a day** for the first three days, then five a day mixed into
  other topics.
- **Target: 30 seconds** a question. Anything over a minute means you do not
  have the rule yet; go back to the table.
- **Where:** IndiaBix "Numbers" and "Problems on Numbers", PrepInsta's number
  system section, and the "Number System" chapter of R.S. Aggarwal's
  *Quantitative Aptitude*.
- Write the unit-digit cycles and the divisibility table from memory once a
  day until you can do it in a minute.

## Practice questions

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. What is the unit digit of 3 to the power 65?"}
  **3.** Cycle of 3 is 3, 9, 7, 1. 65 divided by 4 leaves 1, so the first
  digit in the cycle.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. What is the smallest digit that can replace * in 5*2 so the number is divisible by 3?"}
  **2.** Digit sum is 7 + *. The smallest value making it a multiple of 3 is
  * = 2 (sum 9).
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. How many zeros are at the end of 50 factorial?"}
  **12.** 50/5 = 10, 50/25 = 2, total 12.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="4. How many factors does 72 have?"}
  **12.** 72 = 2^3 x 3^2, so (3+1)(2+1) = 12.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="5. What is the remainder when 17 x 23 is divided by 5?"}
  **1.** Remainders are 2 and 3; 2 x 3 = 6; 6 divided by 5 leaves 1.
  (Check: 17 x 23 = 391, and 391 = 5 x 78 + 1.)
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="6. What is the sum of the first 20 natural numbers?"}
  **210.** 20 x 21 / 2 = 210.
  :::
::
