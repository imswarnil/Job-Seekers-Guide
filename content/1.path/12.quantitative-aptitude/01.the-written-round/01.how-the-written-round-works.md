The last track filled the gaps between your subjects: SDLC, testing, Git,
Linux, and the number systems some papers still test. Every one of those is an
interview answer, and you only reach the interview if you clear the paper
before it. This page answers one question: what exactly is that paper, and how
do you sit it?

## Why it matters

At a walk-in in Bangalore, the written round is the first thing that happens
after you hand in your résumé. At the 34th walk-in I went to, there were
somewhere between 700 and 1,000 of us in the building. Only a small share of
us reached a technical round. Almost everybody else was removed by a multiple-choice test
before a single person had looked at what they could do.

::note
The written round is a cheap filter, not a fair one. A company with 40 seats and
1,000 candidates needs to remove 900 people quickly, and a sixty-minute test
does that for the price of a hall and a question paper. Understanding that is
what stops it feeling personal.
::

In college I sat every written round that came to LNCT and cleared none of
them. Part of that was not knowing the topics. The part I did not see until
later was time: I would sit on one hard question for several minutes, refuse
to leave it, and run out of clock before reaching easy questions at the end of
the section.

Knowing the topics is half of this round. How you spend the minutes is the
other half, and this page is about that half.

## What the paper looks like

Most freshers' aptitude tests in India share one shape. The names change, the
shape does not.

| Part | What it tests | Where you learn it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quantitative aptitude | Arithmetic, numbers, time, counting, geometry, data interpretation | This track |
| Logical reasoning | Series, codes, relations, arrangements, syllogisms, visual reasoning | [Logical reasoning](/logical-reasoning) |
| Verbal ability | Grammar, vocabulary, reading comprehension, para jumbles | [Verbal ability](/verbal-ability) |
| Sometimes: coding | One or two programs, usually easy to medium | The Java and DSA tracks |
| Sometimes: email or essay | A short written piece, marked on format and grammar | [Verbal ability](/verbal-ability) |

Almost always: multiple choice with four or five options, on a computer, with a
separate timer for each section, and one sheet of rough paper.

## Section weightage by company type

These are rough figures from the papers I sat and the ones I have seen since.
Test patterns change almost every year, so treat this table as a shape and
check the current pattern for the specific company before you sit it.

| Kind of test | Examples | Quant | Reasoning | Verbal | Other |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Large service company, national test | TCS NQT | 20–26 questions | 20–30 | 20–25 | Coding in the advanced part |
| Large service company, own test | Infosys, Wipro | 10–20 | 15–25 | 20–25 | Puzzles or pseudocode, sometimes an essay |
| Service company via a test vendor | Accenture, Cognizant, Capgemini | 15–20 | 15–20 | 15–25 | Email writing, pseudocode, coding |
| Employability test | AMCAT, eLitmus, CoCubes | 15–25 | 15–25 | 15–25 | eLitmus is harder and has heavy negative marking |
| Startup walk-in | A printed paper in a room | 15–25 | 10–20 | 5–15 | Often a Java or SQL section |

::tip
Across all of them, quant and reasoning together are about two thirds of the
non-coding marks. That is why these two tracks come first and verbal comes
third, not because English matters less.
::

## The sixty-second rule

Most sections give you somewhere between 45 and 75 seconds a question. Take
sixty as the working number, and train to it from the first day.

::steps
### Read the question once, slowly
Circle the numbers and the unit the answer wants. Most wrong answers are right
methods applied to the wrong quantity.

### Decide in fifteen seconds
Not solve. Decide. Do you recognise the shape, and can you see the route? If
yes, go. If no, mark it and move on.

### Solve inside sixty seconds
If you are past a minute and not close, stop. A question is worth one mark
whether it took eight seconds or eight minutes.

### Check the options before calculating exactly
Often three options are impossible and the fourth is your answer. Approximate
first.
::

## Three passes through a section

Stop doing a section in order. Do it three times.

| Pass | What you attempt | Time share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One | Everything you can finish in under sixty seconds | About 45% of the time |
| Two | Questions that need real working: long sets, second-difference series, four-step calculations | About 35% |
| Three | What is left, plus filling blanks if there is no negative marking | About 20% |

On pass one you will get through 60–70% of the paper, and every mark there is
cheap. Pass two costs two or three minutes a question for exactly the same
marks, which is why it comes second.

::warning
The hard part is not technical. Skipping a question you believe you could solve
feels like giving up. That feeling is the single most expensive thing in the
room.
::

## Negative marking, worked out

Some tests deduct marks for a wrong answer. Freshers respond with fear, and the
fear costs more than the deduction. Do the arithmetic once.

```text [Four options, +1 right, -0.25 wrong]
Blind guess, 1 in 4 chance:
  0.25 x 1  +  0.75 x (-0.25)  =  0.25 - 0.1875  =  +0.0625

One option eliminated, 1 in 3:
  (1/3) x 1  +  (2/3) x (-0.25)  =  0.333 - 0.167  =  +0.167

Two eliminated, 1 in 2:
  0.5 x 1  +  0.5 x (-0.25)  =  0.5 - 0.125  =  +0.375
```

With a quarter-mark penalty and four options, even a blind guess has a slightly
positive expected value, and eliminating one option makes guessing clearly
worth it. With a heavier penalty (eLitmus-style tests have used much heavier
ones), blind guessing turns negative and you should only answer when you can
eliminate at least two options.

::tip
In the first thirty seconds of the test, read the instructions for the marking
scheme and write it at the top of your rough sheet. "+1 / -0.25" or "no
negative". It decides whether you fill blanks at the end.
::

## What to skip on sight

| Skip on pass one | Why |
| --- | --- |
| A long puzzle or arrangement set with six or more clues | Huge value if it resolves, total loss if it does not. Always pass two. |
| Anything needing four or more calculation steps | Same mark, four times the cost. |
| A shape you do not recognise after fifteen seconds | More seconds will not produce a method. |
| A dense paragraph hiding one equation | Often easy once decoded, but the decoding time is unpredictable. |

And what to attempt first in quant, because they are fast and reliable once
practised: percentages, profit and loss, simple interest, ratio, averages,
ages, simplification and number series. Leave probability, mensuration with
several shapes, and caselet data interpretation for pass two.

## Common mistakes

- **Doing the paper in order.** The paper is not ordered by difficulty. The
  easy questions are scattered, and you have to go and find them.
- **Solving in your head to look fast.** Almost every wrong answer I made in
  practice was a correct method with a slipped digit. Write the working.
- **Ignoring sectional cutoffs.** Some tests need a minimum in every section.
  Skipping a whole section can fail you however well you did elsewhere.
- **Misaligned answers.** On paper tests with an OMR sheet, check that your
  answer to question 15 is on line 15 if you skipped 14.
- **Changing confident answers in the last five minutes.** Second-guessing
  under time pressure changes more right answers to wrong than the reverse.

## How to practise

- **Every topic page in this track** ends with a few questions. Time every one
  of them from the first day, even if you are slow. The number you are trying
  to move is seconds per question, not questions attempted.
- **One full mock a week** from week three, and two a week in the last week.
  PrepInsta and IndiaBix have company-wise previous papers; the company's own
  sample tests are the most accurate.
- **After every mock**, write down the three questions that took longest and
  why. That list is your next week's study plan.

::note
You do not need a thousand questions. You need to recognise about forty
question shapes and have a method for each. The pages that follow are those
shapes.
::

## Practice questions

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. A test has +1 for right and -1/3 for wrong, with four options. Is a blind guess worth it?"}
  Expected value = 0.25 x 1 + 0.75 x (-1/3) = 0.25 - 0.25 = 0. A blind guess
  gains nothing on average. Guess only when you can eliminate at least one
  option: then it is (1/3) - (2/3) x (1/3) = 1/3 - 2/9 = +1/9.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. A section has 25 questions in 30 minutes. What is your time per question?"}
  30 x 60 = 1,800 seconds. 1,800 / 25 = 72 seconds a question.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. You are 90 seconds into a question and not close. What do you do?"}
  Mark it and move on. Come back on pass two if there is time. The next easy
  question is worth exactly the same mark.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="4. Candidate A attempts 38 and gets 31 right. Candidate B attempts 52 and gets 41 right. With -0.25 for wrong, who scores more?"}
  A: 31 - 7 x 0.25 = 31 - 1.75 = 29.25. B: 41 - 11 x 0.25 = 41 - 2.75 =
  38.25. B has lower accuracy (about 79% against about 82%) and scores 9 more
  marks.
  :::
::
