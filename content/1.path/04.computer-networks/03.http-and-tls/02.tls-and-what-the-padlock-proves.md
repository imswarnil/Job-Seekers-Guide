Plain readable text, including the token. Anybody on the same wifi, any router
along the path, and your internet provider could all read it. TLS is the layer
that fixes that, and it is worth being precise about what it fixes.

## Why this matters in a job

Because "just add HTTPS" is where most people's understanding stops, and the
three certificate errors you will actually hit each have a different cause and a
different fix. It is also the layer that most often breaks in a deployment, and
it breaks with an error message nobody can read.

## Three guarantees, and two non-guarantees

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-eye-off" title="Confidentiality"}
  Nobody in between can read it. Your token, the applicant's marks, the fee
  amounts.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-badge-check" title="Identity"}
  You are talking to the machine that holds the private key for this name. Not
  "this site is honest" — "this site is who the name says".
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-shield-check" title="Integrity"}
  Nobody changed anything in transit. A modified byte is detected and the
  connection fails rather than delivering altered data.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="NOT: that the site is trustworthy"}
  A phishing site can have a perfectly valid certificate for its own name. The
  padlock says "encrypted to whoever this is", not "this is safe".
  :::
::

::pull-quote
The padlock proves the conversation is private and that the name matches. It
proves nothing at all about whether the person you are talking to deserves your
data.
::

It also does not hide **which** site you visited: the name is visible during the
handshake, and DNS asked for it in the open beforehand. Your provider knows you
went to `university.edu`, not what you read there.

## The handshake, in plain words

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="TLS 1.3 does this in one round trip on top of the TCP one. TLS 1.2 took two."}
  :::flow-step{label="Hello, and what you support" icon="i-lucide-hand"}
  The client lists the ciphers it can use and, crucially, the hostname it wants.
  That last part is how one IP can serve certificates for many sites.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The certificate" icon="i-lucide-file-badge"}
  The server sends its certificate: a public key, the names it is valid for, an
  expiry date, and a signature from a certificate authority.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The client checks it" icon="i-lucide-search-check" highlight}
  Three checks, and all three must pass. Is the name in the certificate the name
  I asked for? Has it expired? Is it signed by an authority I trust, following
  the chain up to a root my operating system ships with?
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Agree a shared key" icon="i-lucide-key"}
  Both sides derive a symmetric key that no observer can compute, even one who
  recorded the whole exchange. From here the conversation is fast symmetric
  encryption; the expensive asymmetric part happens once.
  :::
::

## Look at a real certificate

```bash [Terminal]
openssl s_client -connect github.com:443 -servername github.com < /dev/null 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -subject -issuer -dates
```

```text [Output]
subject=CN=github.com
issuer=C=US, O=DigiCert Inc, CN=DigiCert TLS Hybrid ECC SHA384 2020 CA1
notBefore=Feb 5 00:00:00 2026 GMT
notAfter=Mar 6 23:59:59 2027 GMT
```

Four facts: who it is for, who vouches for it, and the window it is valid in.
Everything TLS decides comes from those.

## The three errors, and what each one means

::compare{caption="All three say 'certificate problem'. They are entirely different problems."}
  :::compare-side{label="Expired" verdict="wrong"}
  `certificate has expired`. The most common production TLS incident there is,
  and it is a calendar failure rather than a technical one. The fix is renewal
  and, more importantly, automated renewal plus an alert at thirty days.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Name mismatch" verdict="wrong"}
  `Hostname mismatch`. You asked for `api.university.edu` and the certificate is
  for `university.edu`. Certificates are per name, and a wildcard covers one
  level only: `*.university.edu` covers `api.` and does not cover
  `api.admissions.university.edu`.
  :::
::

::compare{caption="And the third, which is the one that confuses people most."}
  :::compare-side{label="Unable to verify / PKIX path building failed" verdict="wrong"}
  The chain could not be followed to a trusted root. Either it is self-signed,
  or — far more often — the server is not sending its **intermediate**
  certificate. A browser may hide this by fetching the missing link; Java will
  not, which is why a site that works in Chrome fails from your application.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="How to tell in ten seconds" verdict="right"}
  ```bash
  openssl s_client -connect host:443 -showcerts < /dev/null | grep "^ [0-9] s:"
  ```
  If only one certificate comes back and it is not self-signed, the intermediate
  is missing. That is a server configuration fix, not a client one.
  :::
::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Never "fix" a certificate error by disabling verification. `curl -k`,
`verify=False`, or a Java `TrustManager` that accepts everything all turn HTTPS
back into HTTP with a padlock drawn on it. It is a common suggestion in forum
answers, it removes the entire identity guarantee, and it has shipped to
production more times than anyone would like.
::

::real-life{title="What actually happens when a certificate expires" source="A Sunday morning"}
It does not degrade. At one second past midnight, every client stops connecting
at once: browsers show a full-page warning, mobile apps fail, and internal
services throw handshake exceptions. There is no partial failure, no warning
period and no gradual onset. This is why certificate expiry is monitored like
disk space rather than remembered like a birthday.
::

## What you can do now

You can say exactly what HTTPS guarantees and what it does not, read a
certificate from the command line, and tell the three failure modes apart.

## Your turn

```bash [Terminal]
# 1. A site with a deliberately expired certificate.
curl -I https://expired.badssl.com/
# 2. A deliberate name mismatch.
curl -I https://wrong.host.badssl.com/
# 3. A deliberately incomplete chain.
curl -I https://incomplete-chain.badssl.com/
# 4. How many days until a real one expires?
echo | openssl s_client -connect github.com:443 2>/dev/null \
  | openssl x509 -noout -enddate
```

1. Which of the three checks failed in each of the first three?
2. Why does the third one behave differently in a browser than in `curl` or Java?
3. Write the one-line command you would put in a monitoring job to alert thirty
   days before expiry.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answers" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  1. Expiry, then name, then chain-to-a-trusted-root. Three checks, three
     failures, and every real TLS error is one of them.
  2. Browsers ship a large cache of intermediates and will fetch a missing one
     from the URL in the certificate. Java and `curl` do neither by default, so
     an incomplete chain is invisible in Chrome and fatal from your application.
     This is the single most confusing TLS situation for a junior engineer, and
     "works in the browser, fails in the app" is its signature.
  3. ```bash
     echo | openssl s_client -connect university.edu:443 2>/dev/null \
       | openssl x509 -noout -checkend 2592000 \
       || echo "ALERT: certificate expires within 30 days"
     ```
     `-checkend` takes seconds and exits non-zero if the certificate expires
     within that window. Thirty days is 2,592,000 seconds.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Name the three checks a client makes on a certificate.
2. **An interviewer asks: "does the padlock mean the site is safe?"**

## The interview question this answers

**"How does HTTPS work?"**

Give the three guarantees — confidentiality, identity, integrity — then the
handshake in one sentence: the server proves it holds the key for this name by
presenting a certificate signed by an authority the client already trusts, and
both sides then derive a shared symmetric key. Then the part that distinguishes
you: say what it does *not* prove. A phishing site can hold a valid certificate
for its own name, and the padlock has never meant the site is honest.
