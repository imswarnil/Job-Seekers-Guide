The dean has numbers and cannot read them. A chart is not decoration on a report,
it is a way of answering one question faster than a table can, and choosing the
wrong shape answers a different question confidently.

## Why this matters in a job

Because you will be asked for "a chart of the applications", which is not a
question, and the useful contribution is working out what the reader actually
wants to know.

## The question decides the shape

| What the reader wants to know | The shape |
| --- | --- |
| How do these compare? | **Bar chart**, sorted by value |
| How has this changed over time? | **Line chart**, time on the horizontal |
| How is this distributed? | **Histogram** |
| Is there a relationship between two things? | **Scatter plot** |
| What is one important number? | **The number**, large. Not a chart at all. |

::pull-quote
"Applications per programme" is a comparison, so it is a sorted bar chart.
"Applications per week" is a change over time, so it is a line. The data is the
same; the question is not.
::

## The four that are usually wrong

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-chart-pie" title="Pie charts"}
  Humans compare angles badly. With more than about four slices nobody can rank
  them, and two similar slices are indistinguishable. A sorted bar chart answers
  the same question and can be read instantly. The only defensible pie is two or
  three parts of an obvious whole.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-box" title="3D anything"}
  Perspective makes the front bars look larger than the back ones. The chart is
  now lying about its own data for decoration.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-chart-area" title="Stacked bars, for comparing"}
  Only the bottom segment starts at zero, so only it can be compared across
  bars. The segments above float and cannot be. Fine for showing a total,
  misleading for comparing parts.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-gauge" title="Dual axes"}
  Two different scales on one chart, so the crossing point where the lines meet
  is an artefact of the scales you chose. It implies a relationship you invented.
  :::
::

## The axis decision that lies

::compare{caption="The same four numbers: 412, 428, 441, 460 applications."}
  :::compare-side{label="Axis starting at 400" verdict="wrong"}
  The last bar is about four times the height of the first. It looks like
  explosive growth. The numbers are real and the picture is false, and this is
  the most common way a chart misleads in business software.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Axis starting at zero" verdict="right"}
  Four bars of almost identical height, with a slight rise. Which is the truth:
  applications grew about 12 per cent over four years.
  :::
::

**A bar chart must start at zero**, because the bar's *length* is the encoding
and a truncated bar encodes a false length. A **line** chart may be truncated,
because the encoding is the slope rather than the height, and a line chart of
temperature starting at zero is unreadable. Say which you are doing and why.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Charting libraries choose the axis automatically, and they choose the truncated
one because it looks better. Setting the baseline deliberately is a decision you
have to make on every bar chart you ever produce, and nobody will tell you it was
made wrongly.
::

## Colour carries meaning or it carries nothing

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-palette" title="One colour, unless a second means something"}
  Six programmes in six colours forces the reader to learn a legend. Six bars in
  one colour, sorted, need no legend at all.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-eye" title="Colour must not be the only signal"}
  About one man in twelve cannot distinguish red from green. A chart whose
  entire meaning is red-against-green is unreadable for them. Add a label, a
  shape or a position.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-arrow-down-up" title="Sort by value, not alphabetically"}
  The reader's question is "which is largest". Sorting answers it before they
  have read a single label.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-type" title="Label directly where you can"}
  A number at the end of each bar removes the need to look at an axis and back.
  A legend is a lookup table you are asking the reader to memorise.
  :::
::

## What the chart must say about itself

This is where the SQL track arrives. Every chart of the university's data is a
query, and every query excluded something.

::compare{caption="The same chart, two captions."}
  :::compare-side{label="What gets shipped" verdict="wrong"}
  **Average score by programme**

  A reader assumes this covers every application.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What is true" verdict="right"}
  **Average score by programme**
  From 1,840 marked applications out of 2,040. Excludes 200 unmarked and 6
  withdrawn. Counting applications, not applicants.

  Same chart. Now it is a claim somebody can check.
  :::
::

::pull-quote
Every chart is a query, and every query excluded something. The caption is where
you say what.
::

## What you can do now

You can choose a chart shape from the question rather than from habit, defend the
axis baseline, and write the caption that turns a picture into a claim.

## Your turn

For each request, say what the reader actually wants to know, the shape you
would use, and one thing the caption must disclose.

1. "Show me how admissions are going."
2. "Compare the programmes."
3. "Are higher-scoring applicants more likely to accept their offer?"
4. "How many applicants do we have?"
5. "Show the breakdown of applications by status."

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answers" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **1.** Ambiguous, and the right first move is to ask. "Going" against what —
  last year, or the target? Almost certainly a **line** of applications per week
  with last year's line behind it, because "how is it going" means "compared
  with what we expected". Disclose: which intake, and whether withdrawals are
  included.

  **2.** A comparison, so a **sorted bar chart**, one colour, labelled at the end
  of each bar, baseline at zero. Disclose whether the count is applications or
  applicants, because they differ by 25 per cent.

  **3.** A relationship, so a **scatter plot**: score on one axis, accepted or
  not on the other. With a binary outcome, a better answer is a bar chart of
  acceptance rate by score band. Disclose the band widths, because choosing them
  changes the picture.

  **4.** **Not a chart.** One large number. And a decision first: applicants or
  applications? Put both, labelled, because the moment you show one somebody
  will quote it.

  **5.** A **sorted bar chart**, and resist the pie. Six statuses is past the
  point where angles can be compared. Disclose the date, because status is a
  snapshot and it changes daily.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why must a bar chart start at zero when a line chart need not?
2. **An interviewer asks: "why not use a pie chart?"**

## The interview question this answers

**"How would you visualise this data?"**

Start with the question rather than the chart: what does the reader want to know,
and what will they do differently depending on the answer. Then the shape, then
the two decisions that are usually made by accident — the axis baseline, and
whether you are counting applications or applicants.

Closing with the caption is the part that distinguishes the answer: every chart
is a query, every query excluded something, and saying what is the difference
between a picture and a claim somebody can check.
