The written round is behind you, or at least you know how to clear it now.
What is on the other side is a person across a table, and I was far less ready
for that than I was for the paper.

In my first year in Bangalore I went to 33 walk-ins and was not selected at any
of them.
At the 34th, out of somewhere between 700 and 1000 candidates, I was. The
difference between the 33rd and the 34th was not that I had learned much more
Java in that week. It was that I had finally worked out what the people across
the table were listening for.

## Why it matters

The written round removes most candidates, and it is the one people prepare
for. The interview removes most of the rest, and almost nobody prepares for it,
because it feels like something you either are good at or are not.

It is not. It is a small number of questions that come up again and again, and
answers you can write down, say out loud, and improve.

::real-life{title="What changed between walk-in 33 and walk-in 34" source="Bangalore, 2018–2019"}
For months my "tell me about yourself" was my name, my town, my college
percentage and my hobbies. I answered technical questions with one line and
then waited. I thought HR was a formality. By the end I had a 75-second
introduction that pointed at what I had actually studied, a habit of answering
a technical question in the same five steps every time, and honest answers
ready for the HR questions I used to dread. None of that is talent.
::

## How long it takes

About three to four weeks alongside revision of the technical subjects, at an
hour or two a day. The reading is quick. The practising, out loud and recorded,
is the part that takes the time, and it is the part that changes results.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-arrow-right"}
Start where every interview really starts: a single page that somebody reads for
about ten seconds before deciding whether to call you at all.
::
