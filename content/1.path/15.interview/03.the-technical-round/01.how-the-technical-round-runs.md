This page answers what happens between "tell me about yourself" and "do you have
any questions for us?", so that none of it surprises you.

## Why it matters

A fresher technical round is usually 30 to 60 minutes with one interviewer,
sometimes two. At service companies it is often one round. At product companies
and startups there may be two or three, and one of them is only coding. In every
version I sat, it followed roughly the same three parts.

## The three parts

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Not always in this order, and not always all three. But almost always these three."}
  :::flow-step{label="Resume-driven: 10 to 20 minutes" icon="i-lucide-file-text" highlight}
  The interviewer picks something from your resume or your introduction and
  asks about it. "You mentioned JDBC. How does your project connect to the
  database?" This is where your introduction pays off, because you chose what
  they would pick.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Core subjects: 15 to 30 minutes" icon="i-lucide-book-open"}
  Java and OOPs, then DBMS and SQL, then OS and networks, roughly in that order
  of how often they come up for a Java fresher. Definitions first, then "how
  does it work?", then "give me an example".
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Coding: 10 to 30 minutes" icon="i-lucide-code"}
  A small problem on paper, a whiteboard, a shared editor or a laptop. Reverse a
  string, find duplicates, second largest number, a SQL query on two tables.
  They are watching how you think more than whether you finish.
  :::
::

## What the interviewer is marking

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-layers" title="Depth, not breadth"}
  They push on one answer until you either get more precise or get vague. Which
  of those happens is the result.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-message-square" title="Can you explain it"}
  A developer spends a lot of the day explaining things to other people. A right
  answer said badly scores lower than you would think.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-scale" title="Do you know the trade-off"}
  "When would you not use it?" separates somebody who memorised a definition
  from somebody who understood it.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-shield-check" title="Honesty"}
  They will ask something you do not know. What you say next is being marked.
  :::
::

## What to do

::steps
### Revise your resume before anything else
Every word on it is a possible first question. Read it the morning of the
interview and ask yourself one question per line.

### Revise the core subjects in the order they are asked
Java and OOPs, DBMS and SQL, then OS, then networks. The [fifty most-asked
questions](/interview/the-technical-round/the-fifty-questions) at the end of this
chapter are the checklist.

### Learn one answer format and use it for everything
Next page. It stops the one-line answer and the ten-minute answer, both of which
lose marks.

### Solve five small coding problems out loud the day before
Not to learn anything new. To warm up the habit of talking while you code.
::

## A worked example: one round

What a 40-minute fresher round at a mid-size service company looked like for
somebody like me in 2018. The questions are typical; the order is typical.

| Minute | Question | Where it came from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–2 | Tell me about yourself | Always |
| 2–10 | "Walk me through your student result project. How do you calculate the grade?" | The resume |
| 10–14 | "Why a CHECK constraint? What else could you have used?" | Pushing on the project |
| 14–20 | "What is the difference between an abstract class and an interface?" | Java and OOPs |
| 20–24 | "What is a HashMap? How does it work inside?" | Java collections |
| 24–30 | "What is normalisation? Normalise this table." | DBMS |
| 30–38 | "Write a query for the second-highest marks." | SQL, on paper |
| 38–40 | "Any questions for me?" | Always |

::note
Notice that 12 of the 40 minutes were about the project, and the next question
after that followed from its database side. That is the introduction and the
resume doing their job.
::

## Common mistakes

- **Preparing only the coding.** At most fresher rounds, the coding is ten
  minutes of forty. The subjects and the project are the rest.
- **Not revising your own resume.** Being unable to explain a line of your own
  resume is worse than not knowing a textbook answer.
- **One-line answers.** "HashMap stores key-value pairs." And then silence.
- **Arguing with the interviewer.** If they say an answer is not quite right, ask
  what they mean. You may learn something, and they are watching how you take
  correction.

## How to practise

Ask a friend to run the table above on you, with your own resume. Forty minutes,
recorded. Then read the next page and do it again.

## Next

::callout{icon="i-lucide-arrow-right"}
The single biggest difference between candidates in the subjects part is not
what they know. It is how they say it. A one-line answer sounds like a guess. A
ten-minute answer loses the interviewer. There is a shape in between, and it
works for every technical question you will ever be asked.
::
