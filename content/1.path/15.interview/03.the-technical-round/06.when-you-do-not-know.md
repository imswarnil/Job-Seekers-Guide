Say that you do not know, then say what you do know that is close to it, then
say how you would find out. That is a complete answer, and it is the only one
the next question cannot catch out.

## Why it matters

Every interviewer asks until you run out. It is how they find the edge of what
you know, and there is always an edge, even for the interviewer. What is being
marked is not whether you hit it but what you do when you get there.

A guess almost never works. The interviewer asked because they know the answer,
and a wrong guess makes them doubt the answers you gave correctly before it.

## What to do

There are three situations, and they need different answers.

::tabs
  :::tabs-item{label="You half know it" icon="i-lucide-brain"}
  Think aloud towards it. Say what you do know, reason from there, and say where
  you are unsure.

  "I know garbage collection in Java is based on reachability: an object nothing
  can reach from a live reference can be collected. I think the heap is split so
  that new objects are collected more often, because most objects die young. I'm
  not sure of the names of the collectors beyond that."

  That is more useful to an interviewer than a confident one-liner, because they
  can see how you reason.
  :::
  :::tabs-item{label="You know the neighbour" icon="i-lucide-git-branch"}
  Say you do not know this one, and offer the closest thing you do.

  "I haven't used Kafka. I understand the idea of a message queue: one part of a
  system puts a message in, another takes it out later, so they don't have to be
  running at the same time. I'd expect Kafka to be a version of that."
  :::
  :::tabs-item{label="You have no idea" icon="i-lucide-circle-help"}
  Say so in one sentence, and say how you would find out.

  "I don't know that one. I'd look it up in the official documentation. If you
  tell me the answer I'll remember it."

  Then stop, and let them move on. They will.
  :::
::

## A worked example

::compare{caption="Three answers to 'what is the difference between a process and a thread?' from somebody who half knows it."}
  :::compare-side{label="The bluff" verdict="wrong"}
  "A process is a big program and a thread is a small program. Threads are
  faster because they are small, basically, and processes are used for bigger
  things."

  Vague, partly wrong, and the next question ("so what do threads share?") finds
  the edge. The interviewer now doubts everything said before it.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Thinking aloud" verdict="right"}
  "A process is a running program with its own memory. A thread is a path of
  execution inside a process. I'm fairly sure threads in the same process share
  the heap and have their own stack, which is why they're cheaper to create and
  why two threads can corrupt shared data. I'm less sure how the OS schedules
  them differently; I'd want to read that before I said more."
  :::
::

The second answer is not complete. It still scores far better, because every
claim in it is true and the edge is stated honestly.

## Phrases to have ready

Under pressure, these are hard to invent. Learn them.

```text [phrases.txt]
"Let me think out loud for a moment."

"I don't know that one. What I do know is ___, which is close to it."

"I'm not sure. My guess would be ___, because ___, but I'd check before
relying on it."

"I don't remember the exact syntax. The idea is ___. Can I write it like
this and fix the syntax later?"

"I haven't used ___ yet. I've used ___, which solves a similar problem."

"Could you give me a hint on where to start?"
```

::tip
Asking for a hint is allowed and normal. Interviewers often have one ready.
Taking a hint well, and using it, is itself being marked.
::

## Common mistakes

- **Bluffing.** It is detected more often than you think, and the cost spreads
  to your correct answers.
- **Freezing in silence.** Ten seconds of silence feels like a minute to both of
  you. Say "let me think" out loud and then think.
- **Apologising repeatedly.** One "I don't know" is honest. Three "sorry, I'm
  not sure, sorry" is a candidate losing confidence, and it shows.
- **Giving up on the whole interview after one miss.** One unknown question
  rarely decides a round. Your composure on the next three does.

## How to practise

Ask a friend to ask you questions from a subject you have not finished, on
purpose. Practise the three kinds of answer. The first few will feel like
admitting failure. After a week they feel like what they are: a normal part of
talking to people who know more than you.
