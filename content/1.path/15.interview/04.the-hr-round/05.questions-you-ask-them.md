Always ask two or three questions. Never say "no, you covered everything". The
questions show you were listening, and the answers tell you things about the job
you cannot find anywhere else.

## Why it matters

Two reasons. It is the last thing the interviewer hears from you, so it colours
their memory of the whole conversation. And it is the only point in the process
where you are interviewing them: whether you will be trained, who you will learn
from, what the first months are really like.

## What to do

::steps
### Write three questions down before the interview
You will forget them otherwise, especially after a hard round.

### Ask one about the work, one about learning, one about the next step
That mix works for almost any interviewer.

### Listen to the answer
A follow-up on their answer is better than your next prepared question.
::

## Good questions to ask

::tabs
  :::tabs-item{label="Technical interviewer" icon="i-lucide-terminal"}
  - "What would somebody in this role work on in the first three months?"
  - "What does the team use day to day: which languages, tools, and how code is
    reviewed?"
  - "What surprises new people most about the codebase?"
  - "What do freshers who do well here have in common?"

  The third one gets an honest answer more often than any other question I have
  asked, and it tells you a lot about what you would be walking into.
  :::
  :::tabs-item{label="HR" icon="i-lucide-users"}
  - "How long is the training, and how are people assigned to projects or
    technologies after it?"
  - "What are the next steps in the process, and when should I expect to hear?"
  - "Is the location decided before joining or after training?"
  - "Could you share the component-wise breakup of the salary?"
  :::
  :::tabs-item{label="Manager or founder" icon="i-lucide-briefcase"}
  - "What would make you say, six months from now, that hiring me was the right
    decision?"
  - "Who would I work with most closely, and who would review my code?"
  - "What is the team's biggest challenge this year?"
  :::
::

## A worked example

::compare{caption="The same moment, at the end of an HR round."}
  :::compare-side{label="Wasted" verdict="wrong"}
  **HR:** Any questions for us?
  **Candidate:** No, I think you covered everything. Thank you.

  It reads as not caring, and it throws away the one moment where you control
  the conversation.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Used" verdict="right"}
  **HR:** Any questions for us?
  **Candidate:** Two. How are freshers assigned to a technology after training?
  And what are the next steps, and when should I expect to hear?

  Short, useful, and the second answer means you know when to follow up.
  :::
::

::note
When I joined Accenture I was assigned Salesforce, when I had wanted web
development. Had I asked how assignments worked, I would at least have known it
was coming. At a company that assigns streams after training, you might get
what you want if you ask and push for it, but you have to know that is how it
works first.
::

## Questions not to ask yet

- **"What is the salary?" in a technical round.** Ask HR, not the engineer.
- **"How many leaves do I get?" or "can I work from home?" as your first
  question.** Fine to know, better asked once the offer is on the table.
- **"Did I pass?"** Put them on the spot, and they will not tell you anyway. Ask
  about next steps instead.
- **Anything answered on the first page of their website.** It shows you did
  not look.

## How to practise

Before every interview, write three questions on the same paper as your "tell me
about yourself" notes. After the interview, write down the answers. They are
useful when you are comparing offers.
