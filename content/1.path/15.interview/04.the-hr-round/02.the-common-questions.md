Six questions come up in almost every HR round. None of them has a right answer,
and every one of them has a common answer that quietly loses. The rule for all
six: say something true and specific, and point it at the job.

## Why it matters

These are the most predictable questions in the whole process. You know they are
coming. That makes an unprepared answer look worse, not better, because the
interviewer knows you could have prepared.

## The rule for all of them

::compare{caption="The same claim, twice."}
  :::compare-side{label="Adjectives" verdict="wrong"}
  "I am hardworking, a quick learner and a team player."

  Every candidate says this. It is not disbelieved so much as not heard.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Evidence" verdict="right"}
  "When I moved to Bangalore I trained full-time for three months and studied
  aptitude and English every evening on my own, because I had never cleared a
  written round in college."

  Nobody else in the queue has this sentence, because it happened to you.
  :::
::

## The questions

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. What are your strengths?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  One or two strengths, each with a fact that proves it. Pick ones the job needs.

  ```text [template]
  "One of my strengths is [strength]. For example, [a specific time you
  showed it, with a number]. I think that matters in this role because
  [link to the job]."
  ```

  *"I stick with a problem until I understand it. When I started SQL I could
  not get joins to work for about a week, so I drew every table on paper and
  worked through each join by hand until I could predict the result before
  running it. In a developer role, that's most of debugging."*

  **Loses:** a list of five adjectives with nothing behind them.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. What is your weakness?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  A real weakness, what it has cost you, and what you do about it now. Not a
  disguised strength.

  ```text [template]
  "A weakness of mine is [real weakness]. It [a consequence, briefly].
  What I do about it now is [a specific habit or fix]."
  ```

  *"I sit on problems too long before asking for help. At the institute I once
  lost most of a day on a JDBC error a trainer fixed in five minutes. Now I give
  myself forty-five minutes: if I've made no progress, I write down what I've
  tried and ask."*

  **Loses:** "I'm a perfectionist" or "I work too hard". Interviewers hear these
  hundreds of times a year and both read as evasion.

  **Also loses:** a weakness that disqualifies you for the job. "I'm not good
  with computers" is honest and ends the interview.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. Why should we hire you?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Three things they need, matched to three things you can show. It is a summary,
  not a plea.

  ```text [template]
  "You need someone who [need 1 from the job description], [need 2] and
  [need 3]. I [evidence for 1], I [evidence for 2], and [evidence for 3].
  And I can join [immediately / on date]."
  ```

  *"You need a Java developer who knows SQL and can learn a codebase quickly.
  I've trained full-time in Core Java and SQL for three months, I built a
  result system in Java and MySQL that I can walk you through, and I've learned
  everything I know in the last year, from zero, so learning fast is the one
  thing I've actually proved. And I can join immediately."*

  **Loses:** "Because I need this job", or "because I'm hardworking". Both are
  about you, and the question is about them.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="4. Why do you want to join this company?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  This question tests whether you read anything about them. Spend fifteen
  minutes on their website and one recent news item before the interview.

  ```text [template]
  "I looked at [something specific: their products / clients / domain /
  training programme]. [Why it matters to you]. And this role is [what you
  want to do], which is [what you've been preparing for]."
  ```

  *"You train freshers for three months before putting them on projects, and
  you work across banking and retail clients, so I'd see more than one domain in
  my first years. And this role is Java development, which is what I've spent
  the last year preparing for."*

  **Loses:** "It's a great company with good growth opportunities." The most
  common answer, and it says you read nothing.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="5. Where do you see yourself in five years?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  The real question is "will you still be here, and will you be growing?" Give a
  direction that fits the company, not a job title somewhere else.

  ```text [template]
  "In five years I'd like to be [deeper skill / more responsibility] in
  [area], someone the team relies on for [thing]. The first step is
  [learning this role properly]."
  ```

  *"I'd like to be a strong backend developer, the person on the team who
  understands the database side properly, and ideally leading a small piece of
  a project. The first step is getting really good at the work in this role."*

  **Loses:** "I want to start my own company", "I want to do an MBA", "I'm
  preparing for government exams". All fine things to want, and all answer "will
  you stay?" with no.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="6. Why are you leaving your current company?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  For switchers. Move towards something; never run from something. No
  criticism, even when it is true.

  ```text [template]
  "I've learned a lot at [company], especially [thing]. I'm looking for
  [more of X / a role in Y / more ownership], which [this role] offers
  and my current project can't right now."
  ```

  When I left Accenture I said: *"Three years on one project taught me how a
  system runs from build to support. I want to be closer to the data and the
  business side, with more ownership than a large services project can give,
  and this role is that."* Salary was a real reason too. I did not lead with it.

  **Loses:** "My manager is bad", "there is no growth", "the salary is too low".
  Each may be true. Each makes the interviewer wonder what you will say about
  them.
  :::
::

## A worked example

The same candidate answering "What is your weakness?" twice.

::compare{caption="Honest beats safe."}
  :::compare-side{label="The safe answer" verdict="wrong"}
  "My weakness is that I am a perfectionist. I spend too much time making my
  work perfect."

  A strength wearing a disguise. The interviewer has heard it many times, marks
  it as evasive, and learns nothing.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="The honest answer" verdict="right"}
  "Public speaking. In college I avoided every presentation. Since moving to
  Bangalore I've practised by recording my answers and explaining topics to
  friends at the institute, and I volunteered to present our mini project. I'm
  better, not good yet."

  A real weakness, not disqualifying for a developer, with a fix already under
  way. It is believable because it is not flattering.
  :::
::

## Common mistakes

- **Memorised answers from a website.** Interviewers have read the same website.
- **Answers about what you need** instead of what they need.
- **Lying about plans.** If you are preparing for a government exam and will
  leave if you clear it, do not apply for a job with a two-year bond.
- **Talking for three minutes.** Each answer is 30 to 60 seconds.

## How to practise

Write all six answers using the templates, in your own words. Then say each one
aloud and time it: 30 to 60 seconds. Ask a friend to follow each with "why?",
once. If the "why" breaks the answer, the answer was not true enough.
