A behavioural question asks about something that already happened: "tell me
about a time you failed", "a time you disagreed with somebody". The answer is a
short true story in four parts: **Situation, Task, Action, Result**. That is
STAR.

## Why it matters

Behavioural questions are asked because past behaviour is the best guess anybody
has at future behaviour. They are more common at product companies and startups,
and increasingly at service companies too, sometimes asked by the technical
interviewer at the end.

Without a structure, people either ramble (three minutes of background and no
ending) or freeze (one sentence and silence). STAR fixes both, because you
always know which part comes next and when you are finished.

## The structure

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="About 60 to 90 seconds. Most of it in the A."}
  :::flow-step{label="S: Situation (10 seconds)" icon="i-lucide-map-pin"}
  Where and when, in one or two sentences. "In my final year, four of us had a
  college project due in three weeks."
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="T: Task (10 seconds)" icon="i-lucide-target"}
  What you specifically had to do, or what the problem was. "I was responsible
  for the database and the login."
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="A: Action (40 seconds)" icon="i-lucide-hammer" highlight}
  What **you** did, step by step. "I", not "we". This is the part being marked,
  and the part most people rush.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="R: Result (15 seconds)" icon="i-lucide-flag"}
  What happened, with a number if there is one, and what you learned. One
  sentence of learning is enough; three sounds rehearsed.
  :::
::

```text [star-template.txt]
S  "In/At [place, time], [the situation in one sentence]."
T  "I was responsible for [your task / the problem to solve]."
A  "First I [action]. Then I [action]. When [complication], I [action]."
R  "As a result, [outcome with a number]. What I took from it is [one line]."
```

## A worked example: tell me about a time you failed

::compare{caption="Real failure, real consequence, real change."}
  :::compare-side{label="Without STAR" verdict="wrong"}
  "I don't think I've really failed. Maybe in college I got low marks once
  because I didn't study enough, but I learned from it and worked harder after
  that."

  "I've never really failed" is not believable, and the rest is vague. There is
  no situation, no action, and nothing the interviewer can follow up on.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="With STAR" verdict="right"}
  **S:** "When I first came to Bangalore in 2018, I went to walk-in after walk-in
  and kept failing the written round."

  **T:** "I needed to find out why, because I was studying Java every day and it
  wasn't helping."

  **A:** "I started writing down every question I could remember on the bus
  home. After a few weeks it was obvious: I was losing marks in aptitude and
  English, not Java. So I split my evenings: an hour of aptitude and half an hour
  of reading comprehension every day, timed, on top of the course."

  **R:** "I started clearing written rounds and reaching the interviews. I kept
  going to walk-ins, and I was selected at the 34th. What I took from it is
  that when I keep failing, I write down exactly where, instead of trying
  harder."
  :::
::

## Three more worked stories

::tabs
  :::tabs-item{label="A disagreement" icon="i-lucide-message-square-warning"}
  **Question:** "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate."

  **S:** "In our final-year project, four of us were building an attendance
  system."
  **T:** "A teammate wanted to store attendance as one text column per student,
  with all the dates in it. I thought it should be one row per student per day."
  **A:** "Instead of arguing, I made a small copy of both and wrote the query
  our guide actually wanted: which students are below 75% this month. With
  one row per day it was one query. With the text column it needed code to split
  the text. I showed him both side by side."
  **R:** "He agreed, and we used rows. The next week he found a bug in my login
  code, and I took it the same way. I learned that showing both versions ends an
  argument faster than defending mine."

  Note what it avoids: no blaming, no "he was wrong". A disagreement about a
  decision, resolved with evidence.
  :::
  :::tabs-item{label="Learning something fast" icon="i-lucide-zap"}
  **Question:** "Tell me about a time you had to learn something quickly."

  **S:** "At the institute, we had a mini project due in ten days, using JDBC,
  which hadn't been covered yet."
  **T:** "I had to connect our Java code to MySQL and save marks for each
  student."
  **A:** "I read the official JDBC tutorial for an evening, wrote a program that
  inserted one row, then one that read it back, and only then connected it to our
  project. When it failed with a driver error, I read the full stack trace
  instead of the first line and found the driver jar was missing from the
  classpath."
  **R:** "It worked in four days, and I explained it to three classmates who
  were stuck on the same error. I learned to get the smallest version working
  first."
  :::
  :::tabs-item{label="Pressure (for switchers)" icon="i-lucide-gauge"}
  **Question:** "Tell me about a time you worked under pressure."

  **S:** "On a client project, a data fix had to go out before the client's
  month-end reports ran, with two days' notice."
  **T:** "I owned the script that corrected about 40,000 records."
  **A:** "I ran it first on a copy of the data and compared counts before and
  after. I found 300 records it would have changed wrongly, fixed the condition,
  and ran it again. I told my lead the timing twice a day so nobody was
  surprised."
  **R:** "It went out a day early with no incorrect records, and the reports ran
  on time. I still test every bulk change on a copy first."

  Your own version must be true. The shape is what to copy.
  :::
::

## Four stories cover almost everything

You do not need a story for every possible question. Prepare four, and most
behavioural questions are one of them in a different hat.

| Prepare this story | It answers |
| --- | --- |
| A failure, and what you changed | Failure, mistake, weakness, feedback you received |
| A disagreement, resolved | Conflict, teamwork, a difficult person, persuading someone |
| Learning something fast | Quick learner, new technology, a challenge, initiative |
| Something under pressure or a deadline | Pressure, prioritising, a deadline, going beyond |

::tip
Freshers often think they have no stories because they have no job. You have
college projects, the institute, the job search itself, a part-time job, a
family responsibility, a sports team. The walk-in story above is from a job
search, and it is one of the best stories I have.
::

## Common mistakes

- **"We" all the way through.** The interviewer cannot hire your team. Say what
  you did.
- **A long situation and a short action.** Ten seconds of setup, forty of action.
- **No result.** The story stops. Always end on what happened and one line
  of what you learned.
- **Blaming somebody in a conflict story.** It creeps in through tone. Listen
  for it when you record yourself.
- **Invented stories.** The follow-up questions ("what did your guide say?",
  "what exactly was the error?") find them.
- **Memorising the words.** Memorise the events. The words can change every
  time.

## How to practise

::steps
### Write your four stories in STAR
One page each, in bullet points, not full sentences.

### Say each aloud, timed
60 to 90 seconds. If it runs to three minutes, cut the situation, never the
action.

### Ask a friend to ask two follow-ups on each
"What would you do differently?" and "what did the other person say?" If you can
answer both, the story is ready.
::
