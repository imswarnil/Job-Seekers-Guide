The first ninety days decide how the team sees you for the next two years. None
of it is about being the best programmer. It is about being easy to work with,
finishing small things, and asking at the right time.

## Why it matters

My two first jobs showed me both ends. At the startup there was no orientation
and no training: I was handed a list of Udemy courses and told to watch them.
At Accenture there was a proper orientation, training, friends in the same
batch, and then a project. Wherever you land, the first ninety days are yours to
use or waste, and the habits you form in them last.

## What to do: the first week

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Five days. Almost none of it is code."}
  :::flow-step{label="Get set up, and write down every step" icon="i-lucide-play"}
  Laptop, access, email, tools, the project running on your machine. Your setup
  notes are useful to the next joiner, because the team has forgotten what is
  hard. Offering them is a contribution in week one.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Find out who knows what" icon="i-lucide-users"}
  Who owns which part, who everybody asks, who is patient with questions. Half
  an hour of conversation saves weeks.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Read the structure, then follow one path" icon="i-lucide-route" highlight}
  Look at the folder structure, find where a request starts, and follow one
  feature all the way to the database and back.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Ask for a small first task" icon="i-lucide-check-square"}
  A small bug, a label change, a test. Finishing something in week one matters
  far more than its size.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Say what you do not know, early" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  In week one, "I have not used Git on a team before" is a fact. In month six it
  is something you hid.
  :::
::

## The first month and the first ninety days

| When | Aim for |
| --- | --- |
| Week 1 | Set up, one small task finished, know who to ask |
| Month 1 | Three or four small tasks done without much help. You understand one part of the system well |
| Month 2 | A task of a few days, planned and finished by you. You review someone else's small change |
| Month 3 | Your manager can hand you a normal task and trust it will be done, or that you will say early if it will not |

## The four habits

Not talent. These four are most of what "good to work with" means.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-eye" title="Be visible"}
  Say what you are doing, where you are, and when something changes. The person
  whose work is invisible until it appears is not trusted with anything urgent,
  because nobody can plan around them.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-clock" title="Ask at forty-five minutes"}
  Agree the number with yourself in advance. If you have made no progress in
  forty-five minutes, write down what you tried and ask. Sitting on a problem for
  a day is not perseverance.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-notebook-pen" title="Write things down"}
  The bug and its cause, the missing setup step, the reason for the strange
  condition. In six months you may be the only one who knows.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-check-check" title="Finish things"}
  Including the boring last ten per cent: the test, the error message, the note.
  A reputation for finishing is the most valuable thing a junior can build, and
  it is built entirely from small things.
  :::
::

::pull-quote
Two people at the same level, and one was the better programmer. He was given
less responsibility, and it took me a while to see why: nobody ever knew where
he was until he was finished. Surprise is the thing a manager is trying to
remove.
::

## A worked example: the status message

The habit of being visible, in one message at the end of a day.

::compare{caption="The same day's work, reported two ways."}
  :::compare-side{label="Invisible" verdict="wrong"}
  (nothing all day, then at 7 pm) "Done."

  Nobody knew it was hard, nobody could help, and nobody knows what "done"
  covers.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Visible" verdict="right"}
  "Fee report bug: found the cause (students with two instalments counted
  twice). Fix is written and tested on my machine. Raising it for review
  tomorrow morning. No blockers."

  Four lines, and the lead can plan around it.
  :::
::

## If you were not given what you wanted

At Accenture I wanted web development and was assigned Salesforce. That happens
constantly at large companies, where people are placed by what the projects
need.

::steps
### Learn the assigned technology properly anyway
Everything is easier to leave from a position of being good at it. Salesforce
turned out to be my career.

### Say what you want, once, clearly, to your manager
"I'd like to move towards web development when there's an opening." Then remind
them at appraisal time. You might get what you want if you ask and push for it.
You will not if you only complain.

### Keep learning what you want on your own time
It is what lets you switch into it later, internally or at another company.
::

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting to be told what to learn.** Nobody will. Ask what the team uses and
  start there.
- **Pretending to understand in meetings.** Write the question down and ask
  afterwards, one to one.
- **Comparing salaries with your batch in week one.** Everybody does, and it
  helps nobody.
- **Stopping learning because the job is now the job.** This is the start of
  the three-year trap from the previous page.

## How to practise

Before day one, write three things on a card: your forty-five-minute rule, the
status message format above, and one thing you want to be good at in ninety
days. Look at it at the end of each week.
