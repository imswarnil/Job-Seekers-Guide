For most people in Indian IT, the biggest salary jumps come from switching
companies, not from appraisals. The first switch is usually worth considering
somewhere between eighteen months and three years in, and the mistake to avoid
is not switching too early. It is not noticing you have stopped growing.

## Why it matters

An appraisal is a percentage of your current salary, often single digits for a
fresher at a large company. A switch is priced by a different company against
the market for your skills. The two numbers drift apart every year you stay.

## When to switch

Not a rule. Four honest signals, and one non-signal.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-repeat" title="The last six months taught you nothing new"}
  Not "no new framework". No new **problem**. That is the real measure.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-trending-down" title="You are well below market and a raise will not fix it"}
  At a large service company the pay band for your level is the ceiling, not
  your ability. The switch is the raise.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-users" title="Nobody around you is better than you at anything"}
  You cannot learn from people you have already caught up with.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-heart-crack" title="It is affecting you outside work"}
  I left Cognizant after nine months, because of micromanagement and how people
  were treated, against the advice to "stay two years or it looks bad". It was
  the right decision, and nobody has ever held it against me.
  :::
::

**The non-signal: a bad month.** Every job has them. Leaving during one usually
takes the problem with you.

| Time in the first job | What usually makes sense |
| --- | --- |
| 0–12 months | Learn everything. Switch only for a clear reason: a much better role, a bad environment, or a chance you would regret missing |
| 12–24 months | Start checking your market value. Keep learning deliberately |
| 24–36 months | For most people, the window for the first switch. You have enough experience to be priced properly |
| 36+ months in the same role | Ask yourself honestly whether you are still growing. This is where I got stuck |

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Check the bond before planning a switch. Leaving before a bond ends can cost you
the bond amount. Know the date it ends, and plan around it.
::

## How to look while you are still employed

::steps
### Keep learning on your own time
I studied on Sundays even in my first job, six days a week at ₹13,000 a month,
and that is how I got to Accenture two months later. The same habit, restarted
years later, got me my switch.

### Update your profiles quietly
Naukri lets you hide your profile from your current company. On LinkedIn, set
"open to work" to recruiters only.

### Interview in one window
Several companies in the same three or four weeks, so the offers arrive together
and can be compared. That is how five offers happened for me.

### Know your notice period and your bond end date
They decide when you can actually join, and whether companies will wait.

### Do not resign until the offer letter is signed
Never on a verbal offer. And tell your manager in person before the email, and
leave well: you will meet these people again.
::

## A worked example: my first switch

::real-life{title="From about 5 LPA to 15.5" source="Leaving Accenture"}
After three years at Accenture on about 5 LPA, I met the friend who had been a
job seeker with me. He had switched and was on 21 LPA. That conversation did
more than any appraisal ever had. I decided to switch, prepared properly,
interviewed with several companies, cracked five of them, and joined Cognizant
on the best offer, 15.5 LPA. Nine months later I left, cracked PwC and Twilio,
and joined Twilio at over 30 LPA.
::

What I would tell myself at the start:

- **Check your market value every year**, even if you are happy. Ask people who
  switched. It costs one conversation.
- **Keep learning when the job stops forcing you to.** That is the whole trap.
- **Do not confuse comfort with growth.** Both feel fine from the inside.

## Common mistakes

- **Staying three years without checking the market.** My mistake.
- **Switching every eight months for a small raise.** A pattern of very short
  stays does get questioned. One short stay with a good reason does not.
- **Switching for money alone into a role you will not learn in.** The next
  switch is priced on what you learned in this one.
- **Resigning before the offer is signed.**
- **Telling colleagues before your manager.**

## How to practise

Today, write three numbers: your current in-hand salary, the market range for
your role and years (from at least three sources), and the date your bond or
probation ends. If the gap between the first two is large and the third has
passed, it is time to start preparing.
