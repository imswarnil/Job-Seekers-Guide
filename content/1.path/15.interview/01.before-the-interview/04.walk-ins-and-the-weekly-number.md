A walk-in is an interview drive with no appointment: a company announces a date,
a time and an address, and anybody who matches the eligibility turns up with a
resume. I went to 33 of them. I was selected at the 34th.

## Why it matters

For a fresher with no referral and no campus placement, walk-ins are the one
channel where a person definitely looks at you. Nobody filters your resume out
before you arrive. You are in the building.

They are also exhausting. A big drive can have 700 to 1000 candidates, start at
9 in the morning and finish after dark, with a written test, a technical round
and an HR round in one day, and most people sent home after the first.

::real-life{title="Walk-in number 34" source="Bangalore, 2018–2019"}
I had been to 33. I knew the routine by then: arrive early, queue, written test,
wait, results read out, most of the room leaves. At the 34th there were
somewhere between 700 and 1000 of us. I cleared the written test, then the
technical round, then HR, and I was selected. It was a startup, 1.8 LPA,
₹13,000 a month, six days a week, with a bond. I took it that week and I was
right to.
::

## What to do

::steps
### Find them
Naukri's walk-in filter, LinkedIn posts, company career pages, Telegram and
WhatsApp groups of job seekers, and the notice boards and groups at training
institutes. In BTM Layout and Marathahalli, people in the PG will tell you about
the next day's drives at dinner.

### Check the company before you travel
Search the company name with "reviews" and "fraud". Check it has a real website
and a real office at that address. A drive that charges a fee is not a drive.

### Check the eligibility line
Year of passing, degree, percentage cut-off, skills. If it says 2017 and 2018
pass-outs with 60% throughout, and you are not, you will be sent back at the
gate.

### Go early
Tokens are handed out in the order people arrive. At 9 am the queue is shorter
and the interviewers are fresh.

### Carry the kit
The list is below. Carry it every time, even when the listing does not ask.
::

## What to carry

```text [walk-in-bag.txt]
[ ] 5 printed copies of the resume (they get kept at every desk)
[ ] Photo ID (Aadhaar or PAN) + one photocopy
[ ] Mark sheets and degree certificate: PHOTOCOPIES, not originals
[ ] 2 passport photos
[ ] 2 pens (the written test does not provide one)
[ ] Phone charged, power bank, water bottle
[ ] Something to eat (it can be 8 hours)
[ ] The invite or listing screenshot with the address and contact name
[ ] A notebook with your answers to "tell me about yourself" and your project
```

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
**Never hand over original certificates** at a walk-in, and be very careful at
joining. A legitimate company verifies originals by looking at them and hands
them back. A company that insists on keeping your originals is a company to
walk away from.
::

## Which walk-ins are worth a day

::compare{caption="A day costs you the same either way."}
  :::compare-side{label="Usually a wasted day" verdict="wrong"}
  - "Freshers and experienced, all roles, all streams, 500 openings"
  - The address is a hotel or a coaching centre, not the company's office
  - The role is "BPO / non-voice / back office" and you want development
  - Any mention of fees, deposits or "training charges"
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Usually worth it" verdict="right"}
  - A named role you match: "Java Developer, 0–1 years"
  - At the company's own office, with a named contact
  - Eligibility you meet exactly
  - A company you can find reviews for, from employees
  :::
::

## The weekly number

A job search is a numbers game at the top and a quality game at the bottom. You
need enough applications going in that some become interviews, and enough
interviews that you can learn from them.

| Every week | Target | Why this number |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Online applications, matched to the role | 25–35 | About 5 a day. Enough volume, still each one read properly |
| Referral requests | 5–10 | Most will not reply. The ones that do are worth ten applications |
| Walk-ins | 1–3 | A walk-in costs a full day. More than three and the studying stops |
| Mock interview or recorded practice | 2 | Every real interview is better after one |
| Study hours on the subjects | 20+ | Interviews test what you know, and the search is not an excuse to stop |

::note
These are what worked for people I knew, not a rule. The one rule is to keep
studying through the search. I studied on Sundays even after I had my first job,
and that is how I got to Accenture two months later.
::

Track every application in a sheet. It takes ten seconds per row and saves you
from the call where a recruiter says "you applied to us" and you have no idea
for what.

```text [job-tracker.csv]
date,company,role,channel,contact,status,next_step
2026-10-06,<Company>,Java Developer,Naukri,,applied,
2026-10-06,<Company>,Associate Engineer,Referral,<Name>,referred,follow up 10-10
2026-10-08,<Company>,Java Trainee,Walk-in,,written cleared; tech failed,revise joins
```

The last column is the one that matters. After every rejection, write the one
thing that beat you. After ten rejections you have a study plan that no
institute could give you.

## Common mistakes

- **Stopping studying because "I am busy applying".** The applications are an
  hour a day. The rest is still study.
- **Treating a rejection as a verdict on you.** 33 of mine were. The 34th was
  not. Most rejections at a walk-in are about how many seats there were.
- **Going to every walk-in in the city.** Three good ones a week beat six random
  ones, and leave you days to study.
- **Not writing down what you were asked.** Write the questions on the bus home.
  The same ones come back at the next company.
- **Signing anything on the day without reading it.** Offers at walk-ins can
  come with a bond. Read it; the offer chapter explains what to look for.

## How to practise

After each walk-in, spend fifteen minutes writing three things: the written test
topics, every technical question you remember, and the one answer you would give
differently. Before the next one, read the last three sets of notes.
