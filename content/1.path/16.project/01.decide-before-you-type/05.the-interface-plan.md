Thirteen tables, three roles and a rule for every cell. Now the part a person
actually touches, and the reason to plan it rather than discover it is that
screens invented one at a time never agree with each other.

## Why this matters in a job

Because inconsistency reads as amateur faster than anything else. A reviewer
does not notice a clever query. They notice that the back button is in a
different place on three pages, and that one of them says "Applicant" where the
others say "Candidate".

It also matters because the states are where beginners lose. Everybody builds
the screen that shows twelve applications. Almost nobody builds the screen that
shows zero, or the one that is still loading, or the one where the database is
down.

## The four states, every time

::flow{numbered caption="A screen is not done when it renders data. It is done when it renders all four of these."}
  :::flow-step{title="Loading"}
  A skeleton in the shape of the content, not a spinner in the middle of an
  empty page. The reader should be able to tell what is arriving.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="Empty" highlight}
  Nothing to show, and the reason. "No applications yet" is a statement.
  "No applications yet. They appear here within a minute of the form being
  submitted" is an answer.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="Error"}
  What failed, and what the person can do. Never a stack trace, and never the
  word "error" alone.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="Content"}
  The one everybody builds first and the only one that is easy.
  :::
::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
The empty state is the first thing a reviewer sees, because they open your
deployed project with an empty database. If `/admin` shows a blank white page
with a heading, that is your entire first impression. Seed the database, and
build the empty state as though it is the front page.
::

## The shared pieces

Six components carry all twenty screens. Decide them now and every screen after
the third is quick.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-layout" title="Shell"}
  Header, role-aware navigation, the skip link, and the footer. Written once.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-table" title="DataTable"}
  Sortable, paginated, with its own empty and loading states. Used by eleven of
  the twenty screens.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-badge-check" title="StatusChip"}
  The five admissions statuses, one colour each, **and the word**. Colour alone
  is not a message to somebody who cannot distinguish them.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-form-input" title="Field"}
  Label, input, hint, error. The error is tied to the input with
  `aria-describedby`, so a screen reader announces it.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-panel-top" title="PageHeader"}
  Title, breadcrumb, and the primary action in the same corner on every page.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-message-square" title="EmptyState"}
  Icon, one sentence of what goes here, one of how it gets here.
  :::
::

## The one screen worth designing carefully

`/staff/:subject/attendance`. A lecturer opens it forty times a term, in front
of a class, on a phone, in two minutes between sessions.

::persona
---
name: Priya Menon
role: Assistant Professor, Computer Science
context: Teaches three subjects, 180 students in total, marks attendance from the corridor between classes
---
"The old system took four taps per student. Sixty students, in a corridor, on a
phone. I stopped using it and went back to paper, and then somebody in the
office typed the paper in at the end of the month, and by then the 75% warnings
were useless because the term was over."
::

Which tells you the design: everybody present by default, tap only the absent
ones, one submit. Four taps per student becomes four taps per class, and the
data arrives the same day rather than a month later.

::pull-quote
The most-used screen in a system deserves more design time than the most
impressive one. Nobody demonstrates an attendance register, and it is the reason
the whole thing gets used or abandoned.
::

## Mobile

Not a separate design. The applicant is on a phone at 11pm and the lecturer is
on a phone in a corridor, which is two of your four actors.

The rule that covers most of it: a table with more than three columns becomes a
list of cards below 48rem. `DataTable` handles that once, and eleven screens get
it.

## What you can do now

You can list every screen in a system before building one, name the four states
each needs, and identify the shared components before they have been written
three different ways.

## Check yourself

1. Why is the empty state more important than the content state for somebody
   reviewing your project?
2. **An interviewer asks: "how do you decide what to build first?"**

## The interview question this answers

**"How do you approach the front end of a feature?"**

The sequence: list the screens, decide the shared components, then build the
states in the order loading, empty, error, content, because the last one is the
easy one and building it first is how the other three never get built.

Then the detail that shows you have shipped something: I design the screen
people use most, not the screen that demonstrates best, and I name it. A
lecturer marking attendance from a corridor is a design constraint, and it is a
more useful sentence than anything about frameworks.
