You have every tool. What you do not have is a single sentence saying what the
thing is supposed to do, and that is the gap most first projects fall into.

Here is where it starts. A registrar says: **"we need something to run our
admissions."** That is the whole brief. It is also, genuinely, how the job
arrives.

## Why this matters in a job

Because the most expensive bug in software is building the wrong thing
correctly, and it is invisible until somebody sees it. Nobody rejects your pull
request for it. The code passes review, the tests are green, and four weeks
later the registrar says "but it has to handle a student who re-applies".

In my first job I built a fee report that was flawless and useless, because I
had assumed a fee was paid once. At that university about 40% were paid in two
instalments. Nobody had told me, and I had not asked, so the report showed
roughly half the money the university had actually collected.

::real-life{title="Where the requirement actually comes from" source="Any admissions office, any year"}
Nobody hands you a specification. You get a sentence in a corridor, a
forwarded email with three replies above it, and a spreadsheet somebody has been
maintaining by hand for six years. The spreadsheet is the requirement. Read the
column headings, and ask why each one exists.
::

## The four questions

A requirement document is not long. It answers four things.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Every one of these is a question you ask a person, not a decision you make alone."}
  :::flow-step{title="Who uses it?"}
  The **actors**. Not "users". An applicant is not a student, and a lecturer is
  not an administrator, and the moment you write them down separately you
  discover they need different things.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="What does each one need to get done?"}
  The **jobs**. Written from their side, not yours: "see whether my application
  moved", not "GET /api/applications/:id".
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="What is explicitly not in this?" highlight}
  The **boundary**. This is the one everybody skips and the one that saves the
  project. A thing written as out of scope can be added later on purpose. A
  thing never mentioned arrives as a crisis.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="How do we know it works?"}
  The **acceptance criteria**. A sentence somebody else could test without
  asking you what you meant.
  :::
::

## The actors, for this system

Four, and they never collapse into fewer.

| Actor | Who they actually are | The thing they care about most |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Applicant** | A stranger on the public site, often on a phone, often at 11pm | Did my application go through, and where is it now |
| **Student** | Admitted, has a roll number, logs in all year | Results, attendance, what I owe |
| **Faculty** | Teaches subjects, marks attendance and exams | Getting the class list and entering marks without fighting the interface |
| **Admin** | Registrar and office staff | The pipeline moving, and the numbers being right |

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
An applicant **becomes** a student. That single sentence has consequences for
every table you are about to design, and getting it wrong is the most common
structural mistake in this kind of system. We come back to it in the data model
lesson, and it is worth arriving there already suspicious.
::

## Jobs, written properly

The format that keeps you honest: **as an `<actor>`, I need to `<job>`, so that
`<reason>`.** The reason is the part that does the work, because a job with no
reason is usually a feature somebody imagined.

::compare{caption="The same line, written twice."}
  :::compare-side{label="A feature request" verdict="wrong"}
  "Add a status dropdown to the application page."

  This tells you what to build and nothing about whether it should exist. You
  will build it. It will be wrong, and nobody will be able to say why.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="A job" verdict="right"}
  "As an **admin**, I need to move an application to `SHORTLISTED` **and record
  who did it**, so that when a parent rings to ask why their child was
  rejected, somebody can answer."

  Now you know it needs an audit trail. The dropdown was never the requirement.
  :::
::

Ten to fifteen of those covers this entire system. Write them before you draw
anything.

## The boundary

Say what this is not, in writing, in the same document.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="Not in version one"}
  Online fee payment through a gateway, SMS notifications, a mobile app,
  timetable generation, hostel allocation, and anything involving money leaving
  the university.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-check" title="In version one"}
  Enquiry to admission, the student and faculty portals, fee **records** (not
  payments), attendance, results, and the dashboards the registrar reads on a
  Monday.
  :::
::

::tip
Payment is out of scope on purpose, and it is worth being able to say why: it
brings a payment provider, a reconciliation problem and a compliance question,
and none of those teaches you anything the rest of the system does not. Recording
that a payment happened is a database problem. Taking the money is somebody
else's product.
::

## Acceptance criteria

The difference between "done" and "done".

Take the enquiry form. Here is the criteria I would write, and each one is
testable by a person who did not build it:

1. A stranger can submit the form on a phone with no account.
2. A submission with a malformed email is refused **by the server**, and says
   which field is wrong.
3. A successful submission appears in the `Enquiry` table within a second, with
   its source recorded.
4. Submitting the same email and programme twice within an hour is flagged as a
   possible duplicate rather than silently creating two rows.
5. Thirty submissions from one address in a minute are rate limited.

Criterion 2 is the one that matters most, and I will keep saying it: client-side
validation is a kindness, server-side validation is the truth. Anyone can send a
request that never touched your form.

## What you can do now

You can take one sentence from a person and turn it into a document with four
actors, a dozen jobs, an explicit boundary and testable criteria. That document
is the thing you will point at every time somebody asks for something in week
six.

## Your turn

Write the requirements for the **student portal** alone. Four jobs, in the
"as a, I need to, so that" format, and three acceptance criteria for the
attendance screen.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What a good answer contains" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Jobs that sound like a student rather than a developer:

  - As a student, I need to see my attendance per subject, so that I know which
    class I cannot afford to miss again.
  - As a student, I need to see what I owe and by when, so that I am not blocked
    from sitting an exam.
  - As a student, I need to see my results for every semester, so that I can
    calculate my own aggregate without asking the office.
  - As a student, I need to register for next semester's subjects, so that I do
    not have to queue.

  Criteria for the attendance screen, written so somebody else could test them:

  1. A student sees their own attendance and no other student's, enforced by the
     database rather than by hiding the link.
  2. Each subject shows attended, total, and a percentage to one decimal place.
  3. A subject below the university's 75% threshold is marked visually **and**
     in text, because colour alone is not a message.

  The third criterion is an accessibility requirement written as a requirement
  rather than remembered as an afterthought, which is the only way it survives.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why is "what is not in this" worth writing down, given that not building
   something is the default?
2. **An interviewer asks: "how do you handle a requirement that changes halfway
   through?"**

## The interview question this answers

**"Tell me how you start a project."**

The weak answer names a stack. The answer that separates you describes a
sequence: I find out who the actors are, I write the jobs from their side with
the reason attached, I agree in writing what is out of scope, and I write
acceptance criteria somebody else can test. Then I design the data, because that
is the part that is expensive to change later.

Then add the honest bit: I have built the wrong thing correctly before, and the
cause was an assumption I never said out loud. Naming a specific one, like
assuming a fee is paid once, is worth more than any amount of process language.
