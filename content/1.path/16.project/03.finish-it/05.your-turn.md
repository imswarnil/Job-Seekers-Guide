Twelve answers read is not twelve answers said. This one is uncomfortable and it
is the highest-value exercise in the track, because the gap between knowing your
project and describing it is enormous and completely invisible until you try.

## 1. Record it

Two minutes. Your phone. Out loud, standing up, no notes.

Describe the University Management App using the four-part shape: the problem
with a person in it, what you built in one sentence, one decision argued, one
thing you would change.

Then listen to it. Nobody enjoys this part.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Score yourself against these five" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  1. **Did the first sentence contain a person, or a technology?** If a
     technology appears before a human being, start again. This is the most
     common failure and it costs the most.
  2. **Was it under two minutes?** Most first attempts are four. An interviewer
     stops listening at ninety seconds if there is no shape.
  3. **Did you argue exactly one decision, or mention five?** Five is a feature
     list. One with its rejected alternative is evidence you made it.
  4. **Did you say what you would change?** If the answer ended on a claim of
     success, it sounded like everybody else's.
  5. **Did you say "basically" or "kind of" more than twice?** Those appear when
     you are describing something you do not fully own. They are a signal to
     yourself about which part to go and understand properly.
  :::
::

Do it three times. The third one is the one you will actually give.

## 2. The hostile follow-up

Have somebody ask you this, and answer without preparing:

> "You said authorisation is in the database. Isn't that harder to test, and
> doesn't it tie you to Postgres?"

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What a good answer contains" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Both halves of the question are **true**, and the answer that fails is the one
  that argues they are not.

  Yes, it is harder to test: the policies only exist in a real database, so
  those tests cannot use a mock and are slower. I accepted that, and it had a
  side effect I now think is a benefit, which is that the concurrency tests run
  against real Postgres behaviour too.

  Yes, it ties me to Postgres. I was already tied: the schema uses `CHECK`
  constraints, `FILTER`, and window functions in the reporting views. Portability
  was not a requirement anybody had, and paying for it would have meant giving up
  the thing that makes a forgotten check safe.

  The move being tested is whether you can accept a real cost without getting
  defensive. Conceding a true point and explaining why you paid it is the most
  convincing thing you can do in a technical round.
  :::
::

## 3. The demo that has to work

Open your deployed URL in a private window, with no session, on your phone, and
time yourself getting to something impressive.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What has to be true" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  - A demo account per role is **stated on the page or in the README**. A
    reviewer will not sign up and will not email you.
  - The database is seeded. An empty pipeline is not a demonstration of a
    pipeline.
  - The first screen after signing in as an admin shows the funnel with real
    numbers in it.
  - It loads in under three seconds on mobile data.

  If getting to something impressive takes more than thirty seconds, the
  problem is the landing, not the project.
  :::
::

## 4. The artefacts

Before this track is done, these six files exist and are honest.

| File | Contains | The question it answers in a room |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `README.md` | What, why, live URL, demo accounts, one command to run | "Can I see it?" |
| `docs/requirements.md` | Actors, jobs, scope boundary, criteria | "How did you start?" |
| `docs/decisions.md` | Four decisions, each with its rejected alternative | "Why did you build it that way?" |
| `docs/access.md` | The matrix | "How is it secured?" |
| `db/schema.sql` | Thirteen tables and every constraint | "Walk me through your data model" |
| `db/seed.sql` | Forty realistic rows, including the messy ones | "Can I click around?" |

::tip
`docs/decisions.md` is the file that does the most work per minute spent. It is
four entries, twenty minutes, and it converts "I chose a monolith" from a claim
into something you can open on a screen while answering.
::

## 5. The honest audit

Answer these three for yourself, in writing. Nobody sees them.

1. **Which part of this project could I not rebuild from scratch?** That is the
   part you copied rather than understood, and it is the part an interviewer
   will find, because it is where your answers get vague.
2. **Which decision did I make because a tutorial did it that way?** At least
   one, always. Go and find out what the alternative was, even if you keep the
   choice.
3. **If they open the deployed URL right now, what do they see?** Go and look.

::pull-quote
The project is not the code. It is the code plus your ability to defend it, and
the second half is the half that is actually being interviewed.
::
