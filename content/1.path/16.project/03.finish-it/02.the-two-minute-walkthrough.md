It is built, tested and on your laptop. Now the part that decides whether any of
it counts: saying what it is, to somebody who has 120 seconds and eleven other
candidates.

## Why this matters in a job

Because this is where good projects die. I have watched people who built
genuinely impressive things lose the room in ninety seconds, and it is always
the same mistake: they start with the technology.

"It is a full-stack application built with Java, Postgres, server-side
rendering and row-level security" tells an interviewer nothing except that you
can read your own `package.json`. Everybody's project is a list of technologies.
Almost nobody's starts with a person.

## The shape

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Two minutes, four parts. Time each one. The first is twenty seconds and it does most of the work."}
  :::flow-step{title="The problem, with a person in it" highlight}
  Twenty seconds. Who was struggling, and with what.

  *"A university admissions office was running its intake on a shared
  spreadsheet. Four people edited it, nobody knew who had rejected whom, and
  parents rang asking why their child was rejected and nobody could answer."*
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="What you built, in one sentence"}
  Fifteen seconds, no technology yet.

  *"I built the system that runs the intake: enquiry to admission, with the
  student and staff portals underneath it."*
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="One decision, argued"}
  Sixty seconds. This is the meat, and it is one decision, not five.

  *"The one I would defend hardest: authorisation lives in the database as
  row-level policies, not in the route handlers. A student cannot read another
  student's marks even if I forget a check, because the query returns nothing
  rather than everything. It fails closed."*
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="What you would change"}
  Twenty-five seconds. Ends on honesty rather than a claim.

  *"I built the attendance register the obvious way and a lecturer told me it
  was slower than paper. I would now ask who uses a screen most, and how long
  they have, before designing it."*
  :::
::

::pull-quote
Lead with the problem, not the stack. The stack is the answer to a question they
have not asked yet, and they will ask it.
::

## Why the last part is not a weakness

People fight me on this. Ending on something you got wrong feels like handing
over a reason to reject you.

It is the opposite, and here is why: every candidate claims their project went
well. The one who names a specific thing a real person complained about is the
only one who has evidence that real people used it. It is also the only answer
that cannot be memorised from a template, because it requires having been
corrected.

::real-life{title="What the interviewer is actually doing" source="Any technical round, any company"}
They are not evaluating the project. They mostly cannot, in two minutes. They
are working out whether you understand what you built or assembled it from
answers.

The test is depth on one thing. Anybody can describe five features. Only the
person who made the decision can explain what they rejected and why.
::

## The three follow-ups

They always come, in roughly this order.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. Why did you choose X?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Whatever X is, answer in the same shape every time: **the constraint, the
  choice, the rejected alternative, and when it stops being right.**

  *"One developer, a few thousand users, and every question the registrar asks
  is a join. So a monolith on Postgres. I rejected services because they solve
  an organisational problem, letting teams deploy independently, and I did not
  have teams. It stops being right the moment more than one team needs to
  deploy without coordinating."*

  Naming when your own decision expires is the single most senior-sounding thing
  available in this conversation.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. What was the hardest part?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Not "learning the framework". Pick something with a name.

  *"Two admins accepting the last seat at the same moment. It is a lost update,
  and every major database allows it at its default isolation level. I used a
  conditional update with the row count checked, because clashes are rare here,
  and put a `CHECK` constraint underneath so a script cannot bypass it."*

  Three sentences containing a named bug, a chosen fix with a reason, and a
  defence against the case you cannot control.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. How would you scale it?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  The trap is enthusiasm. The answer that impresses is a refusal followed by a
  plan.

  *"At this size I would not. A few thousand users on one Postgres instance is
  not a scaling problem, and most of what I could add would be complexity with
  no measurement behind it. If it grew: I would measure first, and my guess is
  the reporting queries would hurt before anything else, so read replicas for
  the dashboards and materialised views for the funnel. But I would want the
  slow query log before I touched anything."*

  "I would measure first" said with conviction beats any architecture.
  :::
::

## The one-page README

The walkthrough has a written form, and it is the first file anybody opens.

```md [README.md]
# University Management App

Admissions, student and staff portals for a university intake of ~2,000.
Built to replace a shared spreadsheet that four people edited at once.

**Live:** https://... · **Sign in as:** a demo account per role, below.

## What it does
Enquiry → application → review → admission, then attendance, results and fees.

## Decisions worth reading
- Authorisation as row-level policies, not route checks. [Why](docs/decisions.md#4)
- Monolith, server-rendered. [Why](docs/decisions.md#1)
- Fees stored in paise as integers, frozen at payment. [Why](docs/decisions.md#3)

## Running it
    git clone ... && make dev      # seeds the database and starts on :3000
```

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
**Demo accounts, one per role, stated in the README.** A reviewer with sixty
seconds will not sign up, will not seed a database, and will not email you for
credentials. They will close the tab. This single line is worth more than any
feature you could add in the remaining time.
::

## What you can do now

You can describe eight weeks of work in two minutes, lead with a person rather
than a stack, defend one decision properly, and answer the three questions that
always follow.

## Check yourself

1. Why does ending on something you got wrong make the answer stronger rather
   than weaker?
2. **An interviewer asks: "tell me about a project you are proud of."** Do it in
   two minutes, out loud, timed.

## The interview question this answers

**"Tell me about something you built."**

Problem with a person in it, what you built in one sentence, one decision
argued with its rejected alternative, and one thing you would change because
somebody real complained.

Then stop talking. The silence after a two-minute answer is not a gap you need
to fill; it is the interviewer deciding which of the three follow-ups to ask,
and you already know all three.
