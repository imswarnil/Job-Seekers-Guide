Somebody hands you two paragraphs describing what to build, and half of what you
need is not in them. That is not bad writing. It is what a specification always
is, because the person writing it knows the domain so well that the obvious parts
are invisible to them.

Your job is to find the invisible parts before you build.

## Why this matters in a job

Because building the wrong thing correctly is the most expensive mistake
available to you, and it is entirely preventable by forty minutes of reading.

::real-life{title="The two weeks I lost" source="My first year, a reporting feature"}
I was asked to build a report of "active students". I built it. It was wrong,
because "active" meant enrolled *and* fee-paid *and* not on medical leave, and
every one of those was obvious to the person who asked and invisible to me. Two
weeks. The forty minutes of asking would have cost nothing and I did not ask
because I thought asking would look like not knowing.
::

## The specification

Here is a real-shaped one. Read it and see how complete it feels.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-file-text" color="neutral"}
**From: Registrar's office**
**Subject: Shortlisting for the 2026 intake**

We need a screen where staff can shortlist applicants for a programme. The user
should be able to enter a minimum score and see all applicants above it, then
mark the ones they want to shortlist. Shortlisted applicants should receive an
email.

Please also show the total number of applicants so we know how the intake is
going. This needs to be ready before the 15th.
::

Four sentences. It feels complete. It is missing at least eleven things, and
every one of them will surface as a bug in the third week if you do not ask now.

## The four questions to ask of any specification

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Run every request through these four. They catch almost everything."}
  :::flow-step{label="Which nouns are undefined?" icon="i-lucide-spell-check"}
  Circle every noun. For each one: do I know exactly what set of rows this is?
  "Applicant" and "staff" both look obvious and neither is.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="What happens at the edges?" icon="i-lucide-move-horizontal"}
  Zero, one, many, too many. Exactly equal to the boundary. Missing. Duplicate.
  Every one of these is a decision somebody has to make.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Who is allowed?" icon="i-lucide-lock" highlight}
  "Staff" is not a permission. Which staff, for which programme, and what stops a
  lecturer in physics shortlisting for computer science?
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="What is irreversible?" icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
  That email cannot be unsent. Anything in a specification that reaches the
  outside world needs its own conversation.
  :::
::

## The eleven holes

Applying those four to the four sentences above.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="What is an applicant?"}
  One person, or one application? A person applying to three programmes is one
  applicant and three applications, and the total will be wrong for one of those
  readings.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="Which applicants?"}
  This intake only? Withdrawn ones? Rejected ones from last year who reapplied?
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="Score of what?"}
  Qualifying examination, aptitude test, or the composite? These are three
  different numbers and the request says "score".
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="What about no score yet?"}
  Two hundred applicants are unmarked. Do they appear, disappear, or appear
  flagged? Silently excluding them is the default and it is usually wrong.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="Above, or at and above?"}
  "Above 60" — is 60 in? This sounds pedantic until eleven applicants are on
  exactly 60 and a parent telephones.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="Which staff?"}
  Any staff member, or only that programme's department? This is the security
  question and it is never in the specification.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="Can shortlisting be undone?"}
  If yes, does a second email go out saying "actually, no"? Irreversibility is
  the question nobody asks and everybody regrets.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="What does the email say?"}
  Somebody has to write it, it has to be approved, and it is going to a real
  person's real address. This is a whole task hiding inside the word "email".
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="What if the email fails?"}
  Four hundred sent, one bounces. Is that applicant shortlisted or not?
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="How many is many?"}
  Forty applicants or forty thousand? This one sentence decides whether you need
  pagination, an index, and a background job.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="The 15th of which month?"}
  And is that when it must be live, or when the registrar wants to start testing
  it? Those are two weeks apart.
  :::
::

## What confuses people here

That asking these makes you look inexperienced. It is the exact opposite, and
this is the thing I most wish I had understood in my first year.

::compare{caption="The same person, week two of a first job."}
  :::compare-side{label="Builds from the specification" verdict="wrong"}
  Delivers on the 14th. It counts applications rather than applicants, includes
  withdrawn ones, excludes the unmarked silently, and emails four hundred people.
  Now there is a meeting about it and an email that cannot be recalled.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Sends eleven questions on day one" verdict="right"}
  Gets six answers, three "good question, I will find out", and two "I had not
  thought of that". Delivers on the 15th, correct. Is now the person whose
  estimates people believe.
  :::
::

::tip
Send them as a numbered list, in one message, with your assumption next to each
one. *"7. I am assuming shortlisting can be undone but no second email goes out.
Correct?"* A person can answer that in forty seconds. An open question like "how
should shortlisting work?" hands the work back to them, and busy people do not
answer those.
::

## What you can do now

You can read a request and produce the list of decisions it does not contain,
which is the single most visible thing a junior engineer can do well.

## Your turn

Here is a second request. Find at least six holes.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-file-text" color="neutral"}
*"Students should be able to see their attendance percentage on their dashboard,
and get a warning if it is low."*
::

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Eight I would ask about" icon="i-lucide-list-checks"}
  1. **Percentage of what?** Classes held, or classes scheduled? A cancelled
     lecture changes the denominator and therefore the answer.
  2. **Over what period?** This term, this year, the whole course?
  3. **Per subject or overall?** A student can be at 80 per cent overall and 40
     per cent in one subject, which is the one that will stop them sitting the
     exam.
  4. **What is low?** 75 per cent is the common rule, and it is a policy, not a
     fact. Somebody has to state it and it will change.
  5. **Does the warning do anything?** Is it a colour on a page, or an email to
     the student, or a notification to their tutor? Three different features.
  6. **What if attendance has not been recorded?** A subject with no records will
     show 0 per cent and frighten somebody, or show 100 per cent and lie to them.
     Neither is acceptable and somebody has to choose.
  7. **Who else can see it?** The tutor, the parent, the head of department? This
     is a privacy decision, not an engineering one.
  8. **Rounded how?** 74.6 per cent displayed as 75 per cent, against a 75 per
     cent rule, is a student who thinks they are safe and is not.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. What are the four questions to run any specification through?
2. **An interviewer asks: "you are given a vague requirement and the person who
   wrote it is on leave. What do you do?"**

## The interview question this answers

**"How do you handle unclear requirements?"**

A weak answer is "I ask for clarification", which is true and says nothing. A
strong one is the method: I list the nouns and check each is defined, I list the
edge cases, I check permissions and anything irreversible, and I send my
assumptions as statements so they can be corrected in forty seconds rather than
as open questions that need an essay. Then give the shortlisting example. It is
memorable because it is specific.
