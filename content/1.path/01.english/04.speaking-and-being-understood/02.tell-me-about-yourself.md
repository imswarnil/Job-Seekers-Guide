Four words, and a friend told me in 2019 that they decide more than the technical
round does. I did not believe him. He was right, and it took me three years and
about forty interviews to act on it.

## Why this matters in a job

Because it is asked first, every time, and because everything after it is
coloured by it. An interviewer who has just heard a clear, specific ninety
seconds is now looking for reasons to hire you. One who has heard four minutes of
biography is now looking for reasons not to.

::pull-quote{attribution="Durgesh, 2019. He came up the same way I did, and this is the most useful thing anybody told me in those years."}
"Tell me about yourself." That is what decides whether you get selected. Not the
algorithm question.
::

## What the question is actually asking

Not for your life story. They have your CV. The question is: **why are you in
this room, and what should I ask you about?**

That second part is the one nobody exploits. You are being handed ninety seconds
of uninterrupted control over what the rest of the interview is about. Every
noun you mention is a door you are inviting them to open, so only mention doors
you want opened.

## The four sentences

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Ninety seconds. Roughly one sentence each, and the fourth is the one people leave out."}
  :::flow-step{label="Where you are now" icon="i-lucide-map-pin"}
  One line. "I finished my computer science degree in 2026." Not where you were
  born, not your school, not your percentage.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The decision you made" icon="i-lucide-git-fork"}
  What you found out, and what you did about it. This is the sentence that makes
  you a person rather than a CV, and it is where honesty beats polish.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The evidence" icon="i-lucide-hammer" highlight}
  The thing you built. Specific: what it does, what it is made of, that it is
  live. This is the sentence the rest of the interview will come from.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Why this role" icon="i-lucide-target"}
  Connect the evidence to the job advert. One line. Almost nobody does this and
  it is the difference between "a candidate" and "a candidate for this".
  :::
::

## Watch it happen

::compare{caption="Same person, same CV, same year."}
  :::compare-side{label="What I said for three years" verdict="wrong"}
  "My name is Swarnil. I am from Mahroni, a small town in Uttar Pradesh. I did my
  schooling there and then I went to Kota for two years to prepare for IIT. After
  that I did my B.Tech in computer science from LNCT Bhopal, where I scored 71 per
  cent. I am passionate about technology and I like to learn new things. I am a
  quick learner and a good team player. Currently I am looking for an opportunity
  where I can utilise my skills and grow with the organisation."

  Ninety seconds, and not one sentence that could not have been said by any of
  the other four thousand applicants. Nothing to ask a follow-up about. No door
  opened.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What works" verdict="right"}
  "I finished computer science at LNCT in 2018, and I came out of it able to pass
  exams and not able to build anything. So I spent the year after that learning
  Java and SQL properly, from the ground up rather than from tutorials.

  What I built with it is an admissions system for a university: applications,
  shortlisting by score, fee tracking and results. It is live, it is about six
  thousand lines, and the part I am most pleased with is that it handles the
  cases that actually break these systems, like an applicant applying to three
  programmes and being counted three times.

  I am applying for the backend role because the part I enjoyed most was the data
  model, and I would rather spend my time on what is true than on what it looks
  like."
  :::
::

Count the doors in the second one: Java, SQL, the admissions system, shortlisting,
the counting bug, the data model. Six follow-up questions, all of which you want.

## The three versions that lose it

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="The biography"}
  Starting at school. Every second spent before you are eighteen is a second not
  spent on evidence, and the interviewer is already scanning your CV for where
  you are going.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="The adjective list"}
  "Quick learner, hard worker, good team player." Unfalsifiable, so it carries no
  information. Everybody says it, including the people who are none of those.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="The apology"}
  "I do not have much experience, but…" Never open on your weakest point. They
  know it is your first job; it is on the CV and it is why the role is open.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="The recital"}
  Memorised word for word and delivered at speed, eyes up and left. Rehearse
  until it is *yours*, not until it is *fixed*. Those are different amounts of
  practice and the second one is audible.
  :::
::

## What confuses people here

That the honest version is the weak version. The strongest answer I ever gave
opened with an admission that my degree had not made me employable. It works
because it is specific, it is clearly true, and it sets up the decision that
follows it. A weakness with a decision attached is a strength story.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Do not invent the evidence. If you say you built something, you will be asked
about it for twenty minutes, and every one of those minutes will find out whether
you did. This is precisely why this course builds one real system across every
track instead of a folder of exercises.
::

## What you can do now

You have the shape and you know what the question is for. What you do not yet
have is the answer, and it only exists once it has been said out loud several
times.

## Your turn

Write yours. Four sentences, one per beat. Then say it out loud five times and
record the fifth.

If you have not built anything yet, your evidence sentence is what you are
building and where you are in it. That is a legitimate answer in month three, and
it is far better than an adjective.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="The version for somebody mid-course" icon="i-lucide-clock"}
  "I graduated in mechanical engineering last year and found out during my final
  project that the part I liked was the data, not the machines.

  So I have spent the last five months learning Java and SQL properly. Right now
  I am about halfway through building an admissions system for a university:
  applications and shortlisting work end to end, and I am on the fee module now.

  I am applying for this role because it is backend work on a real system, and I
  would rather learn the rest of it in a team than alone."

  Honest about where they are, specific about what exists, and it opens the same
  doors. The only thing that would make this weak is if the system did not exist.
  :::
::

::ai-prompt{title="Pressure-test your own answer" model="Any"}
Here is my answer to "tell me about yourself" for an entry-level backend role:
[paste it]. Do not rewrite it. Tell me which sentences carry no information,
which claims I would be asked to prove, and then ask me the five follow-up
questions an interviewer would ask after hearing this.

  #why
  The follow-up questions are the real output. If they are questions you cannot
  answer well, your opening has opened the wrong doors, and that is fixable in
  ten minutes before an interview rather than discovered during one.
::

## Check yourself

1. What are the four sentences, and which one does almost everybody leave out?
2. **Say your own answer out loud, right now, and time it.** Over two minutes
   means cut a sentence.

## The interview question this answers

It is the question. Every interview, every round, including the ones with a
whiteboard. Get this one right and you have spent your first ninety seconds
deciding what the next forty minutes will be about, which is the only free
advantage available to a candidate.
