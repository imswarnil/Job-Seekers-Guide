Ninety seconds that decide the interview, and you have not yet said them out
loud. So before the machine, one week of doing rather than reading. Every
exercise here produces an artefact you keep and use for years.

::note
Nothing in this file is optional if you want the track to have been worth the two
weeks. Reading about clarity does not produce clarity; it is a performance skill
and it only exists after repetition.
::

## Monday: the passage, timed twice

Find one comprehension passage from any aptitude source. Do it cold, the way you
normally read, and time it. Then do a second passage with the technique:
questions first, skim for structure, locate-a-fact questions, inference last.

**Keep:** the two times, written down.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What you are looking for" icon="i-lucide-timer"}
  Most people find the second is thirty to fifty per cent faster with the same
  or better accuracy. If it is not, the usual cause is that you read the passage
  anyway out of habit before answering. Try again and genuinely do not.
  :::
::

## Tuesday, six sentences, six rules

Write six sentences about the university, each containing one of the six errors
from chapter one, and then fix each one. Writing the broken version is the
exercise: you cannot plant an error you cannot see.

**Keep:** the twelve sentences.

## Wednesday: the eleven questions

Take this request and produce the list of decisions it does not contain. Aim for
eight.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-file-text" color="neutral"}
*"We want to show each department head how their department is doing this
semester, attendance, results and fee collection on one page."*
::

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Ten I would ask, grouped by the four questions" icon="i-lucide-list-checks"}
  **Undefined nouns.** What is "their department": the one they head, or every
  one they teach in? Does "this semester" mean the current one, which is
  incomplete, or the last completed one? Are results the internal assessments,
  the final examinations, or both?

  **Edges.** What does a department with no results yet show, zeros, blanks, or
  "not published"? What about a subject taught jointly by two departments, which
  department's numbers is it in? What happens to a student who transferred
  mid-semester?

  **Permission.** Can a head see another department's page? Can they see
  individual students, or only aggregates? Fee collection is financial data about
  named people, so who signed off on a head seeing it at all?

  **Irreversible.** Nothing here writes anything, which is worth saying out loud
  because it means this feature is much safer than it looks, and can ship
  earlier.
  :::
::

**Keep:** the list. This exact exercise is what your first week at a job looks
like.

## Thursday: a stack trace, read cold

Take any error you have hit, or generate one deliberately: index an array past
its end, or call a method on something you know is null. Then write four lines:
what failed, where it noticed, where your own code is in the trace, and what you
would look at first.

**Keep:** the four lines. Do this three more times over the next month and the
method becomes automatic.

## Friday: the three messages

Write all three for one incident: the applicant count on the dean's dashboard
reading 2,040 when the registrar's figure is 1,602.

1. **The bug report** to your team. Steps, expected, actual, theory labelled as
   theory.
2. **The commit message** for the fix. Subject line under seventy characters,
   blank line, body explaining why.
3. **The email** to the registrar's office telling them what happened. The ask in
   the first line.

**Keep:** all three. Together they are a complete worked example of professional
written communication, and you can show them to somebody.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What makes the email hard, and how to handle it" icon="i-lucide-mail"}
  The email is the one people get wrong, because the temptation is to explain the
  technical cause. The registrar does not want the cause. They want to know
  whether the number they have been quoting in meetings for three weeks was
  wrong, and what to say about it.

  > **Subject:** Dashboard applicant total was counting applications, corrected today
  >
  > The intake total on your dashboard has been showing 2,040. The correct number
  > of applicants is 1,602, which matches your own report. Nothing was wrong with
  > the data; the screen was counting applications, and an applicant can hold
  > several.
  >
  > It is corrected now and the screen shows both figures with labels. If the
  > 2,040 figure has gone out anywhere, it is not wrong as a count of
  > applications, it was just labelled as applicants.
  :::
::

## Saturday, explain three things out loud

Ninety seconds each, recorded. Answer first, a smaller version of the real thing
second, the cost third, then stop.

1. Why applicants and applications are two tables.
2. The difference between a `NULL` score and a score of zero.
3. Why a check in the browser can never be the real check.

**Keep:** the recordings. Listen to one of them. That is the exercise.

## Sunday: the ninety seconds

Write your answer to "tell me about yourself". Four sentences: where you are, the
decision you made, the evidence, why this role.

Say it out loud five times. Record the fifth.

**Keep:** that recording, and redo it every three months as the evidence sentence
gets better.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
This is the single highest-return hour in this entire course, and it is the one
most likely to be skipped, because recording yourself is uncomfortable and
reading another lesson is not. I skipped it for three years and it cost me more
than any technical gap did.
::

## The cheat sheet

Everything from two weeks, on one card.

| Situation | The shape |
| --- | --- |
| Comprehension passage | Questions → structure → facts → inference |
| Subject-verb agreement | Cross out everything between subject and verb |
| A word you do not know | Break it into prefix, root, suffix |
| Four options | Remove the opposite, the wrong part of speech, the wrong register |
| Scrambled sentences | Find the one with no back-reference; chain on connectives |
| A specification | Undefined nouns → edges → who is allowed → what is irreversible |
| A stack trace | Message → your topmost frame → the call chain → `Caused by` |
| Documentation | Example first → signature, not prose → check the version |
| A new codebase | Run it → folder tree → entry point → one path all the way down |
| A commit message | Imperative subject, blank line, why not what |
| A bug report | Steps → expected → actual → theory, labelled |
| A daily update | Finished → on now → in my way |
| Stuck | Trying to → tried → getting → how long |
| An email upward | Ask first → options → deadline with a reason → context last |
| Explaining out loud | Answer → smaller version → cost → stop |
| Tell me about yourself | Now → the decision → the evidence → why this role |
