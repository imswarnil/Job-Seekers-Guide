The last piece of writing goes to somebody outside your team: a manager, a
client, a registrar. They have power over your work and about eleven seconds of
attention, and your message is one of ninety they received today.

## Why this matters in a job

Because the message that does not get answered is indistinguishable from the
message that was never sent, and the reason it was not answered is almost never
that they did not care.

::real-life{title="Why my emails went unanswered for a year" source="My first job"}
I wrote long, polite, careful emails. I opened with context, explained the
background, described what I had done, and put the question at the bottom. Nobody
answered them. I thought it was rudeness. It was that my question was on line
fourteen and the reader stopped at line three, and every one of my emails asked
them to do work before they could find out what I wanted.
::

## The shape

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Decision first. Everything else is available below it if they want it."}
  :::flow-step{label="A subject line that is the point" icon="i-lucide-type"}
  "Shortlisting email wording, need approval by Thursday", not "Update" or
  "Question". Many people decide whether to open from this line alone.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The ask, in the first sentence" icon="i-lucide-target" highlight}
  What you need and by when. Before any context at all. If they read one line,
  this is the line.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Options, not an open question" icon="i-lucide-list"}
  "A or B, I recommend A because…" is answerable in four seconds. "What should we
  do?" hands them your work, and busy people do not do other people's work.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Context underneath" icon="i-lucide-align-left"}
  Short, and last. It is there for the reader who wants it and skippable for the
  one who does not.
  :::
::

## Watch it happen

::compare{caption="Same request. One of these gets a reply the same morning."}
  :::compare-side{label="What I used to write" verdict="wrong"}
  > **Subject:** Regarding shortlisting
  >
  > Dear Sir,
  >
  > Hope you are doing well. I have been working on the shortlisting feature for
  > the 2026 intake as discussed in last week's meeting. The feature allows staff
  > to filter applicants by score and mark them as shortlisted. I have completed
  > the query and the screen and also handled the case of withdrawn applicants
  > which we discussed.
  >
  > Now the next part is the email which will be sent to the shortlisted
  > applicants. I was thinking about what the content should be. Please let me
  > know your thoughts on this.
  >
  > Thanks and regards
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What works" verdict="right"}
  > **Subject:** Shortlisting email wording, need a decision by Thursday
  >
  > I need the wording for the email that goes to shortlisted applicants, or
  > approval to use the draft below. Thursday, so the feature ships on the 15th.
  >
  > **Option A (recommended):** reuse the existing admission-offer template with
  > the offer paragraph removed. Already approved by legal last year, so no
  > review needed.
  >
  > **Option B:** new wording, which I can draft, but it would need a legal
  > review and that has taken two weeks before.
  >
  > Everything else is done: the query, the screen, and the withdrawn-applicant
  > case. This is the only thing between here and shipping.
  :::
::

The second one is shorter, and it is also more respectful, which is the opposite
of what people assume. The long one is polite in its sentences and demanding in
its structure: it makes the reader excavate.

## The four habits that make a junior's email unanswerable

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="Burying the ask"}
  The question on line fourteen. If they read three lines and there is no ask,
  the message is filed as "something to read later", which means never.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="Asking an open question"}
  "Please share your thoughts." Now they have to think, write, and take
  responsibility. Options let them tick a box.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="No deadline"}
  With no date, everything is equally urgent, so it is all equally ignorable.
  "By Thursday, because it ships on the 15th" gives them a reason.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-x" title="Apologising throughout"}
  "Sorry to disturb you", "sorry for the trouble". It is not a disturbance, it is
  the job, and repeated apology makes the reader feel the message is a burden
  before they have read it.
  :::
::

::tip
Formality is not the same as clarity, and in Indian workplaces the two get
confused constantly. "Dear Sir, hope you are doing well" is fine and costs
nothing. Putting your question after three paragraphs of it is what costs you.
Keep the courtesy, move the ask.
::

## What confuses people here

That being direct is rude. Directness about *what you need* is a kindness to a
busy person; directness about *people* is rudeness. They are unrelated. "I need a
decision by Thursday" is direct and warm. "You did not reply to my last email" is
direct and not.

::side-note{kind="go-deeper"}
Everything in this lesson applies to a pull request description too, which is an
email to a reviewer. Same shape: what this changes and why, in the first two
lines; what you want from them; anything you are unsure about; the detail
underneath. A pull request that opens with "this PR adds the shortlisting
feature" and stops has wasted the most-read two lines in the review.
::

## What you can do now

You can write a message that gets answered the same day, which compounds: the
person who is easy to answer is the person who gets answers, and therefore the
person who is never blocked for long.

## Your turn

Rewrite this into something answerable. It is real in shape.

> **Subject:** Query
>
> Dear Ma'am, hope this mail finds you well. As you know we are working on the
> attendance module. There is an issue where students who have exactly 75%
> attendance are being flagged as short of attendance. The rule says below 75%
> should be flagged. I have checked the code and it seems the comparison is
> using less than or equal. But I am not sure if this was intentional as maybe
> the university policy is different. Kindly guide.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="One version" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  > **Subject:** Attendance rule, is exactly 75% short or not? Blocking 14 students
  >
  > I need one clarification: should a student at exactly 75% attendance be
  > flagged as short, or not? Fourteen students in CS-201 are affected and they
  > are currently being flagged.
  >
  > The written rule says "below 75%", which would mean exactly 75% is fine. The
  > code uses "75% or below", which flags them. One of the two is wrong and I do
  > not know which.
  >
  > If the written rule is correct I can change it today. If the current
  > behaviour is what the university actually wants, I will update the policy
  > wording instead so they match.

  What changed: the question is now the first sentence, the impact is in the
  subject line, and the reader is given two outcomes rather than being asked to
  "guide". Note it also does not assume the code is the bug, which the original
  quietly did.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why does offering two options get a faster reply than asking an open question?
2. **An interviewer asks: "how would you tell a client that a deadline will
   slip?"** Use the shape from this lesson.

## The interview question this answers

**"How do you communicate with stakeholders?"**

The word "stakeholder" invites a vague answer. Make it concrete: the ask in the
first line, options rather than open questions, a date with a reason attached,
and bad news early. Then give the deadline example: what is done, what is not,
what caused it, and the choice between a smaller thing on time and the whole
thing late. That is a real answer and almost nobody gives it.
