There is no problem, and you still have to write something. This sounds like the
least important message in the job and it is the one that quietly decides whether
anybody believes your dates.

## Why this matters in a job

Because trust in this industry is built almost entirely out of small, boring,
accurate updates. Nobody is assessing whether you are brilliant. They are
assessing whether what you said last Tuesday turned out to be true.

::real-life{title="What being trusted actually looked like" source="A team I was on, year three"}
There were two of us at the same level. One was a better programmer than me by a
clear distance. He was given less responsibility, and it took me a year to
understand why: nobody knew where he was until he was finished. He was never
late, and he was always a surprise. Surprise is the thing a manager is actually
trying to eliminate.
::

## The daily update

Three lines. That is the whole format, and the third one is the only one anybody
reads carefully.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Stand-up, a chat message, an email. Same three lines."}
  :::flow-step{label="What I finished" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Finished, not "worked on". If it is not finished, it belongs on the next line.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="What I am on now" icon="i-lucide-play"}
  One thing. If it is three things, say the one you will actually be doing this
  morning.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="What is in my way" icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle" highlight}
  The only line with any information in it. "Nothing" is a valid and common
  answer, and saying it plainly is what makes the other days believable.
  :::
::

::compare{caption="Same morning, same work."}
  :::compare-side{label="What people say" verdict="wrong"}
  > Yesterday I was working on the shortlisting. Today I will continue on the
  > shortlisting. No blockers.

  Said every day for four days. Nobody knows anything. On day five it is not
  done and everybody is surprised.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What to say" verdict="right"}
  > Yesterday: the shortlisting query is done and tested, including the
  > withdrawn-applicant case.
  >
  > Today: the email that goes out to shortlisted applicants.
  >
  > Blocked on one thing: I do not have the wording, and I do not think I should
  > write it. Who owns that text?

  Forty seconds, and now somebody can unblock you before lunch.
  :::
::

## Saying you are stuck

The hardest message a junior sends, and the one they delay longest. I once sat on
a problem for three days because asking felt like an admission.

The fix is a format. A stuck message has four parts, and the fourth is what makes
it a professional message rather than a cry for help.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-target" title="What I am trying to do"}
  One sentence, at the level of the goal rather than the code. "Send an email to
  shortlisted applicants."
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-list-checks" title="What I have already tried"}
  The list. This is what proves you are stuck rather than lazy, and it stops the
  helper suggesting the first three things you already did.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="What is actually happening"}
  The exact error, the exact behaviour. Same discipline as a bug report.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-clock" title="How long I have been on it"}
  The part nobody includes. "Two hours" tells the reader how to weigh it, and it
  gives you permission to ask, because two hours is a reasonable amount of time
  to have spent.
  :::
::

::pull-quote
Sitting on a problem is not perseverance. It is spending the team's money on your
discomfort.
::

## The rule that makes this easy

Agree a number with yourself, in advance. Mine is **forty-five minutes**: if I
have made no progress at all in forty-five minutes, I write the four-part
message. Not because forty-five minutes is special, but because deciding in
advance removes the decision at the moment you are least able to make it.

::tip
Ask in a shared channel rather than a direct message, unless it is sensitive.
Three reasons: somebody other than the person you would have asked usually
answers faster, the answer is findable by the next person, and it demonstrates
that you are working rather than quiet.
::

## What confuses people here

That frequent updates make you look slow. They make you look legible, which is a
different thing and a more valuable one. The engineer whose work is invisible
until it appears is not trusted with anything urgent, because nobody can plan
around them.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
The one update that genuinely damages you is the late one about a date. If
Thursday is not going to happen, Monday is when to say so. Wednesday evening is
when it becomes everybody else's problem too, and that is the message people
remember.
::

## What you can do now

You can make your work visible in forty seconds a day, and you have a format and
a timer for the message you would otherwise avoid sending for three days.

## Your turn

Write the stuck message. You are two hours into trying to send the shortlisting
email. The mail library throws `AuthenticationFailedException`. You have checked
the username and password in the config, tried the same credentials in a mail
client successfully, and read the library's documentation page on authentication.

Four parts. Six lines maximum.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="One version" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  > Stuck on sending the shortlisting email, about two hours in.
  >
  > **Trying to:** send mail through our SMTP server from `EmailService`.
  >
  > **Getting:** `javax.mail.AuthenticationFailedException: 535 5.7.8 Username
  > and Password not accepted` on every attempt.
  >
  > **Tried:** the same username and password work in a normal mail client;
  > checked they match `application.properties` exactly including no trailing
  > space; read the library's authentication page and added
  > `mail.smtp.auth=true`, no change.
  >
  > **Guess:** the server may require an app-specific password rather than the
  > account one, but I do not have access to create one. Who does?

  The last line is what turns a request for help into a request for a specific
  decision, which is far easier for a busy person to answer.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Which of the three daily lines carries the information, and why is "nothing"
   a useful answer to it?
2. **An interviewer asks: "what do you do when you are stuck?"** Answer with a
   number in it.

## The interview question this answers

**"What do you do when you get stuck?"**

Everyone says "I try to solve it myself first, then I ask". That is the right
answer and it is unmemorable without a number and a format. "I give it about
forty-five minutes of real progress. If I have none, I write what I am trying to
do, what I have tried, the exact error and how long I have been on it, and I ask
in the team channel rather than privately so the answer is findable." That
describes a person who has actually worked somewhere.
