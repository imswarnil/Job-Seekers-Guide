The part people call unfair. A word you have never used, four options, forty
seconds. Memorising a thousand-word list is the standard advice and it is a bad
use of a month, because the papers do not draw from one list.

There is a better way in, and it has two halves: build the word from its parts,
and eliminate rather than choose.

## Why this matters in a job

The vocabulary half, honestly: mostly for the round. The reasoning half transfers
directly, because "arrange these five sentences into a coherent paragraph" is
exactly what you do when you write a bug report or explain a fault to a manager.

## Building a word you have never seen

English technical vocabulary is assembled from a small number of parts. You
already know most of them from computing.

| Part | Means | You already know it from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `de-` | down, away, reverse | `deprecate`, `decrement`, `deploy` |
| `re-` | again, back | `refactor`, `retry`, `revert` |
| `in-` / `im-` | not, or into | `invalid`, `immutable` |
| `sub-` | under | `subclass`, `subquery`, `subnet` |
| `super-` | above | `superclass`, `supervisor` |
| `inter-` | between | `interface`, `internet`, `interpreter` |
| `trans-` | across | `transaction`, `transfer`, `translate` |
| `-able` | capable of being | `readable`, `scalable` |
| `-ous` | full of | `ambiguous`, `erroneous` |
| `-ate` | to make | `validate`, `allocate` |
| `syn-` / `sym-` | together | `synchronise`, `symmetric` |
| `mal-` | bad | `malformed`, `malware` |

Now try one you probably have not used. **Intransigent.**

`in-` is "not". `trans-` is "across". The middle is from a root meaning "to go
over, to come to an agreement". So: not able to be moved across. Unwilling to
compromise.

That is not a trick. That is how most of the words in these papers are built, and
thirty minutes on the table above outperforms a week on a word list.

## The elimination method

You almost never need to know the right answer. You need to remove three wrong
ones, and wrong options in these papers are wrong in predictable ways.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Four options, forty seconds. Never start by looking for the right one."}
  :::flow-step{label="Remove the opposite" icon="i-lucide-x"}
  One option is usually the antonym. It is there to catch somebody who read the
  question too fast, and it is the easiest mark on the paper.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Remove the wrong part of speech" icon="i-lucide-x"}
  If the blank needs a verb and an option is a noun, it is gone regardless of
  meaning.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Remove the wrong register" icon="i-lucide-x"}
  A formal passage will not take a casual word. This removes one more, reliably.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Choose between the two that are left" icon="i-lucide-check" highlight}
  You are now at fifty per cent with no vocabulary at all, and usually the
  distinction between the last two is one you can feel.
  :::
::

## Sentence ordering, which is the one that transfers

Five sentences, scrambled, arrange them. This looks like a puzzle and it is
actually a test of whether you can see the skeleton of an argument.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-shuffle" color="neutral"}
**Arrange these.**

A. However, the aptitude component proved a better predictor of first-year
   results.
B. The university shortlisted applicants on qualifying marks alone until 2019.
C. It therefore now carries thirty per cent of the composite score.
D. This was simple to administer and easy to defend.
E. Internal boards varied by as much as fourteen percentage points, so the marks
   were not comparable.
::

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="The order, and how to find it without reading them all carefully" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **B, D, E, A, C.**

  You find it by looking at the joining words, not the content.

  - **B** has no back-reference at all. Nothing in it points at an earlier
    sentence, so it is the opening. There is usually exactly one of these.
  - **D** starts with "This", so it must follow something. It refers to the old
    system, so it follows B.
  - **E** starts with the problem and ends "so the marks were not comparable",
    which contradicts D's "easy to defend". A contradiction follows the claim it
    contradicts.
  - **A** starts with "However", which is the strongest signal on the page: it
    must follow something it is turning against.
  - **C** starts with "It therefore", which is a conclusion. Conclusions go last.

  The technique: find the sentence with no back-reference, then chain on the
  connecting words — this, however, therefore, so, it. You never have to
  understand the argument.
  :::
::

::real-life{title="Where this is your actual job" source="Writing an incident summary"}
When something has broken and a manager asks what happened, you are doing this
exercise with real sentences: what was true before, what changed, what that
caused, what we did, what we will do. Get the order wrong and the reader has to
reconstruct it, which is how a five-line message becomes a twenty-minute meeting.
::

## What confuses people here

That vocabulary is the same as intelligence. It is not. It is exposure, which is
mostly an accident of which school you went to and what was in your house. It is
also, unlike intelligence, purchasable with a month of deliberate practice, and
this is the good news in the whole lesson.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
I took my failure at this section as information about my ability. It was
information about my exposure. If you take one thing from this track, take that
distinction.
::

## What you can do now

You can attack a word you have never seen from its parts, remove three options
from four without knowing the answer, and order a scrambled paragraph from the
joining words. That is the whole verbal section.

## Your turn

Work out these four from their parts, without looking them up. Write your guess
first, then check.

1. `circumvent` — `circum-` is "around"
2. `retrospective` — `retro-` is "backward", `spect` is "look"
3. `malleable` — `mal` here is from a root meaning "hammer", `-able` is "able to be"
4. `incontrovertible` — `in-` not, `contro-` against, `vert` turn

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answers" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  1. To go around, to avoid a rule or obstacle.
  2. Looking backwards. You will attend one every two weeks in a real job.
  3. Able to be hammered into shape. Figuratively, easily influenced.
  4. Not able to be turned against. Impossible to dispute.

  Note that you got all four without ever having seen them, which is the point of
  the lesson.

## The interview question this answers

Not a question, a habit. Every technical interview involves explaining something
in order, and the sentence-ordering skill is that skill. Candidates who answer
"what happened with that bug?" in the order they *discovered* things sound
confused; candidates who answer in the order the reader needs sound senior.
