A passage of text, six questions about it, and not enough time. That is what
eliminated me, and the reason it eliminated me is that I read it the way I was
taught to read in school: start at the top, understand everything, then answer.

That approach cannot finish. The round is not testing whether you understand the
passage. It is testing whether you can **find a specific fact in unfamiliar text
under time pressure**, which is a different skill and a trainable one.

## Why this matters in a job

Because the skill transfers exactly. On a Tuesday somebody sends you eleven pages
of requirements and asks whether the system handles case X. You are not going to
read eleven pages. You are going to locate the answer.

::real-life{title="The same skill, three years later" source="A Salesforce implementation"}
A client sent a 40-page process document and asked whether our system could
support their approval chain. Reading it front to back would have taken two
hours. Scanning for the four words that mattered — "approval", "escalate",
"delegate", "reject" — took eleven minutes and produced a better answer, because
I could quote the four places the document contradicted itself.
::

## The technique

Four steps, always in this order. The order is the whole thing.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Never read the passage first. This is the single change that fixes most people's score."}
  :::flow-step{label="Read the questions first" icon="i-lucide-list"}
  Thirty seconds. You are loading what to look for. Now every sentence of the
  passage is being scanned against six specific targets instead of being
  understood in general.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Skim for structure, not meaning" icon="i-lucide-layout-list"}
  Forty seconds. First sentence of each paragraph only. You are building a map:
  paragraph two is the objection, paragraph four is the data.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Answer the locate-a-fact questions" icon="i-lucide-search" highlight}
  Usually four of the six. Go to the paragraph your map points at, read those
  three sentences properly, answer, move on. These are free marks and most people
  run out of time before reaching them.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Then the inference questions" icon="i-lucide-brain"}
  "The author would most likely agree that…" These need the whole shape, which
  you now have, and they are where the remaining time should go.
  :::
::

## Watch it happen

Here is a passage of the kind these rounds use. Read the questions below it
first, as the technique says. Time yourself: eight minutes for all six.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-file-text" color="neutral"}
**Passage**

In 2019 the university replaced its admissions cut-off with a composite score.
Previously an applicant was shortlisted on marks alone; a candidate scoring 78
was admitted and one scoring 77 was not, regardless of anything else. The new
formula weights the qualifying examination at 60 per cent, an aptitude test at 30
per cent and an interview at 10 per cent.

The registrar's office defended the change on two grounds. First, that marks
alone rewarded students from schools with generous internal assessment, which
varied by as much as fourteen percentage points between boards. Second, that the
aptitude component predicted first-year performance more reliably than the
qualifying marks did, a finding drawn from four years of internal data.

Critics within the faculty were unconvinced. Professor Iyer of the mathematics
department argued that the interview component, though small, introduced a
subjective element that could not be audited, and that a 10 per cent weight was
sufficient to move a borderline candidate across the line. He noted that no
record of interview reasoning was retained beyond a single numerical score.

The office has since agreed to publish interview rubrics, though not the
individual scores. Admissions in the first year under the new formula rose 4 per
cent in applications from rural districts and fell 2 per cent overall.
::

**The questions.** Read these before you go back to the passage.

1. What percentage weight does the aptitude test carry?
2. What variation between boards does the second paragraph cite?
3. What was Professor Iyer's specific objection to the interview component?
4. What happened to overall applications in the first year of the new formula?
5. Which of the following is stated as a *finding from internal data*?
6. The author's tone toward the change is best described as…

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answers, and which paragraph each one lived in" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **1. 30 per cent.** Paragraph one, last sentence. A locate-a-fact question. Two
  seconds if your map said "paragraph one is the formula".

  **2. Fourteen percentage points.** Paragraph two. Locate-a-fact.

  **3. That it is subjective and cannot be audited, and that 10 per cent is
  enough to move a borderline candidate.** Paragraph three. Note the trap: a
  careless reader answers "he thought interviews were unfair", which is not what
  the passage says and is the wrong option in every version of this question.

  **4. They fell 2 per cent.** Paragraph four, last sentence. The trap here is
  the 4 per cent rise, which is about rural districts only. Mixing those two is
  the most common error on this passage type.

  **5. That the aptitude component predicted first-year performance more
  reliably.** Paragraph two says "a finding drawn from four years of internal
  data". Everything else in the passage is an argument or a policy, not a
  finding. This question is really asking: can you tell a claim from evidence?

  **6. Neutral or balanced.** The passage presents the office's case and the
  critics' case and endorses neither. Tone questions almost always resolve to
  neutral in these tests, because the passages are written to be unarguable.
  :::
::

## What confuses people here

**The trap is always a true sentence that does not answer the question.** Look at
question four again. "Applications from rural districts rose 4 per cent" is
factually in the passage. It is the wrong answer, and it is wrong in a way that
feels right, which is exactly why it is there.

::compare{caption="The same question, two readers."}
  :::compare-side{label="Reads for meaning" verdict="wrong"}
  Absorbs the whole passage, forms an impression that the policy broadly worked,
  and answers question four from the impression. Picks the 4 per cent.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Reads for the target" verdict="right"}
  Has the word "overall" loaded from the question, scans paragraph four, finds
  "fell 2 per cent overall", answers, moves on. Eleven seconds, no impression
  formed at all.
  :::
::

## What you can do now

You have the technique. It is worth roughly fifteen to twenty marks in a round
that eliminates two thirds of applicants, and it costs an hour a week for a
month to make automatic.

## Your turn

Find one real comprehension passage. Any aptitude preparation site, any previous
paper, anything. Do it twice:

- **Once the way you normally read.** Time it.
- **Once with the technique.** Questions first, skim for structure, facts, then
  inference. Time it.

Write both numbers down. The gap is the exercise.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Do not skip the timing. Everybody believes they already read efficiently, and
almost nobody does under pressure. The number is the only thing that will
convince you, and it convinced me about four years too late.
::

## Check yourself

1. Why does reading the questions before the passage improve your score, rather
   than just changing the order?
2. **In the passage above, what is the difference between a claim and a finding,
   and where does the passage mark it?**

## The interview question this answers

There is no interview question for this one, and pretending otherwise would be
dishonest. This lesson is about a gate, not about the job. Its value is that it
gets you into the room where the interview questions happen, and for me that gate
was closed for four years.
