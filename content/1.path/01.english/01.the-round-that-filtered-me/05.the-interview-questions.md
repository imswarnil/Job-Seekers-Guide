Nobody will ask you to define a dangling modifier. They will ask you things that
are really about whether you can be put in front of a client, and those questions
look harmless.

## The six

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. Your English is not very strong. Is that going to be a problem?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  **Asked more often than anyone admits**, usually more politely than this, and it
  is a real question about client-facing work rather than an insult.

  **What does not work:** apologising, or over-promising. "Sorry sir, I will
  improve" concedes the point and gives them nothing.

  **What works:** separate fluency from clarity, and give evidence of the second.
  *"My grammar is not native and I know it. What I can do is explain a technical
  problem so the person I am talking to can act on it. Can I show you? Ask me
  about the bug I fixed in my admissions project."* Then do it in ninety seconds.

  The demonstration is the answer. Nobody can argue with a clear explanation
  delivered in front of them.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="2. Explain a technical concept to a non-technical person." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  **What is being tested:** whether you can drop jargon without dropping
  accuracy. It is a proxy for "can we send this person to a client meeting".

  **What does not work:** an analogy that breaks. "A database is like a cupboard"
  gets you three sentences before it fails.

  **What works:** one concrete example from a domain the listener already has.
  *"Say the university keeps one register for applicants and another for their
  exam results. A join is the operation that lines those two registers up on the
  applicant's roll number, so you can ask a question that needs both, like which
  shortlisted applicants actually scored above 80. Without it you would be
  flipping between two books by hand."*

  Note it contains no metaphor at all. It contains a smaller version of the real
  thing, which never breaks.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="3. How would you explain a delay to a client?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  **What is being tested:** whether you will hide bad news, which is the single
  most expensive habit a junior can have.

  **What does not work:** "I would work extra hours to make sure there is no
  delay." They asked what you would say, not how you would avoid saying it.

  **What works:** early, specific, with an option. *"As soon as I know, not the
  day before. I would say what is done, what is not, what caused it, and give
  them a choice: the simple version on Friday or the complete version on
  Tuesday. Most of the damage from a delay comes from it being a surprise."*
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="4. Tell me about a time you had to explain something to somebody who disagreed with you." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  **What is being tested:** whether you argue to win.

  **What does not work:** a story where you were right and they came around.
  Everybody tells that story.

  **What works:** a story where you found out what they were actually worried
  about. *"I wanted to add a check that slowed the import. The tester thought it
  was unnecessary. When I asked why, it turned out she had seen the import time
  out before, and her real concern was the timeout, not the check. We added the
  check and moved the import to a background job. I had been arguing about the
  wrong thing for two days."*
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="5. Why does written communication matter for a developer?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  **What is being tested:** whether you understand that code is read more than it
  is written.

  **What works:** the economics. *"Most of the cost of software is after it is
  written. A commit message, a variable name and a bug report are all messages to
  somebody who does not have my context, often me, in six months. If they have
  to reconstruct what I meant, that is the expensive part of the project, not the
  typing."* Then a one-line example: a bug report that says "it does not work"
  versus one that says what you did, what you expected, and what happened.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="6. Tell me about yourself." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  **The most-asked question in hiring**, and the one a friend told me decides most
  of the outcome. Taken apart properly in chapter four of this track and again in
  the interview track.

  **What does not work:** a biography from school onward. They have your CV.

  **What works, in ninety seconds:** where you are now, the decision you made,
  the evidence, and why this role. *"I finished my degree in 2026 and came out of
  it able to pass exams but not able to build anything. So I spent the last year
  learning Java and SQL properly and building an admissions system for a
  university, applications, shortlisting, fees, results, live on its own domain.
  The part I enjoyed most was the data model, which is why I am applying for a
  backend role rather than anything else."*

  Four sentences. Every one of them invites a follow-up you want.
  :::
::

## What every one of these has in common

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-target" title="They are not about English"}
  Every one is really about judgement, honesty or whether you can be trusted with
  a client. Fluency is not what is being marked.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-box" title="They are answered with an example"}
  A claim is worth nothing. "I communicate well" is a claim. The bug you
  explained is evidence.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-clock" title="Ninety seconds, not five minutes"}
  Long answers read as nervousness. Say the thing, stop, let them ask.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-repeat" title="They are rehearsable"}
  Out loud, to a wall, until it stops sounding rehearsed. This feels ridiculous
  and it is the highest-return hour in interview preparation.
  :::
::

Record yourself answering question six. Ninety seconds. Play it back.

You will hate it. Do it four more times. By the fifth you will have a version you
can use for the next two years, and it is the single most valuable artefact in
this entire track.
