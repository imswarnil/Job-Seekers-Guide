Agile is the idea: deliver working software in small pieces and let the plan
change. Scrum is the most common way teams turn that idea into a calendar, and
it comes with a vocabulary you will be using by the end of your first week at
work.

## Why this matters in a job

Your first team will almost certainly run Scrum, or something that borrows its
words. You will be in a stand-up on day two. You will be asked "how many points
is this?" in your first planning meeting, and "what went well?" in your first
retrospective. Knowing the words before you arrive is the difference between
nodding along and taking part.

In interviews: "What is Agile?", "What is Scrum?", "Who is a Scrum Master?",
"What happens in sprint planning?", "What is a story point?" They are asked of
developers and testers alike.

## Agile, then Scrum

In 2001, seventeen developers wrote the **Agile Manifesto**: four values, each
one preferring the left over the right, without throwing the right away.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-users" title="Individuals and interactions"}
  over processes and tools.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-monitor-check" title="Working software"}
  over comprehensive documentation.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-handshake" title="Customer collaboration"}
  over contract negotiation.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-refresh-cw" title="Responding to change"}
  over following a plan.
  :::
::

**Scrum** is a framework built on those values. An analogy: a college fest
committee. Somebody decides what the fest must have (the priority list), a
small team of volunteers builds it in two-week bursts, and a coordinator makes
sure nothing blocks them. Each fortnight the committee shows the principal what
is ready and adjusts the plan.

## How Scrum works

### Three roles

| Role | Who | Owns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Product Owner** (PO) | Usually from the client or business side | The **product backlog** and its order. Decides *what* is built and *why*. |
| **Scrum Master** (SM) | A facilitator, not a manager | The process. Runs the events, removes blockers, protects the team from interruptions. |
| **Development team** | Developers, testers, designers, typically 3–9 people | *How* the work is done, and delivering it within the sprint. |

### Three artefacts

- **Product backlog**: every piece of wanted work, ordered by value.
- **Sprint backlog**: the items the team committed to this sprint, plus the plan
  to finish them.
- **Increment**: the working, tested software at the end of the sprint.

Work items are usually written as **user stories**, in one sentence:

```text [A user story]
As a  student,
I want to pay my semester fee by UPI,
so that I do not have to queue at the accounts office.

Acceptance criteria:
  - A successful payment shows a receipt with a receipt number
  - A failed payment shows the reason and does not mark the fee paid
  - The accounts team sees the payment in their report within 5 minutes
```

### Five events

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="One sprint, usually two weeks. The events repeat every sprint."}
  :::flow-step{label="Sprint planning" icon="i-lucide-calendar"}
  Start of the sprint, 2–4 hours. The team picks items from the top of the
  product backlog and agrees a **sprint goal**.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The sprint" icon="i-lucide-timer" highlight}
  A fixed time box of 1–4 weeks (two is most common). Scope is not changed
  mid-sprint without the team agreeing.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Daily stand-up" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  15 minutes, every day, same time. What I did yesterday, what I will do today,
  anything blocking me.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Sprint review" icon="i-lucide-presentation"}
  End of sprint. The team demonstrates the increment to the PO and stakeholders.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Retrospective" icon="i-lucide-rotate-ccw"}
  After the review. What went well, what did not, what we will change next
  sprint.
  :::
::

A sixth activity, **backlog refinement** (or grooming), happens during the
sprint: the PO and team clarify and estimate upcoming stories so the next
planning meeting is quick.

### Story points and velocity

A **story point** is a unit of *relative effort*, not hours. The team agrees
that "add a receipt number to the fee receipt" is a 1, and sizes everything else
against it, usually on the Fibonacci-like scale 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13. Estimation is
often done with **planning poker**: everyone shows a card at once, so nobody
anchors on the senior's number.

**Velocity** is the points a team finishes per sprint, averaged over the last
few sprints. It is used to forecast, never to compare two teams.

```text [Velocity worked example]
Last three sprints finished: 21, 18, 24 points
Average velocity           : (21 + 18 + 24) / 3 = 21 points
Backlog left               : 105 points
Sprints remaining          : 105 / 21 = 5 sprints, about 10 weeks
```

## Real use case

A **burndown chart** is the picture every Scrum Master shows: points remaining
on the vertical axis, days of the sprint on the horizontal. A line that goes
flat for three days is a stand-up conversation waiting to happen.

::real-life{title="What a story point is really for" source="Twilio, the GTM team"}
Points are a conversation tool. When one person says 2 and another says 13 for
the same story, one of them knows something the other does not: an old API, a
missing permission, a report nobody has looked at. The number matters less
than that conversation. That is the answer I would give an interviewer who asks
why we do not estimate in hours.
::

## What confuses people here

- **The Scrum Master is not the team's boss.** They serve the team. The PO does
  not manage people either. A Scrum team has no "manager" role in the framework.
- **Agile does not mean no documentation.** It means *working software over*
  documentation, not instead of it.
- **Story points are not hours.** "One point is four hours" defeats the point of
  relative sizing.
- **Scrum versus Kanban.** Kanban has no sprints and no fixed roles: work flows
  across a board (To do, In progress, Done) with a limit on how many items can be
  in progress at once. Support teams often prefer it.

## Interview questions

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What is Agile? Is Scrum the same thing?"}
  Agile is a set of values and principles for building software in small,
  frequent, working increments and welcoming change. Scrum is one framework that
  implements it, with defined roles, events and artefacts. Kanban and XP are
  others.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="What are the roles in Scrum?"}
  Product Owner (owns and orders the backlog, decides what), Scrum Master
  (facilitates, removes blockers, coaches the process), and the development
  team (decides how and delivers the increment). No project manager.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="What happens in a daily stand-up?"}
  Fifteen minutes, standing, same time daily. Each person says what they did
  yesterday towards the sprint goal, what they will do today, and what is
  blocking them. Problem-solving is taken offline afterwards so it stays short.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="What is a story point, and what is velocity?"}
  A story point is a relative estimate of effort, complexity and uncertainty,
  usually on 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13. Velocity is the average number of points a team
  completes per sprint, used to forecast. Example: 21, 18, 24 average to 21, so
  105 remaining points is about five sprints.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="Sprint review versus retrospective?"}
  The review is about the **product**: demo the increment to stakeholders and
  gather feedback. The retrospective is about the **process**: the team alone
  discusses how it worked and what to change.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="What is the Definition of Done?"}
  A shared checklist every item must meet to count as finished: code reviewed,
  tests written and passing, deployed to the test environment, acceptance
  criteria met. Without it, "done" means something different to everybody.
  :::
::
