The screen works: a click changes the page, the page talks to the user. But a
real product is hundreds of those screens built by twenty people over a year,
and somebody has to decide what gets built first. That decision has a name, and
it is the first "other subject" an interviewer reaches for.

## Why this matters in a job

In a service company (Accenture, TCS, Infosys, Wipro, Cognizant) your project is
run to a model, and the model decides your week: whether you get requirements in
one fat document or in small pieces every fortnight, whether testing happens at
the end or all the time, and how often a client sees your work.

In interviews it is the classic fresher question. "What is SDLC?", "Explain the
Waterfall model", "Waterfall versus Agile". A fresher who can say *when* each
model fits, rather than reciting its phases, stands out immediately.

## The life cycle, phase by phase

**SDLC** stands for **Software Development Life Cycle**: the stages every piece
of software passes through, from "somebody wants something" to "it is running
and being looked after". Think of building a college hostel. Somebody decides
how many rooms are needed, an architect draws it, builders build it, an
inspector checks it, students move in, and the plumbing gets fixed for the next
thirty years. Software is the same six steps.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="The six phases. Every model uses all six; they differ in how often they go round."}
  :::flow-step{label="Requirements" icon="i-lucide-clipboard-list"}
  What should it do? The registrar wants students to pay fees online and see a
  receipt. Written down in an **SRS** (Software Requirements Specification).
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Design" icon="i-lucide-pencil-ruler"}
  How will it do it? Screens, tables, which service talks to which.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Implementation" icon="i-lucide-code" highlight}
  The code. The part you trained for, and the smallest share of the time.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Testing" icon="i-lucide-bug"}
  Does it do what the requirements said, and nothing it should not?
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Deployment" icon="i-lucide-rocket"}
  Put it where real users can reach it.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Maintenance" icon="i-lucide-wrench"}
  Fix bugs, adapt to change, add features. Most of a system's life is here.
  :::
::

## How the five models work

A **model** is a decision about how to walk through those six phases: once, in
order, or many times in small loops.

| Model | How it moves | Fits when | Breaks when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Waterfall** | Each phase once, in order, no going back | Requirements are fixed and well understood (a government exam portal with rules set by law) | The client changes their mind after design is signed |
| **V-model** | Waterfall, but every build phase has a matching test phase planned at the same time | Safety-critical work where every requirement must be proved tested (medical, banking) | Same as Waterfall: change is expensive |
| **Iterative** | Build a rough version of the whole, then improve it in rounds | The big picture is known but details are not | Nobody controls how many rounds there are |
| **Spiral** | Iterative, with an explicit **risk analysis** at the start of every loop | Large, expensive, risky projects | Small projects: the risk analysis costs more than the build |
| **Agile** | Small working pieces every one to four weeks, with the client reviewing each | Requirements will change, and the business wants something usable early | The client cannot give time every fortnight, or the contract fixes scope and price |

### The V-model, drawn

The V is the one people struggle to picture. The left arm goes down through the
build phases, the right arm comes back up through the test phases, and each pair
faces its partner across the V.

::compare{caption="Each test level is designed at the same time as the phase opposite it."}
  :::compare-side{label="Left arm: verification (planning)" verdict="neutral"}
  Requirements, then system design, then architecture, then module design, then
  code at the bottom of the V.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Right arm: validation (testing)" verdict="neutral"}
  Unit testing, then integration testing, then system testing, then acceptance
  testing at the top, checked against the requirements.
  :::
::

**Verification** asks "are we building the product right?" (reviews,
walkthroughs, no code runs). **Validation** asks "are we building the right
product?" (the code runs and is tested). That pair is an interview question on
its own.

### A worked comparison

The same request, an online fee payment page for a university, run two ways:

```text [Waterfall timeline]
Month 1     Requirements: 40-page SRS signed by the registrar
Month 2     Design: every screen and table drawn
Month 3-4   Build
Month 5     Test
Month 6     Go live. Registrar sees it for the first time.
            "We also need instalments." Back to month 1.
```

```text [Agile timeline]
Weeks 1-2   Pay full fee by UPI, get a receipt. Registrar reviews it.
Weeks 3-4   Instalments added (the registrar asked in week 2).
Weeks 5-6   Late-fee rule and an admin report.
Weeks 7-8   Scholarship discounts. Live since week 4.
```

::pros-cons
---
title: Waterfall, honestly
pros:
  - Easy to plan, price and staff, which is why fixed-price contracts like it
  - Every phase leaves documentation behind
  - Works well when the rules truly cannot change
cons:
  - The client sees nothing working until the end
  - A mistake in requirements is found in testing, when it is most expensive
  - Change is treated as a failure rather than as normal
---
::

## Real use case

At Accenture my Salesforce project called itself Agile and ran in sprints. But
the contract with the client had a fixed list of features and a fixed date,
which is a Waterfall idea. Most real projects are a mix like this, and saying so
in an interview is a sign you understand the models rather than having memorised
them.

::real-life{title="What the model decides for you" source="A service-company project"}
On a Waterfall project a fresher often spends the first month reading the SRS
and design documents before writing any code. On an Agile project you are given
a small story in week one and expected to finish it inside the sprint. Neither
is better for you. Ask in the first week which one you are on, because it
changes what "doing well" means.
::

## What confuses people here

- **"Agile is a model like Waterfall."** Partly. Agile is a set of values (the
  Agile Manifesto, 2001). Scrum and Kanban are the frameworks teams actually
  follow. The next lesson separates them.
- **Iterative and incremental.** *Incremental* adds new pieces (fees, then
  hostel, then library). *Iterative* improves the same piece in rounds (a rough
  fee page, then a better one). Agile does both.
- **Spiral is not "Agile with a different name".** Its defining feature is risk
  analysis every loop, and it was built for big, expensive projects.
- **Maintenance is not the boring leftover.** It is usually 60% or more of a
  system's total cost.

## Interview questions

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What is SDLC? Name its phases."}
  The Software Development Life Cycle: the stages software goes through from idea
  to retirement. Requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment,
  maintenance. Add one line on why it exists: it makes a large project
  plannable and makes sure testing and maintenance are not forgotten.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="Waterfall versus Agile: which is better?"}
  Neither in general. Waterfall fits fixed, well-understood requirements and
  fixed-price contracts. Agile fits changing requirements and a client who can
  review work every couple of weeks. Give one example of each (an exam portal
  with rules set by law; a start-up's student app).
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="What is the V-model, and how is it different from Waterfall?"}
  Waterfall with testing planned in parallel. Each development phase has a
  matching test level designed at the same time: requirements with acceptance
  testing, system design with system testing, architecture with integration
  testing, module design with unit testing. Defects in the plan are caught
  earlier.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="What is the difference between verification and validation?"}
  Verification: are we building the product right? Reviews and inspections, no
  code runs. Validation: are we building the right product? The software runs
  and is tested against what the user needs.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="When would you choose the spiral model?"}
  For a large, high-risk project where the cost of getting it wrong is severe,
  because every loop starts with identifying and reducing risk. Not for a small
  project, where that analysis costs more than it saves.
  :::
::
