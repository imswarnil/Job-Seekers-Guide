Three lessons on charts. These six are all drawn from real data, none of them
contains a false number, and every one of them misleads.

::pull-quote
A chart does not have to contain a wrong number to be a lie. Most of the
misleading charts you will ever see are arithmetically perfect.
::

## 1. The axis that starts at 68

Attendance across four departments: 71%, 73%, 74%, 76%. The bar chart's y-axis
runs from 68 to 78, and Computer Science looks three times better than
Electronics.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answer" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  A **bar** chart encodes value as length, so the length must be proportional to
  the value. Starting the axis at 68 makes a 5-point difference look like a
  3:1 ratio.

  The fix is either honest option:

  - Start the bar axis at **zero**, and accept that the bars look similar,
    because they are similar.
  - Use a **dot plot** on a zoomed axis. Position, unlike length, does not imply
    proportion, so a truncated axis is legitimate.

  The rule: **bars start at zero. Lines and dots need not.** A line chart of
  temperature starting at zero would be useless, and nobody reads a line's
  distance from the bottom as a quantity.
  :::
::

## 2. The pie with eleven slices

Applications by source: website, agent, walk-in, referral, school visit, and
seven more, as a pie.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answer" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Nobody can compare eleven angles, and the small slices are unreadable and
  unlabellable.

  ```text
  A sorted horizontal bar chart:
    Website     ████████████████████  48%
    Agent       ██████████            24%
    Walk-in     ██████                14%
    Referral    ███                    7%
    Other (8)   ███                    7%
  ```

  Sorted by size, labelled with the number, and the tail grouped into "other"
  with the count in the label so nothing is hidden.

  A pie is defensible for two or three parts of a whole where the point is "this
  is about half". Beyond that it is decoration that prevents comparison.
  :::
::

## 3. The two lines on one axis

Fee collection in crores and application count, plotted together with two
different y-axes. They appear to move together.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answer" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  A dual axis lets you produce any apparent relationship you like by choosing
  the two scales, and the reader cannot see that choice.

  Two honest alternatives:

  - **Two charts stacked**, sharing the x-axis. The reader compares shapes and
    no scale is being implied.
  - **Index both to 100** at the start of the period, on one axis. Now the chart
    is explicitly about relative change and says so.

  If somebody insists on a dual axis, that is usually a sign the real question
  is "are these related", and the answer to that is a scatter plot, not a line
  chart.
  :::
::

## 4. The trend from three points

Electronics applications: 900, 860, 800. The chart has a trend line extended to
2028, showing the department reaching zero.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answer" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Three points is not a trend, and the extrapolation is presented with exactly
  the same visual authority as the measured data.

  Two rules for this:

  - **Draw measured data solid, projections dashed**, and say so in the legend.
  - **Do not project further than you have measured.** Three years of data does
    not support a two-year projection.

  And the honest sentence to put on the chart: "three years of data; the change
  may be capacity rather than demand." Whoever reads it will ask that question
  anyway, and answering it first is what makes the chart trustworthy.
  :::
::

## 5. The correlation that reads as a cause

A scatter of attendance against average marks, with a fitted line, titled
"Attendance improves results".

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answer" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  The chart shows that students who attend more have higher marks. The title
  claims attending causes it, and the data cannot support that.

  The obvious confounder: a student who is ill, working nights, or struggling
  attends less **and** scores less, and the cause is the third thing.

  The fix is the title. "Attendance and results move together" describes what is
  drawn. Then a note: "this does not establish a cause; students facing
  difficulty tend to have both."

  Why this matters beyond honesty: the university will act on this chart. Under
  the first title they enforce attendance. Under the second they ask which
  students are struggling and why, which is a different and better intervention.
  :::
::

## 6. The percentage with no denominator

A card on the dashboard: **"Shortlist rate: 42%"**.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Answer" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Forty-two percent of what, over what period, at what stage?

  Of all applications, or of those reviewed? This intake or all time? Including
  the ones still pending?

  ```text
  Shortlist rate
  42%
  173 of 412 reviewed · June intake · 188 still pending
  ```

  Three extra numbers and the figure can no longer start an argument between two
  correct reports.

  This is the single most valuable habit on a dashboard and it costs one line.
  Every number that is a proportion states what it is a proportion of.
  :::
::

## The checklist before shipping a chart

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-bar-chart" title="Does the encoding match the claim?"}
  Length implies proportion, so bars start at zero. Position does not, so dots
  and lines may be zoomed. Angle is hard to compare, so pies stay at two or
  three slices.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-type" title="Does the title describe or claim?"}
  "Attendance and results move together" describes. "Attendance improves
  results" claims something the data cannot show.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-divide" title="Does every percentage say of what?"}
  A proportion with no denominator is the most common misleading figure on any
  dashboard.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-eye" title="Does it work without colour?"}
  Print it in grey. If the meaning disappears, colour was carrying it, and about
  one man in twelve cannot read it.
  :::
::
