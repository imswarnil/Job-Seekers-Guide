Charts come up in front-end, data and analytics interviews, and the questions are
rarely about a library.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. How would you visualise this data?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Start with the question, not the chart: what does the reader want to know, and
  what will they do differently depending on the answer. "Applications per
  programme" is a comparison and therefore a sorted bar chart; "applications per
  week" is a change over time and therefore a line.

  Then name the two decisions that are usually made by accident: where the axis
  starts, and whether you are counting applications or applicants, which differ
  by twenty-five per cent in this data.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="2. Why not a pie chart?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Because the encoding is angle, and humans compare angles badly. Past about
  four slices nobody can rank them and two similar slices are
  indistinguishable. A sorted bar answers the same question instantly and needs
  no legend.

  The honest exception, which is worth giving: two or three parts of an obvious
  whole, where "roughly half" is the entire message.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="3. Should a chart's axis start at zero?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  A **bar** chart must, because the bar's *length* is the encoding and a
  truncated bar encodes a false length. A **line** chart need not, because the
  encoding is the slope, and a temperature chart starting at zero is unreadable.

  Then the practical edge: charting libraries pick the truncated axis by default
  because it looks better, so the baseline is a decision you make on every bar
  chart and nobody will tell you that you made it wrongly.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="4. How do you make a chart accessible?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Colour is never the only signal, because about one man in twelve cannot
  separate red from green: add a label, a shape or a position. Label directly
  rather than with a legend, which is a lookup table you are asking the reader
  to memorise.

  And the part most people omit: the underlying numbers must be available as
  text. Either the `alt` carries them, or there is a table on the page and the
  chart is decorative, which makes `alt=""` correct.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="5. The numbers on this chart do not match the report. Why?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  The best question in this track, because it is not about charts at all. Every
  chart is a query, and the two queries disagree about what they excluded:
  withdrawn applications, unmarked scores, which intake, and whether the unit is
  an application or an applicant.

  Which is why the caption states it. "Average score 72.4" is a number;
  "72.4, from 1,840 marked applications by 1,602 applicants, excluding
  withdrawals" is a claim somebody can check. Saying that you write the second
  one is the answer.
  :::
::
