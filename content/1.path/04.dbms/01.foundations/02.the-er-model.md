Before any table is created, somebody decides what the data even is: students,
courses, departments, which belongs to which, and how many of each. That decision
is drawn as an **ER diagram** (entity-relationship), and it is the first thing a
design question in an interview asks you to produce.

## Why this matters in a job

Every new feature that stores data starts as a conversation about entities and
relationships, usually on a whiteboard, long before anyone writes `CREATE
TABLE`. In interviews, "draw an ER diagram for a college / library / hospital"
is the classic design question, and the follow-ups are about cardinality ("can a
student have two departments?") and weak entities. Get the relationships right
and the tables almost write themselves.

## Nouns and verbs

Describe the college in one paragraph: "A **student** belongs to a
**department**. A student **enrols** in many **courses**, and each course has
many students. A **faculty** member **teaches** courses. Each student makes
**fee payments**." The nouns are **entities**; the verbs are **relationships**;
the facts about each noun (a student's name, email, date of birth) are
**attributes**. An ER diagram is that paragraph drawn in a standard notation.

| Concept | Meaning | College example | Drawn as (Chen notation) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Entity | A thing with its own identity | One student, Anita Rao | |
| Entity set | All entities of one kind | Student | Rectangle |
| Attribute | A fact about an entity | `email`, `date_of_birth` | Ellipse |
| Key attribute | Identifies the entity | `student_id` | Underlined ellipse |
| Relationship | An association between entities | Anita enrols in CS201 | Diamond |
| Weak entity | Cannot be identified on its own | A fee instalment of a student | Double rectangle |

### Kinds of attribute

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-type" title="Simple vs composite"}
  `email` is simple. `full_name` is composite if you split it into first and
  last name; `address` into street, city, PIN.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-list" title="Single-valued vs multi-valued"}
  A student has one `date_of_birth` but may have several `phone_numbers`
  (double ellipse). Multi-valued attributes become their own table.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-calculator" title="Stored vs derived"}
  `date_of_birth` is stored; `age` is derived from it (dashed ellipse) and
  usually not stored at all.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-key" title="Key attribute"}
  Unique for each entity: `student_id`, or `email`. Underlined.
  :::
::

## How it works: cardinality and participation

**Cardinality** says how many of one entity can relate to how many of the other.
It is the most important thing on the diagram, because it decides where the
foreign keys go.

| Cardinality | Read it as | College example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1:1** | One A to at most one B, and back | A department has one head; a faculty member heads at most one department |
| **1:N** | One A to many B; each B to one A | A department has many students; a student belongs to one department |
| **M:N** | Many A to many B | A student enrols in many courses; a course has many students |

**Participation** says whether every entity must take part:

- **Total** (double line): every student **must** belong to a department.
- **Partial** (single line): not every faculty member heads a department.

::note
The notation varies. Chen notation (rectangles, diamonds, ellipses) is what
Indian university textbooks and most interviewers draw. **Crow's foot** notation
(lines with a three-pronged "foot" for "many") is what design tools and most
companies use. Both say the same things; use whichever the interviewer uses.
::

### Weak entities

A **weak entity** cannot be identified by its own attributes alone. A fee
instalment is "instalment 2" of **some student**: instalment number 2 exists for
hundreds of students, so it needs its owner's key to be unique. Its key is the
owner's key plus its own **partial key** (`instalment_no`), and it always has
total participation in its identifying relationship (double diamond).

## From ER diagram to tables

The mapping rules are mechanical, and interviewers like to hear them stated:

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Turning an ER diagram into a relational schema."}
  :::flow-step{label="Each strong entity becomes a table" icon="i-lucide-table"}
  Its key attribute becomes the primary key. Composite attributes become
  separate columns; derived ones are usually dropped.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="1:N: foreign key on the N side" icon="i-lucide-arrow-right"}
  Student gets `department_id`. The "many" side points at the "one" side.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="M:N: a new junction table" icon="i-lucide-combine" highlight}
  Enrolment(student_id, course_id, grade), primary key the pair. Attributes of
  the relationship (grade) live here.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="1:1: foreign key on either side" icon="i-lucide-equal"}
  Usually on the side with total participation: Department gets
  `head_faculty_id`, with `UNIQUE`.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Weak entity: owner's key in its key" icon="i-lucide-key-square"}
  FeeInstalment(student_id, instalment_no, amount), primary key both.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Multi-valued attribute: its own table" icon="i-lucide-list"}
  StudentPhone(student_id, phone), primary key both.
  :::
::

::runner{lang="sql"}
```sql [er-to-tables.sql]
PRAGMA foreign_keys = ON;
CREATE TABLE Faculty    (faculty_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, full_name TEXT NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE Department (department_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, name TEXT NOT NULL,
                         head_faculty_id INTEGER UNIQUE REFERENCES Faculty);          -- 1:1
CREATE TABLE Student    (student_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, full_name TEXT NOT NULL,
                         department_id INTEGER NOT NULL REFERENCES Department);       -- 1:N, total
CREATE TABLE Course     (course_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, code TEXT NOT NULL,
                         faculty_id INTEGER REFERENCES Faculty);                      -- 1:N
CREATE TABLE Enrolment  (student_id INTEGER REFERENCES Student, course_id INTEGER REFERENCES Course,
                         grade TEXT, PRIMARY KEY (student_id, course_id));            -- M:N
CREATE TABLE FeeInstalment (student_id INTEGER REFERENCES Student, instalment_no INTEGER,
                         amount INTEGER, PRIMARY KEY (student_id, instalment_no));    -- weak

INSERT INTO Faculty VALUES (1, 'Dr. Meera Joshi'), (2, 'Vikram Singh');
INSERT INTO Department VALUES (1, 'CSE', 1);
INSERT INTO Student VALUES (1, 'Anita Rao', 1), (2, 'Rahul Verma', 1);
INSERT INTO Course VALUES (101, 'CS201', 2), (102, 'CS202', 2);
INSERT INTO Enrolment VALUES (1, 101, 'A'), (1, 102, 'B'), (2, 101, 'C');
INSERT INTO FeeInstalment VALUES (1, 1, 42500), (1, 2, 42500), (2, 1, 42500);

SELECT s.full_name, c.code, e.grade, f.full_name AS taught_by
FROM   Enrolment e
JOIN   Student s ON s.student_id = e.student_id
JOIN   Course  c ON c.course_id  = e.course_id
JOIN   Faculty f ON f.faculty_id = c.faculty_id
ORDER BY s.full_name, c.code;
```
::

## Real use case

A college decides to add hostel allotment. Before anyone writes code, the team
answers the ER questions on a whiteboard, and every answer changes the tables:

- Is a room an entity? Yes: it has a number, a block, a capacity.
- Student to room: can a student have two rooms at once? No. Can a room hold
  several students? Yes, up to its capacity. So **N:1**, foreign key on the
  student side... unless the college wants **history** of who lived where each
  year, in which case it is **M:N over time**, and it becomes a junction table
  `RoomAllotment(student_id, room_id, from_date, to_date)`.
- Participation: must every student have a room? No, day scholars do not. So
  partial, and the column (or the junction row) is optional.

The second answer is the important one: "does it change over time, and do we
need the history?" turns an N:1 into an M:N more often than any other question.
Asking it in an interview design round is what makes the answer sound
experienced.

## What confuses people here

- **Entity vs attribute.** Is "department" an attribute of a student or an
  entity? If it has facts of its own (a head, a building), it is an entity. If it
  is only a label, it can be an attribute. Most things that interviewers ask
  about turn out to be entities.
- **Putting the foreign key on the wrong side of a 1:N.** A department cannot
  hold a list of student ids in one column (that breaks the first normal form,
  in the normalisation chapter). The "many" side holds the key.
- **M:N cannot be a foreign key.** It always needs a third table.
- **Relationship attributes.** A grade belongs to neither Student nor Course; it
  belongs to the enrolment. Attributes of an M:N relationship live in the
  junction table.

## Interview questions

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What is an ER model? What are its components?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  A conceptual model of the data as entities, their attributes and the
  relationships between them, drawn as an ER diagram before tables are designed.
  Components: entity sets (rectangles), attributes (ellipses), relationships
  (diamonds), with cardinality and participation on the lines.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="What is a weak entity? Give an example." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  An entity with no key of its own, identified by its owner's key plus a partial
  key. A fee instalment: (student_id, instalment_no). Drawn as a double rectangle
  with a double diamond, and always total participation.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="How do you convert an M:N relationship into tables?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  A new junction table holding both entities' primary keys as foreign keys, with
  the pair as its primary key, plus any attributes of the relationship.
  Enrolment(student_id, course_id, grade).
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="What are the types of attributes?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Simple and composite (name split into parts), single-valued and multi-valued
  (several phone numbers), stored and derived (age from date of birth), and key
  attributes.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="What is the difference between total and partial participation?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Total: every entity must take part in the relationship (every student belongs
  to a department); drawn as a double line, and becomes `NOT NULL` on the foreign
  key. Partial: some may not (not every faculty member heads a department).
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="Draw an ER diagram for a college." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Start by asking two questions (can a student be in two departments? do we need
  history?). Then: Department 1:N Student, Student M:N Course through Enrolment
  with grade, Faculty 1:N Course, Faculty 1:1 Department as head, Student 1:N
  weak entity FeeInstalment. Name the keys and the cardinalities as you draw.
  :::
::
