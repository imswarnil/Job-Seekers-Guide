Partial dependency and transitive dependency are now defined precisely, and
they are exactly what the second and third normal forms forbid. This lesson
takes one badly designed college table and walks it through 1NF, 2NF and 3NF,
one problem at a time, with the tables at every step.

## Why this matters in a job

"Explain 1NF, 2NF and 3NF with an example" is the single most asked DBMS
question in fresher interviews, and the weak answer recites definitions. The
strong answer takes a table, names the dependency that breaks each form, and
shows the split. Nobody at work will ask you to define 3NF; several people will
show you a bad table and ask what is wrong with it.

## Three problems, three forms

I was taught the forms as definitions and could recite them without being able
to apply one. They make sense the other way round: each form removes **one
specific problem**.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Each normal form includes the one before it."}
  :::flow-step{label="1NF: one value per cell" icon="i-lucide-grid-3x3"}
  No lists, no repeating groups. Every attribute atomic.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="2NF: no partial dependency" icon="i-lucide-split"}
  1NF, and no non-prime attribute depends on only part of a candidate key.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="3NF: no transitive dependency" icon="i-lucide-link-2-off" highlight}
  2NF, and no non-prime attribute depends on another non-prime attribute.
  :::
::

The informal version that is easy to remember: every non-key attribute must
depend on **the key** (1NF gives you a key), **the whole key** (2NF), **and
nothing but the key** (3NF).

## How it works: one table, three steps

### The starting point (not even 1NF)

The exam cell's register, one row per student, with their courses in one cell:

| student_id | student_name | department | dept_head | courses (code: title: marks) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Anita Rao | CSE | Dr. Meera Joshi | CS201: Data Structures: 87, CS202: DBMS: 72 |
| 2 | Rahul Verma | ECE | Dr. Suresh Rao | CS201: Data Structures: 64 |
| 3 | Priya Nair | CSE | Dr. Meera Joshi | CS202: DBMS: 91 |

### Step 1: First normal form

**Rule:** every attribute holds a single, atomic value from its domain. No
lists, no repeating groups.

The `courses` cell breaks it. Asking "who scored above 80 in DBMS?" means
parsing text with `LIKE`, which also matches the wrong things and cannot use an
index:

::runner{lang="sql"}
```sql [not-1nf.sql]
CREATE TABLE Register (student_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, student_name TEXT, courses TEXT);
INSERT INTO Register VALUES
  (1, 'Anita Rao',   'CS201: Data Structures: 87, CS202: DBMS: 72'),
  (2, 'Rahul Verma', 'CS201: Data Structures: 64'),
  (3, 'Priya Nair',  'CS202: DBMS: 91');

-- "Who took DBMS?" The best a list-in-a-cell allows.
SELECT student_name FROM Register WHERE courses LIKE '%DBMS%';
```
::

```text [Output]
student_name
------------
Anita Rao
Priya Nair
```

It works on this data, and now try "who scored above 80 in DBMS": there is no
column to compare. **The fix:** one row per student per course. The key becomes
`{student_id, course_code}`:

`R1(student_id, student_name, department, dept_head, course_code, course_title, marks)`

| student_id | student_name | department | dept_head | course_code | course_title | marks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Anita Rao | CSE | Dr. Meera Joshi | CS201 | Data Structures | 87 |
| 1 | Anita Rao | CSE | Dr. Meera Joshi | CS202 | DBMS | 72 |
| 2 | Rahul Verma | ECE | Dr. Suresh Rao | CS201 | Data Structures | 64 |
| 3 | Priya Nair | CSE | Dr. Meera Joshi | CS202 | DBMS | 91 |

This is in 1NF. It is also the wide table from the anomalies lesson, with every
anomaly still present. 1NF is necessary and nowhere near sufficient.

### Step 2: Second normal form

**Rule:** in 1NF, and **no non-prime attribute is partially dependent** on a
candidate key (depends on only part of it).

The FDs, from the last lesson:

- `student_id → student_name, department`
- `department → dept_head`
- `course_code → course_title`
- `{student_id, course_code} → marks`

The candidate key is `{student_id, course_code}`. Check each non-prime attribute:

| Attribute | Depends on | Whole key or part? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `marks` | `{student_id, course_code}` | Whole key: fine |
| `student_name` | `student_id` | **Part**: partial dependency |
| `department` | `student_id` | **Part**: partial dependency |
| `dept_head` | `student_id` (through department) | **Part**: partial dependency |
| `course_title` | `course_code` | **Part**: partial dependency |

**The fix:** move each group of partially dependent attributes into a table
keyed on the part it depends on:

- `Student(student_id, student_name, department, dept_head)`: key `student_id`
- `Course(course_code, course_title)`: key `course_code`
- `Result(student_id, course_code, marks)`: key `{student_id, course_code}`

Each split is lossless: `Result` shares `student_id` with `Student`, which is
`Student`'s key, and `course_code` with `Course`, which is `Course`'s key.

::note
A table whose candidate keys are all **single attributes** is automatically in
2NF: there is no "part" of a one-column key to depend on. 2NF problems only
exist with composite keys, which is why the example needs one.
::

### Step 3: Third normal form

**Rule:** in 2NF, and **no non-prime attribute is transitively dependent** on a
candidate key. Formally: for every non-trivial FD X → A, either X is a super key
or A is a prime attribute.

`Course` and `Result` are fine. `Student` is not:

`student_id → department` and `department → dept_head`, and `department` is not a
key of `Student`. So `dept_head` depends on the key only **through** another
non-key column. That is a transitive dependency, and it is the source of the
"two heads of CSE" update anomaly.

**The fix:** move the transitively dependent attribute into a table keyed on the
attribute it really depends on:

- `Student(student_id, student_name, department)`
- `Department(department, dept_head)`

### The final design, built and checked

::runner{lang="sql"}
```sql [3nf-design.sql]
PRAGMA foreign_keys = ON;
CREATE TABLE Department (department TEXT PRIMARY KEY, dept_head TEXT NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE Student    (student_id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, student_name TEXT NOT NULL,
                         department TEXT NOT NULL REFERENCES Department);
CREATE TABLE Course     (course_code TEXT PRIMARY KEY, course_title TEXT NOT NULL);
CREATE TABLE Result     (student_id INTEGER REFERENCES Student, course_code TEXT REFERENCES Course,
                         marks INTEGER CHECK (marks BETWEEN 0 AND 100),
                         PRIMARY KEY (student_id, course_code));

INSERT INTO Department VALUES ('CSE', 'Dr. Meera Joshi'), ('ECE', 'Dr. Suresh Rao');
INSERT INTO Student    VALUES (1, 'Anita Rao', 'CSE'), (2, 'Rahul Verma', 'ECE'), (3, 'Priya Nair', 'CSE');
INSERT INTO Course     VALUES ('CS201', 'Data Structures'), ('CS202', 'DBMS');
INSERT INTO Result     VALUES (1, 'CS201', 87), (1, 'CS202', 72), (2, 'CS201', 64), (3, 'CS202', 91);

-- Lossless: the join rebuilds exactly the four original 1NF rows.
SELECT r.student_id, s.student_name, s.department, d.dept_head, r.course_code, c.course_title, r.marks
FROM   Result r
JOIN   Student s    ON s.student_id  = r.student_id
JOIN   Department d ON d.department  = s.department
JOIN   Course c     ON c.course_code = r.course_code
ORDER BY r.student_id, r.course_code;

-- And the question 1NF could not answer.
SELECT s.student_name, r.marks
FROM   Result r JOIN Student s ON s.student_id = r.student_id
             JOIN Course c ON c.course_code = r.course_code
WHERE  c.course_title = 'DBMS' AND r.marks > 80;
```
::

```text [Output]
student_id  student_name  department  dept_head        course_code  course_title     marks
----------  ------------  ----------  ---------------  -----------  ---------------  -----
1           Anita Rao     CSE         Dr. Meera Joshi  CS201        Data Structures  87
1           Anita Rao     CSE         Dr. Meera Joshi  CS202        DBMS             72
2           Rahul Verma   ECE         Dr. Suresh Rao   CS201        Data Structures  64
3           Priya Nair    CSE         Dr. Meera Joshi  CS202        DBMS             91

student_name  marks
------------  -----
Priya Nair    91
```

Four tables. Every fact in exactly one place: a head's name once, a course
title once, a student's department once. The joins rebuild the original sheet
exactly, so nothing was lost.

## Real use case

A timetable table, the kind a college office builds in its first week:

| faculty | faculty_email | department | course_code | course_title | room | day | slot |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vikram Singh | vikram@… | CSE | CS201 | Data Structures | A-101 | Mon | 09:00 |
| Vikram Singh | vikram@… | CSE | CS202 | DBMS | A-101 | Tue | 11:00 |
| Divya Menon | divya@… | ECE | EC201 | Signals | B-204 | Mon | 09:00 |

FDs: `faculty → faculty_email, department`; `course_code → course_title`;
`{room, day, slot} → faculty, course_code` (one class per room per slot). The
3NF design:

- `Faculty(faculty_id, name, email UNIQUE, department)`
- `Course(course_code, course_title)`
- `TimetableSlot(room, day, slot, faculty_id, course_code)`, primary key
  `(room, day, slot)`, plus `UNIQUE (faculty_id, day, slot)`

That last `UNIQUE` is the part worth noticing. Once the data is normalised, a
real rule that was impossible to express before ("nobody teaches two classes at
once") becomes one line the database enforces on every write. That is the
pay-off of normalising, and textbooks rarely say it.

## What confuses people here

- **2NF only matters with composite keys.** If every candidate key is a single
  column, a 1NF table is already in 2NF.
- **3NF allows an exception for prime attributes.** "X → A where A is prime" is
  allowed even if X is not a super key. That loophole is exactly what BCNF
  closes, in the next lesson.
- **Normalising a surrogate-keyed table.** Adding an `id` column to the wide
  table does not make it 2NF-clean in any useful sense: `department → dept_head`
  is still there. Look at the dependencies, not at the key column.
- **Each form includes the previous.** A table in 3NF is in 2NF and 1NF.

## Interview questions

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Explain 1NF, 2NF and 3NF with an example." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  1NF: atomic values, no lists in a cell (split "CS201, CS202" into rows). 2NF:
  no non-prime attribute depends on part of a composite key (move
  `student_name`, which depends only on `student_id`, out of the
  (student_id, course_code) table). 3NF: no non-prime attribute depends on
  another non-prime attribute (move `dept_head`, which depends on `department`,
  into a Department table). Show the tables.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="What is the difference between 2NF and 3NF?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  2NF removes partial dependencies (on part of a candidate key). 3NF also removes
  transitive dependencies (a non-prime attribute depending on another non-prime
  attribute). `student_id → department → dept_head` is fine for 2NF when the key
  is `student_id`, and breaks 3NF.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="R(A, B, C, D), key AB, with AB → C and B → D. Highest normal form?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  1NF only. D is non-prime and depends on B, which is part of the key AB: a
  partial dependency, so not 2NF. Decompose into R1(A, B, C) and R2(B, D); both
  are then in BCNF.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="R(A, B, C), key A, with A → B and B → C. Highest normal form?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  2NF (the key is a single attribute, so no partial dependency), not 3NF: C
  depends on B, a non-prime attribute, so A → B → C is transitive. Decompose into
  R1(A, B) and R2(B, C).
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="Is a table with a single-column primary key always in 2NF?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Yes, if it is in 1NF and that is its only candidate key: there is no proper part
  of a one-attribute key to depend on. If it has another, composite, candidate
  key, check that one too.
  :::
::
