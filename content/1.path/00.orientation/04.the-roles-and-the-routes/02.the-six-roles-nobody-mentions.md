Six more, and between them they hire more people than front-end and back-end
combined. Almost nobody is told about them before they graduate, which is how a
lot of well-suited people end up in the wrong queue.

## Why this matters in a job

Because if the only role you know about is "software developer", then the only
market you can apply into is the most competitive one, with the highest filter,
at the exact moment you are least prepared for it.

Three of the six below routinely take people whose degree is not computer
science, and two of them are the most common back doors into engineering that
exist.

## The six

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-bar-chart-3" title="Data analyst / data engineer"}
  An analyst answers questions with data: which programmes are under-subscribed,
  which department's attendance predicts failure. An engineer builds the pipes
  that make the answering possible. **SQL is the whole job** for the first one,
  which makes it the most accessible technical role in this list.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-bug" title="Test / QA engineer"}
  Not "clicking things". Designing what evidence would convince you the system is
  correct, then automating it. Pays a little below a developer and is one of the
  two classic doors into engineering, because you read more code than you write.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-server-cog" title="Infrastructure / DevOps / SRE"}
  Keeping it running. Deployments, monitoring, capacity, the 3 a.m. page. Pays at
  or above developer level, and it is not an entry-level role — you get here from
  support or from development, usually around year three.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-headset" title="Technical support engineer"}
  The other classic door. You learn the product and the customers faster than
  anybody in the building, which is exactly the knowledge an engineering team
  lacks. Lower bar to enter, and a real ceiling unless you move deliberately.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-shield" title="Security engineer"}
  Finding what is exploitable before somebody else does. Rarely entry-level, very
  well paid, and reachable from any of the roles above. It is a way of thinking
  about systems rather than a tool you learn.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-clipboard-list" title="Product / business analyst"}
  Deciding what should be built, and writing down precisely enough that it can
  be. Technical without being a coding job, and the most common landing place for
  people who like the domain more than the machine.
  :::
::

## What the same feature looks like to each of them

One request: *"let the registrar shortlist applicants."* Six different jobs.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Same sentence, six different first questions."}
  :::flow-step{label="Product analyst" icon="i-lucide-clipboard-list"}
  What decision follows from the shortlist? Does a shortlisted applicant get an
  email? Can they be un-shortlisted? Writes it down so it can be built.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Back-end engineer" icon="i-lucide-server"}
  Who is allowed, what the rule is, and how the query performs over 40,000 rows.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Test engineer" icon="i-lucide-bug" highlight}
  What about score exactly equal to the cutoff? A withdrawn applicant? Two
  registrars at once? What proves this still works next month?
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Data engineer" icon="i-lucide-bar-chart-3"}
  Is the shortlisting recorded so we can ask, next year, whether the cutoff was
  a good predictor of results?
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Infrastructure" icon="i-lucide-server-cog"}
  What happens on results day when this is called four thousand times a minute,
  and what do we see when it breaks?
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Security" icon="i-lucide-shield"}
  Can a student call this endpoint directly? Does the error message reveal
  whether an applicant exists?
  :::
::

The highlighted one is worth dwelling on. The test engineer's questions are the
questions an interviewer asks a developer, which is why doing that job for two
years makes people good.

## Which take you without a CSE degree

::compare{caption="Honest, for the Indian market as it stands."}
  :::compare-side{label="Routinely, on evidence" verdict="right"}
  Data analyst, test engineer, technical support, product analyst. A degree in
  any discipline plus demonstrable SQL and clear writing is genuinely enough for
  the first two.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Harder without one, not impossible" verdict="neutral"}
  Front-end, back-end, infrastructure, security. Not because the work needs the
  degree, but because the filter at the top of the funnel uses it when there are
  4,000 applicants and one day.
  :::
::

::real-life{title="The person on the highest salary in my batch" source="Bangalore, ten years on"}
Started in technical support at a product company, because that was the offer
that came. Learned the product properly, built two internal tools nobody asked
for, moved into engineering in year two, and was a senior engineer by year six.
The route was invisible on the CV by then. What made it work was building
something real while in a role nobody considered technical.
::

## What confuses people here

That these are lesser roles. A data analyst who can write a query that answers a
vice-chancellor's question is more valuable to a university than a developer who
can build a screen nobody asked for. The hierarchy people imagine is mostly about
which role has the word "engineer" in the title.

## What you can do now

You can name eight roles rather than one, and you can apply into four markets
instead of the single most crowded one.

## Your turn

Pick the two roles from this lesson you would least like to do. Write one
sentence each on why.

That is not a throwaway exercise. Knowing what you do not want is far more
reliable than knowing what you do, and it is a genuinely good thing to be able to
say in an interview without sounding negative.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="How to say it in an interview without it landing badly" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Frame it as a preference, not a judgement. "I would rather not be in a role
  that is mostly incident response, because I do most of my good work in long
  uninterrupted stretches" is honest and tells them something useful. "I do not
  want to do support, it is not real engineering" is the same preference, said in
  a way that will cost you the offer.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Which two roles are the classic doors into engineering, and what do they both
   give you that a developer role does not?
2. **An interviewer asks: "where do you see yourself in three years?"** Use this
   lesson to answer without either lying or naming their job.

## The interview question this answers

**"Would you consider a role in support or QA?"**

Asked far more often than freshers expect, and usually because that is the
opening they actually have. The answer that works: yes, with a reason that shows
you know what the role gives you, and a statement of where you want to end up.
Saying no reflexively closes the most reliable door into the industry that
exists.
