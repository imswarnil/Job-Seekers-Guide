The last one, and a course that cannot say this is selling something.

## Why this matters in a job

Because the cost of finding out in year three that you dislike this is measured
in years, and the cost of finding out now is measured in the next ten minutes.

## Four honest cases

**You do not like reading.** Not "you are not a reader of novels". You dislike
sitting with text you do not understand until you do. This job is that, most
days: somebody else's code, an error message, a specification, a log. It is the
one thing on this page I would not try to talk you out of.

**You want a predictable, finished skill.** Software does not have one. It is
roughly a subject a year, indefinitely. Some people find that energising and some
find it exhausting, and both are legitimate. If you want to become expert once and
then practise that expertise, there are excellent careers shaped that way and this
is not one of them.

**You need visible appreciation to keep going.** Work that goes right is
invisible. The fee calculation that has been correct for two years gets no thanks;
the hour it was wrong gets an email from the vice-chancellor. If that would grind
you down, look at the roles closer to people: support engineering, solution
consulting, product, teaching. They are real careers, they pay well, and they use
most of this same material.

**You are only here for the money.** This is the one people expect me to be
scornful about and I am not. It was most of my reason. It is a genuinely
reasonable motivation and it got me from thirteen thousand rupees to a life my
family did not have. It is just not sufficient on its own for the eighteen months
of being bad at something in public that comes first, so pair it with at least
mild curiosity about the machine, or the eighteen months will beat you.

::pull-quote
I was not talented. I was not passionate about software. What I eventually got
was a sequence, and the willingness to be bad at it for a year.
::

## The honest timeline

Nobody publishes this, so here it is with the numbers in.

::timeline{label="From nothing to a first job, at an hour or two a day"}
  :::timeline-item{title="Months 1–3" date="The machine, English, Java basics" state="done"}
  Slow, and it feels like nothing is happening. Most people quit here. Nothing is
  wrong with you; this is the shape of it.

  :::timeline-item{title="Months 4–8" date="Java properly, SQL, the first real thing" state="done"}
  The turn. You start being able to build something that works without following

  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Months 9–12" date="The web, the system, deployment" state="current"}
  You have a real application on a real address that a stranger can open. This

  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Months 12–18" date="Applying, failing, applying" state="todo"}
  I cleared roughly one interview in forty. Budget for that ratio and it stops
  being evidence about you.
  :::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
If somebody is telling you six weeks, they are selling a course. Twelve to
eighteen months, at an hour or two a day, is the honest range for somebody
starting from nothing. It is faster if you are already studying this full time.
::

## The two sentences

The first: **being confused is the normal condition of this work, not a signal
that you are unsuited to it.** I took confusion as evidence about my ability for
four years. It was evidence about my teaching.

The second: **the sequence is the scarce thing, not the content.** Every subject
in this course is free on the internet and always was. What ₹40,000 bought me in
Bangalore was somebody saying "learn this, now, in this order, and here is how to
talk about it". That is what the rest of this platform is, and it is free.

::real-life{title="What actually happens if you continue" source="My own, with the numbers"}
Four years of college with nothing to show. Not one written round cleared. A year
of walk-ins and rejections. An institute I could not really afford. A first job
at ₹13,000 a month, which I was delighted with. Then ₹24,000, then a long flat
stretch where I stopped pushing, and then, once I learned how to interview
properly, four offers in a year and eventually more than I ever thought I would
earn. It took seven years. It did not take talent.
::

## If you are continuing

Then this track has done its job, and the next thing is not code.

It is English, which is the thing that beat me in every first round for four
years, and which I did not understand was a technical subject until far too late.
Reading a specification, writing a bug report somebody can act on, and answering
"tell me about yourself" are not soft skills. They are the gate.

## Check yourself
