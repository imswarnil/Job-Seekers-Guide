Straight answer, then. This is the lesson I most needed and did not get, and the
decision it is about was made for me by a sentence from my brother.

## Why this matters in a job

Because the most expensive mistake in this field is not a bad framework choice.
It is four years and a large amount of somebody's money spent on a direction
nobody checked, and the check takes an afternoon.

## What a degree actually buys

Three things. Notice that "the ability to do the job" is not one of them.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-door-open" title="Access to the campus funnel"}
  The single biggest thing it buys. Companies come to your college and hire in
  bulk. Without a degree that door does not exist, and it is the easiest first
  job in the market.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-filter" title="Passing an automated filter"}
  Many large employers screen on degree before a human reads anything. This is
  lazy and it is real, and it costs you nothing to know it.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-clock" title="Four years and a structure"}
  Time to learn slowly, with deadlines somebody else set. Genuinely valuable if
  you use it, and it is the part I wasted.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-users" title="A batch"}
  Sixty people going through the same thing. Underrated, and the hardest thing
  to reproduce if you learn alone. I did learn alone, and it was the worst part.
  :::
::

::pull-quote
I completed a computer science degree and could not write a program. Passing and
understanding are different things, and the marksheet does not have a column for
it.
::

## The branches, honestly

::pros-cons
---
title: Computer science (CSE)
pros:
  - Every filter in the market is designed for you
  - The syllabus is the right syllabus, OS, DBMS, networks, DSA are the job
  - The campus drives that visit are the ones hiring for software
cons:
  - Taught almost everywhere without any connection to the work, which is the whole problem this course exists to fix
  - The most competitive branch, so the same marks put you lower in the queue
---
::

::pros-cons
---
title: Information technology (IT)
pros:
  - Treated as equivalent to CSE by essentially every employer
  - Often slightly less competitive for the same outcome
cons:
  - Occasionally excluded by a badly written filter that names CSE only
  - Curriculum can drift toward tools rather than foundations, which ages faster
---
::

::pros-cons
---
title: Another engineering branch, mechanical, civil, electrical, electronics
pros:
  - Software companies do hire from every branch, and service companies hire at scale from all of them
  - Electronics in particular shares real ground: architecture, embedded systems, signals
  - You can enter through data, testing or support with no disadvantage at all
cons:
  - You must teach yourself the four core subjects, because nobody will teach them to you
  - Some campus drives are branch-restricted, so your funnel is narrower
  - You will be asked "why the switch?" in every interview, so have the answer ready
---
::

::pros-cons
---
title: No degree, or a degree in something unrelated
pros:
  - No four years and no fees; you can be employable in twelve to eighteen months
  - Judged almost entirely on evidence, which is fairer than it sounds
  - After the first job nobody asks again, genuinely, nobody
cons:
  - The first job is materially harder to get, and the campus funnel is closed
  - Automated filters at large employers will remove you before a person reads anything
  - You need enormous self-direction, and no batch to carry you through the bad months
---
::

## What confuses people here

**"A degree from a tier-3 college is worthless."** It is not. It is worth exactly
the four things in the list above, and the campus funnel is the big one. I went to
LNCT Bhopal, cleared no written round in four years, and the degree still opened
the door at Accenture eventually.

**"I should do an MTech to fix a weak profile."** Usually no. Two more years and
more fees to solve a problem that eighteen months of building and applying solves
better, unless you specifically want research or teaching. I would want a very
concrete reason before spending that.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Be careful about who is answering this question for you. An institute charging
₹50,000 to ₹1,50,000 has an interest in the answer being "yes, and with us". So,
for that matter, does a free course wanting your attention. Judge by whether the
advice tells you when not to buy.
::

## What you can do now

You can make this decision with the actual trade-offs in front of you instead of
with what a relative said at a wedding, which is how I made it.

## Your turn

Answer these three in writing. They take ten minutes and they are worth more than
any amount of further reading.

1. Which route am I on, and did I choose it or did it happen to me?
2. What does it cost, in money and years, and who is paying?
3. If I am already on it, what am I going to do with the part I control?

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Why question three is the only one that matters if you are already enrolled" icon="i-lucide-key"}
  Because the branch is decided and the fees are spent. What is still open is
  whether you graduate having built something, or having passed. I passed. It
  cost me a year and ₹40,000 afterwards to buy back what I could have had for
  free during four years I was already paying for.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Name the four things a degree actually buys, and say which is the largest.
2. **An interviewer asks: "you studied mechanical engineering. Why software?"**
   Answer in three sentences, without apologising for the degree.

## The interview question this answers

**"Why are you switching from your branch?"**

Do not apologise and do not tell a story about passion. Say what you found out,
what you did about it, and what you built. "I found the part of my project that I
enjoyed was the data analysis, so I learned SQL and Java properly and built X."
That is a decision, evidenced. Anything that sounds like "there were no jobs in
my branch" invites the follow-up you do not want.
