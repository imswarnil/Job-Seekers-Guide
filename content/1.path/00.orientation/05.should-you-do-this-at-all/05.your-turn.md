You know what the job is and you can say why you want it. This is the last thing
before the technical part, and it is the exercise most people skip and then wish
they had not.

None of it is code. All of it is written down, kept, and read again in three
months.

::note
Write these in a file you will still have later. `notes/starting.md`, or a
notebook. The point of several of them is comparing what you believed at the
start against what turned out to be true, and that only works if you can find
it.
::

## 1. Your honest starting point

Write one paragraph answering: **what can I do today?**

Not what you studied. What you could sit down and do right now, without help.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Why this is worth doing, and what honest looks like" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Because in four months you will not remember, and you will assume you always
  knew the things you now know. That assumption is what makes people feel they
  are making no progress when they are making a great deal.

  Honest looks like: "I can write a for loop in C if somebody reminds me of the
  syntax. I have never used a terminal. I have never used Git. I do not know
  what a database actually is, beyond that it stores things. I have never
  written anything longer than a hundred lines."

  Mine, in 2017, was shorter than that and I was a graduate. That is normal, and
  writing it down is the only way to prove to yourself later that it changed.
  :::
::

## 2. The hours you actually have

Not the hours you wish you had. Look at a real week.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="How to do this properly" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Write out seven days with real numbers, including the days you will do
  nothing, because there will be some and planning for zero of them is how
  plans fail.

  Then be pessimistic: whatever number you arrive at, subtract 30%. Something
  always happens.

  The useful part is that this number tells you how long the path takes, and a
  real answer is better than an optimistic one. Ten hours a week is enough. It
  is a longer road than twenty, and knowing which road you are on stops you
  concluding you are failing when you are simply going at ten hours a week.
  :::
::

## 3. What you are giving up

This is the one people skip, and it is the one that predicts whether they
finish.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Be specific" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Ten hours a week comes from somewhere. Name where. Evenings with friends,
  a side income, sleep, a game, time with family.

  Write the actual sentence: "this comes out of weekday evenings, which means
  two fewer evenings a week with people I like."

  Why it matters: an unnamed cost gets paid anyway and resented. A named one is
  a decision you made, and in month three, when the novelty is gone, the
  difference between those two is whether you continue.
  :::
::

## 4. Which of the two readers you are

The orientation track opened by naming two people. Decide which is you, and
write down what follows from it.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What follows from each" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **Not started yet, choosing a branch or a degree.** What follows: you have
  time, so foundations are worth more to you than tools. Do not skip the
  operating systems or networks tracks to get to a framework faster. You are the
  reader this path is shaped for.

  **Graduated and unemployed.** What follows: you need something to show, and
  you need it sooner than the full path allows. You may reorder. Do the terminal
  track, Java, SQL and then go straight to the build, and come back for
  operating systems and networks while applying. Say out loud what that costs:
  you will be weaker in exactly the rounds that ask about systems, and you will
  need to fill that in later.

  Either is fine. Choosing without deciding is not.
  :::
::

## 5. Your definition of done

Write the sentence that says when this has worked.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What a good one looks like" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Bad: "get a job in software." Too large, no date, nothing you control.

  Better: "by the end of this year, I have a deployed system I can demonstrate,
  and I have cleared at least one written round."

  Both halves are things you control. Neither depends on anybody hiring you,
  which matters, because the hiring part has a great deal of luck in it and a
  goal made of luck is a goal you can do everything right and still fail.

  Then the second sentence, and it is the important one: **what will I do if I
  reach that and still have no offer?** Answer it now, calmly, rather than in
  November when it is a crisis.
  :::
::

## 6. The thing you are most worried about

One line. The real one.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="The four that come up most, and what is true about each" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **"I am too old."** The four most common starting ages in this industry are
  21, 24, 27 and 31. Nobody in a technical interview has ever asked me my age.
  A career change at 30 is ordinary.

  **"I am not good at maths."** Most software needs arithmetic and clear
  thinking, not mathematics. The exceptions are graphics, machine learning
  research and cryptography, and those are a small fraction of the jobs. The
  aptitude round in this path is the most mathematics you will meet, and it is
  learnable in four weeks.

  **"My college is not good enough."** It affects which companies come to your
  campus and it stops affecting anything about eighteen months into your first
  job. The path around it is evidence: a deployed system beats a college name in
  the rooms that matter, and in the rooms it does not, you were not getting in
  anyway.

  **"Everyone else started earlier."** Some did. They also had nobody telling
  them what mattered, which is why most of them spent two years on the wrong
  things. Starting later with a sequence beats starting earlier without one, and
  that is the entire argument of this course.
  :::
::

## Read this again in three months

::pull-quote
Put a reminder in your calendar for three months from today, pointing at this
file. The single most common reason people stop is the belief that they are not
improving, and the only reliable cure is evidence from your own handwriting.
::
