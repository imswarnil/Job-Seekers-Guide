Fifty words about an industry you have not joined. Before anybody tests your
code there is a conversation about why you are doing this at all, and it happens
earlier than people expect.

## Why this matters

The first filter at most companies is a fifteen-minute phone call with somebody
who is not an engineer. They are checking three things: that you are real, that
you want this specific kind of work, and that you will still be there in a year.

You can fail that call with a perfect technical background, and people do.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. Why do you want to work in software?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  The bad answers are the ones everybody gives: "it is a growing field", "there
  is good scope", "I am passionate about technology". None of them is a reason.
  They are things you would say about any industry.

  A real answer is small and specific. "I liked that I could change something
  and see it work immediately. I built a script to sort my college attendance
  spreadsheet and it saved somebody four hours a month, and that was more
  satisfying than anything I did in a lab."

  Small and true beats large and impressive, every time. Nobody believes "I have
  been passionate about coding since I was eight". Plenty of people believe a
  spreadsheet script.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="2. What do you think a developer actually does all day?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  This is a genuine screening question and it catches people out, because the
  honest answer is not what most candidates expect.

  Mostly reading existing code, understanding a problem, and making a small
  careful change. Some meetings. A fair amount of working out why something
  does not do what it appears to say. Writing new code from scratch is the
  smallest part of the week.

  Saying that tells a recruiter you have looked at the job rather than the idea
  of the job, and it is unusual enough to be memorable.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="3. Front end or back end, and why?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Have a preference. "I am open to anything" sounds flexible and reads as "I
  have not thought about it".

  Give the preference and the reason, then the flexibility: "back end, because
  the part I enjoy is the data model and getting the rules right. I am happy
  working across the front end, and I have, but that is where I would want to go
  deep."

  The reason is what matters. Any of the three answers is correct.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="4. Services company or product company?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Asked by recruiters at both, and there is a trap: disparaging the kind of
  company you are talking to.

  The honest answer if you are early: a services company gives you exposure to
  several clients and problems quickly, which is a real advantage at the start.
  A product company gives you depth on one system over years. I would take
  either for a first role, and what I care about more is whether I will have
  somebody reviewing my code.

  That last clause is the strongest thing in the answer, and it is true.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="5. You do not have a computer science degree. Why should we hire you?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Or the variant: your degree is from a college nobody has heard of.

  Do not apologise and do not oversell. Point at evidence: "I do not have the
  degree. What I have is a system I built end to end, which is deployed and
  which you can open, and I can walk you through why I made each decision in it.
  That is the thing the degree was meant to produce."

  Evidence beats credentials in this industry more than in almost any other, and
  this is the one question where that is unambiguously in your favour.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="6. Where do you see yourself in five years?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Nobody knows and everybody knows nobody knows. What is being checked is
  whether your answer is compatible with the role.

  A direction is enough. "Deeper on backend and data, and eventually the person
  the team asks about the schema." Name the next step rather than a title.

  Avoid: wanting to start your own company, wanting to move abroad quickly, or
  wanting to move into management within two years. All fine things to want, and
  all of them answer their real question, "will you still be here", with a no.
  :::
::

## The one rule underneath all six

::pull-quote
Be specific and be true. A small honest reason is more convincing than a large
invented one, because the follow-up question cannot catch it out.
::

Every one of these questions has a follow-up, and the follow-up is where
invented answers fall apart. "You said you are passionate about technology.
What was the last thing you read about it?" ends more calls than any technical
question.

## The questions to ask them

You get asked "do you have any questions" on this call too, and saying no is a
wasted opportunity.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-users" title="Who would I be working with?"}
  Team size, and whether there is somebody senior who reviews code. In a first
  job this matters more than the salary, and it is the single best question you
  can ask.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-calendar" title="What does the process look like?"}
  How many rounds, what each one covers, and how long between them. Practical,
  reasonable, and it lets you prepare properly.
  :::
::
