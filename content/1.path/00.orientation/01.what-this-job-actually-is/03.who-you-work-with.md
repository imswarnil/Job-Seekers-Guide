There are about six of them. Product, design, a senior engineer, a tester, an
operations person, and whoever inside the organisation actually uses the thing.
Each one is judged on something different, and once you know what, their
otherwise baffling requests stop being baffling.

## Why this matters in a job

Because almost every "difficult person" in a junior's first year is a person
whose pressure you cannot see. The product manager who keeps changing their mind
is being asked for a date by somebody above them. The tester who files what looks
like a pedantic bug is the only person in the building whose job is to find it
before a student does.

I lost most of a year being quietly annoyed at people whose jobs I had not
bothered to understand.

## The six

::persona
---
name: Priya
role: Product manager
background: Judged on whether the right thing got built
---
Brings you a sentence, not a specification: "the registrar needs to shortlist
faster". Wants to know when, and will accept "I do not know yet, I will know by
Thursday" far better than a guess she plans on. Ask her what happens in the odd
cases. She will usually not have thought about them, and she will be glad you
did.
::

::persona
---
name: Arjun
role: Designer
background: Judged on whether it is usable
---
Hands you a picture. The picture will not say what happens when there are two
thousand rows, or when a name is sixty characters long, or when the network is
slow. Those three questions, asked early, are the most useful thing a junior
engineer can contribute to a design.
::

::persona
---
name: Shishir
role: Senior engineer
background: Judged partly on your output, which is the part nobody tells you
---
Reviews your work. Will ask you to change things. Is not attacking you, is
protecting a system they will be maintaining in two years. The way to be liked by
this person is not to be brilliant, it is to be predictable: say what you are
doing, say when you are stuck, and never silently sit on a problem for three
days.
::

::persona
---
name: Fatima
role: Tester, quality assurance
background: Judged on what escapes to real users
---
Will find the thing you did not think of. A bug report from her is not a
complaint, it is the cheapest possible version of the same discovery. The
expensive version is a student seeing the wrong fee amount.
::

::persona
---
name: Ravi
role: Operations and platform
background: Judged on whether it stays up at 3 a.m.
---
Cares about the questions you have not yet learned to ask: what happens when this
runs on two thousand records, what it logs when it fails, and whether it can be
switched off without a deploy. Every one of those is taught later in this course,
and this is the person they are for.
::

::persona
---
name: Mrs Nair
role: The registrar: the person who actually uses it
background: Judged on getting three hundred admissions done by Friday
---
The most important person on this list and the one a junior speaks to least. She
has a spreadsheet she trusts more than your software, for reasons that are
probably good. Fifteen minutes watching her work will teach you more about the
system than a week of reading it.
::

## What confuses people here

That your job is to satisfy all six. It is not. They genuinely want different
things and some of those conflict: Priya wants it Thursday, Fatima wants it
right, Ravi wants it boring. Your job is to make the conflict visible early
rather than absorb it silently and miss Thursday.

::tip
The single most valuable sentence available to a junior engineer: "I can have the
simple version by Thursday, or the version that handles withdrawn applications by
Monday. Which do you want?" It converts your problem into their decision, which
is where it belongs.
::

## What you can do now

You can read a request and ask who it came from and what that person is measured
on. This is most of what people mean when they say somebody is "good to work
with", and it costs nothing to learn before you have a job.

## Your turn

Take one sentence: *"Can we show the dean how this intake is going?"*

Write down the three questions you would ask before writing anything, and who
you would ask each one.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="The three I would ask" icon="i-lucide-list-checks"}
  **To the dean, or Priya on their behalf:** what decision will you make from
  this screen? A number you glance at and a number you act on are different
  products.

  **To the dean:** compared with what? "Going well" is meaningless without last
  year beside it.

  **To Ravi:** how live does this need to be? Recalculated on every page load and
  recalculated nightly are two different systems, and one of them is ten times
  cheaper.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Which person on that list is a junior most likely to under-consult, and why is
   that expensive?
2. **An interviewer asks: "how do you handle it when requirements change halfway
   through?"** Answer using what this lesson says about who is under what
   pressure.

## The interview question this answers

**"Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone on your team."**

Nearly everyone answers this as a story about being right. A good answer shows
you understood what the other person was being measured on, and describes making
the trade-off explicit rather than winning. If you have no work example, a group
project counts, as long as you are honest about it.
