Where you physically are matters more for a first job than for any job after it,
and the reason is narrow: almost everything you need in year one is learned by
overhearing.

## Why this matters in a job

Because the advice "remote is better" is written by people with ten years of
experience, for whom it is true. For a first job it is often the opposite, and
nobody says so because it sounds reactionary.

::real-life{title="What I actually got from Accenture, and it was not the training" source="Bangalore, 2019"}
The formal Salesforce training was useful. What was more useful was sitting near
people. I heard how a senior person talked to a client when something had broken.
I heard the words they used for things. I found out what was normal to not know.
None of that was in a course, and none of it would have reached me over chat.
::

## The three arrangements

::pros-cons
---
title: In an office
pros:
  - You learn by overhearing, which is most of year one
  - Being stuck is resolved in two minutes by turning around
  - People form an opinion of you from more than your output
cons:
  - Commute, and in Bangalore that can be three hours of your day
  - Cost of living where the offices are
  - Open-plan noise is real and nobody fixes it
---
::

::pros-cons
---
title: Hybrid
pros:
  - Most of the overhearing, most of the quiet
  - The usual arrangement now, so you are not asking for an exception
cons:
  - You still have to live near the office
  - The in-office days are often meeting days, which is the wrong half
---
::

::pros-cons
---
title: Fully remote
pros:
  - No commute, live anywhere, often a wider set of employers
  - Genuinely better once you know what you are doing
cons:
  - As a first job it is the hardest possible setting to learn in
  - You must ask for everything explicitly, and juniors under-ask
  - It is possible to be quietly failing for four months and not know
---
::

::tip
If your first job is remote, buy the thing you are missing deliberately: ask for
a weekly thirty minutes with one senior engineer, with no agenda. It replaces
overhearing with something scheduled. Ask in week one, when it is a normal
request rather than a signal.
::

## The four things nobody advertises

**It is a reading job.** You will read far more code than you write, most of it
written by people who have left, some of it bad, none of it explained. If you
hate reading, this is the wrong career and that is worth knowing now.

**You will feel behind permanently.** There is always a technology you have not
learned. This does not stop at senior level, it just stops frightening you. The
people who look like they know everything have simply been confused for longer.

**The learning never finishes.** Roughly a subject a year, forever. That is a
feature if you like it and a grind if you do not.

**Your work is invisible when it goes right.** Nobody thanks you for the fee
calculation that has been correct for two years. They notice the one hour it was
wrong. You have to take satisfaction from the thing itself, because the applause
is not coming.

::side-note{kind="go-deeper"}
That last one is the real reason people leave this field, far more often than
difficulty. If you need visible appreciation to keep going, look at the roles in
chapter four that sit closer to people: support engineering, solution consulting,
developer relations. They are real careers and they pay well.
::

## What you can do now

You can choose a first job with the learning arrangement in mind instead of only
the salary, and you can name what you would need to make a remote first job
survivable.

## Your turn

Answer these three honestly, in writing, for yourself:

1. When I am stuck, do I ask quickly or do I sit on it?
2. Do I actually enjoy reading things other people wrote?
3. Do I need somebody to notice that I did well?

There are no wrong answers. There are answers that should change which job you
take, and answer two is the one that should change whether you take this job at
all.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Why question one decides remote or office" icon="i-lucide-help-circle"}
  If you sit on problems, an office fixes it for you: somebody sees your face.
  Remote does not have that mechanism, so a sitter-on-problems in a remote first
  job can lose a month to something a two-minute question would have solved. Know
  which one you are before you choose, not after.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why is fully remote work harder for a first job than for a fifth one?
2. **An interviewer asks: "what do you find difficult about this kind of work?"**
   Give a real answer that is not a disguised boast.

## The interview question this answers

**"What do you find difficult about this work?"**

The trap is answering with a hidden strength ("I care too much about quality").
Interviewers have heard it. A real answer, honestly held, plus what you do about
it, is better in every case: "reading somebody else's code and holding the whole
thing in my head. I take notes as I go now, which slows me down and stops me
getting lost."
