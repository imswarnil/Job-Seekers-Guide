A real Tuesday, then. Not a promotional one. I am going to walk you through a
day the way it actually runs, because when I was deciding on this I had a picture
in my head made entirely of films, and the picture was wrong in every detail.

## Why this matters in a job

Because you are about to spend somewhere between one and four years preparing for
a job you have never seen. That is the most expensive unverified assumption most
people make in their twenties.

It also matters in the interview. Almost everybody answers "why do you want this
job?" with something about passion for technology. The people who answer it with
a description of the work sound like they have already done it.

::real-life{title="What I said, and what I should have said" source="Tech Mahindra HR round, 2018"}
I was asked why I wanted to be a software engineer. I said I was passionate about
technology and wanted to learn new things. It is not a lie and it is not an
answer, and I could see it land as nothing. What I could have said, if anybody
had shown me a Tuesday: "because most of the work is reading something somebody
else wrote and working out why it is behaving differently from how it reads, and
I like that kind of puzzle."
::

## The day

::timeline{label="A Tuesday on a university admissions system"}
  :::timeline-item{title="09:15: Arrive, read" date="35 min" state="done"}
  Messages first. Overnight someone reported that the applicant list is showing
  the wrong count on the dean's dashboard. Nobody has told you to fix it yet.
  You read the report twice and go and look at the screen yourself.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="09:50: Stand-up" date="12 min" state="done"}
  Everyone says what they did yesterday, what they are doing today, and what is
  blocking them. Three sentences each. If it runs longer than fifteen minutes
  somebody is explaining rather than reporting.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="10:05: The count bug" date="1 h 40 min" state="done"}
  You reproduce it. Then you find where the count is calculated, read it, and
  discover it counts applications rather than applicants, and one applicant can
  apply to three programmes. You write the fix in four lines and a test in
  twelve.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="11:45: Review someone else's work" date="30 min" state="done"}
  A colleague has changed how fee receipts are numbered. You read it, do not
  understand one part, and ask. The asking is the job, not an admission.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="14:00: The actual feature" date="2 h 30 min" state="current"}
  Shortlisting. The registrar wants to filter applicants by score and by
  programme at once. You spend forty minutes deciding what it should do in the
  edge cases before you write anything.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="16:30: A meeting you are quiet in" date="45 min"}
  Product, design and two other engineers, deciding what next month looks like.
  You say two things and take notes on the rest.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="17:15: Push, write it down, stop" date="30 min"}
  Commit, open your change for review, write two lines on the count bug so the
  next person who meets it finds them.
  :::
::

## What confuses people here

Look at the shape of that day. Roughly two and a half hours of it was writing new
code. The rest was reading, reproducing, asking, deciding and explaining.

That ratio is not a bad day or a junior's day. It is the job. And in 2026 the
typing share has gone **down**, not up, because a machine will now produce a
first draft of the four-line fix in nine seconds. What it will not do is notice
that the count was counting the wrong noun.

::compare{caption="The same bug, two people."}
  :::compare-side{label="Fast typist" verdict="wrong"}
  Reads the report, sees a count that looks low, adds a `DISTINCT` to the query
  because that usually fixes counts. The number changes. It is now wrong in a
  different way, and nobody notices for a month.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Slow reader" verdict="right"}
  Asks what the dean means by "applicants", finds out one person can hold three
  applications, and fixes the thing that was actually wrong. Takes an hour
  longer. Is the person they ask next time.
  :::
::

## What you can do now

You can describe a working day in this job to another person. Try it out loud
once. It is the raw material for two interview answers ("why this job", "what do
you think the role involves") and both of those are asked constantly.

## Your turn

Write down, in your own words, where you think your own day would go. Six lines,
with rough times. Keep it. In a year, when you have the job, compare them.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What most people get wrong when they try this" icon="i-lucide-eye"}
  Two things, every time. They put seven hours of coding in it, and they leave
  out every conversation. If your imagined day has no other humans in it, you
  have imagined a different job from this one.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. In the Tuesday above, roughly what fraction of the day was spent writing new
   code, and what was the rest?
2. **An interviewer asks: "what do you think a software engineer actually does
   all day?"** Answer in four sentences, without using the word "passion".

## The interview question this answers

**"Why do you want to be a software engineer?"**

A good answer names the work. Something you find satisfying about reading a
system and working out why it behaves the way it does, or about being the person
who makes a confusing thing clear. Anything that would be equally true of a job
you have never seen is not an answer, and every interviewer has heard it four
hundred times.
