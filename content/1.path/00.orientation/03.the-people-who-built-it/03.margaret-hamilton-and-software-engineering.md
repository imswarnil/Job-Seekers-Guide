An engineering discipline, then. The word "software engineering" was coined by
somebody who had to argue that what she did was engineering at all, and three
minutes of evidence for her case arrived in July 1969, on the way down to the
Moon.

## Why this matters in a job

Because this lesson is the origin of the one habit that separates a working
program from a professional one: **deciding in advance what happens when things
go wrong, rather than assuming they will not.**

Every `try`/`catch` you will write, every check on user input, every "what if
this is null" is this lesson. It is also, reliably, the difference between a
fresher's project and one that gets somebody hired.

## What happened

Margaret Hamilton led the team that wrote the Apollo Guidance Computer software.
She used the term "software engineering" deliberately, and partly to be taken
seriously, in a period when the hardware people regarded the code as an
afterthought.

Her team designed the flight software with **priority scheduling**: if the
computer were ever overloaded, it would not fail. It would discard the least
important work and keep the most important running.

::timeline{label="Apollo 11, the last three minutes of the descent"}
  :::timeline-item{title="Programme alarm 1202" date="T−5 min" state="done"}
  The guidance computer reports that it is out of capacity. Then again. Then
  three more times.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="The cause" date="—" state="done"}
  A rendezvous radar had been left in a position that flooded the computer with
  work it did not need during a landing.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="What the software did" date="—" state="current"}
  Exactly what it had been designed to do: dropped the low-priority tasks,
  restarted cleanly, and kept the landing guidance running. It did not crash and
  it did not silently continue with bad numbers.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="The landing" date="T−0" state="done"}
  They landed. A mission was not aborted because somebody had thought, years
  earlier, about what should happen when there was too much to do.
  :::
::

## The idea, stated plainly

::compare{caption="Two responses to more work than you can handle."}
  :::compare-side{label="Undefined behaviour" verdict="wrong"}
  Do as much as you can, silently drop the rest, and report success. The caller
  gets a number and no reason to doubt it. This is the most dangerous failure
  mode in software, because nothing is on fire.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Defined degradation" verdict="right"}
  Decide beforehand what matters most, shed the rest deliberately, and say
  clearly that you did. The result may be smaller. It is never a lie.
  :::
::

::real-life{title="The same decision, in the university system" source="Results day"}
Results are published and forty thousand students load the page at once. The
design question is identical to Apollo's: what do you shed? The honest answer is
usually the analytics dashboard, the recommendation panel and the "students also
viewed" section, so that the one thing that must work: a student seeing their
own marks, keeps working. A site that instead tries to serve everything and
falls over has made the 1202 decision by accident, and made it badly.
::

## Watch it happen

Here is the same idea at the size you will actually meet it. Fee calculation,
where one applicant's record is malformed.

```java [FeeReport.java]
// Undefined: one bad record and nobody gets a report.
double total = 0;
for (FeePayment payment : payments) {
  total += payment.getAmount();      // NullPointerException on record 4,182
}
```

```java [FeeReport.java]
// Defined: the report survives, and says exactly what it could not count.
double total = 0;
List<String> skipped = new ArrayList<>();

for (FeePayment payment : payments) {
  if (payment.getAmount() == null) {
    skipped.add(payment.getReceiptNumber());
    continue;
  }
  total += payment.getAmount();
}
```

```text [Output]
Collected: ₹42,18,000 from 1,840 payments.
Excluded 3 payments with no amount recorded: FEE-2026-0412, FEE-2026-0819, FEE-2026-1133.
```

The second one is more code and it is the one a bursar can act on. Note what it
refuses to do: it does not quietly treat a missing amount as zero, which would
produce a plausible, wrong total and tell nobody.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Silently substituting zero for missing data is the single most common way a
beginner's program becomes confidently wrong. The number looks fine. It reconciles
against nothing. It is found in November by an auditor.
::

## What confuses people here

That this is about catching exceptions. It is about **deciding, before the
failure, what the correct behaviour under failure is.** A `catch` block that
swallows the error and continues is not error handling. It is the 1202 alarm with
the light removed.

## What you can do now

You can tell the difference between a program that works and one that behaves
predictably when it does not, and that distinction is most of what "production
code" means.

## Your turn

Take the fee loop above. Three more things can go wrong that neither version
handles. Name them, and say what the defined behaviour should be for each.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Three, and what I would do" icon="i-lucide-list-checks"}
  **A negative amount.** A refund recorded as a payment. Defined behaviour:
  exclude it from "collected", report it separately, because a bursar needs both
  numbers and they are not the same number.

  **A duplicate receipt number.** The same payment imported twice. Defined
  behaviour: count it once and report the duplicate loudly, because it is a data
  problem somebody has to fix at source.

  **An amount in the wrong currency or unit.** Paise recorded as rupees. This one
  cannot be detected by the loop at all, which is the real lesson: some errors
  have to be prevented by the type or the schema rather than caught at the point
  of use. That is the DBMS track.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why is "silently use zero when the amount is missing" worse than crashing?
2. **An interviewer asks: "what does your code do when something goes wrong?"**
   Answer with a real example of defined behaviour rather than with the word
   "try-catch".

## The interview question this answers

**"Tell me about error handling in something you have built."**

Weak answers describe syntax. Strong answers describe a decision: here is what
could fail, here is what I decided should happen, and here is how the caller
finds out. If you have built the fee report above you have that answer ready,
which is the entire reason this course builds one system rather than many toys.
