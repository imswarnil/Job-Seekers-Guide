The people who built this did not arrive the way the stories say. Start with the
argument that made your entire career possible: that a human should not have to
write in numbers.

## Why this matters in a job

Because the most valuable idea in this history is one you will use in your first
week, and it is not a technology. It is the idea that **code is written for
people to read**, and that spending machine time to save human time is almost
always correct.

Everything in this course that looks like extra work, naming things properly,
writing a test, splitting a method, is that argument, applied.

## Grace Hopper, and the compiler

In 1952 Grace Hopper wrote a program whose input was other programs. Her claim
was that a machine could translate human-readable instructions into the numbers
the machine executes.

The reaction is the part worth knowing.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-quote" color="neutral"}
She was told, repeatedly, that computers could only do arithmetic, and that a
program which wrote programs was not a serious idea. The objection was not
technical. It was that the machine's time was precious and the programmer's was
not, and translation wasted machine time.
::

She was right, and the reason she was right is the economics lesson from the
previous chapter arriving thirty years early: as soon as programmers become the
scarce resource, spending machine cycles to save human effort is obviously
correct.

She went on to lead the work that became COBOL, deliberately designed to read
like English so that people who were not mathematicians could write it. That
design decision is why COBOL is still running your bank.

::real-life{title="The same argument, in your first code review" source="Any team"}
Somebody will suggest a change that makes your code slower and clearer. Nine
times out of ten, take it. The tenth time is when you have measured that this
exact line is the bottleneck, and "measured" is the whole of the difference.
::

## John Backus, and FORTRAN

In 1957 Backus's team at IBM shipped FORTRAN with a claim nobody believed: that
a compiler could produce machine code nearly as good as a human writing it by
hand.

If that had been false, high-level languages would have stayed a toy for people
who did not care about speed. It was true, and the consequence is everything.

::compare{caption="The same calculation, a cutoff check on an applicant's score."}
  :::compare-side{label="What you would have written in 1954" verdict="wrong"}
  Load the score into a register. Compare with a constant held at a fixed memory
  address. Jump if less. You are managing the machine, and a change to the
  cutoff means finding that address again.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What you write now" verdict="right"}
  ```java
  if (applicant.getScore() >= PASS_MARK) {
    shortlist.add(applicant);
  }
  ```
  The compiler produces the registers and the jump. You spend your attention on
  whether `PASS_MARK` is the right rule, which is the only part a university
  actually cares about.
  :::
::

## What confuses people here

That this makes the low level irrelevant. It does not, and the way it bites you
is specific: the compiler hides the machine until something goes wrong, and then
the machine is what the error message is about. `OutOfMemoryError`,
`StackOverflowError`, a query that takes nine seconds, all of those are the
layer underneath, speaking up.

That is exactly why the next track in this course is the machine and the
terminal, before any language at all.

## What you can do now

You can defend a decision that trades machine time for clarity, with an argument
that is seventy years old and has never lost.

## Your turn

Find one piece of code you have written, or one from any tutorial, and rename
every variable so that the code reads as a sentence about the university. No
logic changes. Time yourself.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Why this is not a cosmetic exercise" icon="i-lucide-type"}
  Because naming is where you find out whether you understood the problem. If you
  cannot name a variable without a comment, you do not yet know what it holds.
  `temp`, `data` and `flag` are almost always a confession, and an interviewer
  reading your code can see it.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. What was the actual objection to Grace Hopper's compiler, and why did it stop
   being a good objection?
2. **An interviewer asks: "when would you choose slower but clearer code?"**
   Answer, then say what would change your mind.

## The interview question this answers

**"How do you balance readability and performance?"**

Default to readable, optimise where you have measured. The strong version of this
answer names the measurement: "I would profile it first, because the bottleneck
is almost never where I expect." Candidates who say "always optimise" reveal that
they have never maintained anything.
