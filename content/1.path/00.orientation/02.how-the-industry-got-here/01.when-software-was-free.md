None of it is an accident, and the reason goes back about seventy years. Start
here: for the first twenty years of this industry, software was not a product. It
was thrown in free with the machine.

## Why this matters in a job

Because two facts about your job market were set in that period and have never
moved: the job is defined by what the expensive resource is, and the expensive
resource has changed four times.

When the machine cost more than the building, programmers optimised for the
machine. When programmers became scarcer than machines, everything flipped. That
flip is why your salary exists.

## What a mainframe actually was

One enormous computer, one room, one organisation. A university in 1965 with a
computer had *a* computer: a single machine, administered by a priesthood, that
you did not touch.

::timeline{label="How you ran a program in 1965"}
  :::timeline-item{title="Write it on paper" state="done"}
  On a coding form, by hand. No screen.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Punch it onto cards" state="done"}
  One card per line. Drop the deck and your program is shuffled.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Hand the deck to an operator" state="done"}
  You do not enter the machine room. You queue.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Come back tomorrow" state="current"}
  A printout. If you had a typo, that was your day. One typo, one day.
  :::
::

::real-life{title="Why this is not a museum piece" source="Indian banking and insurance, today"}
Mainframes did not die. LIC, the State Bank of India, most of the airline
reservation systems and a large amount of global banking still run COBOL on
IBM mainframes, and there is a real and well-paid shortage of people who can
maintain them. That is not the career this course points you at, and it is worth
knowing it exists, because "old technology" and "no jobs" are not the same claim.
::

## What confuses people here

That this was primitive. It was not primitive, it was **expensive**, and every
practice of the era follows from the cost.

::compare{caption="The same discipline, two causes."}
  :::compare-side{label="Why they checked everything before running it" verdict="right"}
  A run cost a day. So you read your own code, by hand, line by line, and you
  reasoned about whether it was correct rather than finding out.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Why we mostly do not" verdict="wrong"}
  A run costs 200 milliseconds. So we run it and see, which is faster and is also
  why a generation of developers cannot tell you why their code works, only that
  it does.
  :::
::

That habit is worth stealing, and this course keeps making you use it. Reading a
query and predicting its result before running it is a 1965 discipline and it is
the one interviewers test.

## Why the software was free

IBM sold machines. Software was support material, like a manual. It came with
source code, and customers modified it and shared the modifications, because
nobody had yet had the idea that the instructions themselves could be the
product.

The idea arrived in 1969, when IBM unbundled software from hardware under
pressure from an antitrust suit. That is the moment a software industry becomes
possible, and therefore the moment your job becomes possible.

::side-note{kind="go-deeper"}
The free-sharing culture of that era did not disappear when software became a
product. It went underground, and came back as free and open-source software,
which is what almost every tool in this course is. You will install roughly forty
pieces of software before the end of it and pay for none of them.
::

## What you can do now

You can answer "why does COBOL still have jobs" and "why was software ever free"
without hand-waving, and you have the one habit worth taking from the era:
predict, then run.

## Your turn

Find out one thing: what does your own bank's mobile app most likely talk to, at
the very back? You will not get a certain answer, and the searching is the
exercise. Write down what you found and how confident you are.

## Check yourself

1. Why did programmers in 1965 read their code so carefully before running it?
2. **An interviewer asks: "why do old systems survive so long?"** Answer in two
   sentences, using cost rather than nostalgia.

## The interview question this answers

**"Why do companies not just rewrite their old systems?"**

Because a working system encodes twenty years of decisions nobody wrote down, and
a rewrite has to rediscover all of them while the old one keeps running. Cost,
risk and the fact that the requirements live in the code rather than in a
document. Saying that shows you have thought about software as something that is
maintained rather than something that is written.
