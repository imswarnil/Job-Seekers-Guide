A machine now writes code faster than you and is confidently wrong roughly one
time in ten. Both halves of that sentence matter, and most advice you will read
only believes one of them.

## Why this matters in a job

Because this is the actual state of the market you are entering, and being
wrong about it in either direction is expensive. If you believe the job is gone,
you will not prepare. If you believe nothing has changed, you will prepare for
2019.

## What genuinely changed

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-zap" title="Producing syntax is close to free"}
  "Write a Java method that filters applicants above a cutoff" is nine seconds of
  work. This used to be most of a first-year developer's day.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-door-closed" title="The bottom rung got higher"}
  Work that used to be given to freshers because it was simple is now the work
  that is easiest to automate. Fewer roles exist for "can write basic code and
  nothing else".
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-gauge" title="Learning got much faster"}
  You can ask unlimited stupid questions at three in the morning with no shame.
  For somebody teaching themselves alone, with no senior nearby, this is the
  single biggest advantage anyone in my position has ever had.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-scan-eye" title="Reviewing became the core skill"}
  Somebody has to decide whether the generated thing is right. That somebody is
  paid, and it is the job.
  :::
::

## What did not change at all

The four things underneath. None of them are produced by a model, because none of
them are in the text it was trained on.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="A model has no access to any of these, and all four are learnable."}
  :::flow-step{label="Knowing what should happen" icon="i-lucide-help-circle"}
  Whether a withdrawn applicant belongs in the average. Whether a rejected
  applicant can reapply next intake. These are decisions made by a registrar in a
  room, and no amount of training data contains them.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Knowing whether the answer is right" icon="i-lucide-shield-check" highlight}
  The generated query runs. It returns a number. Judging that number is the
  whole job, and you cannot judge what you do not understand.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Holding a system in your head" icon="i-lucide-network"}
  Why the dashboard is slow, when the slowness is in a database index four layers
  away from the screen that is slow.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Being answerable" icon="i-lucide-user-check"}
  When it is wrong, a person is asked why. "The model wrote it" ends a career.
  :::
::

## Watch it be confidently wrong

This is the part you have to see rather than be told. Here is a request of the
kind people make daily, and a completely typical answer.

**Asked:** "Write SQL to find the average score of applicants in the 2026 intake."

```sql [generated.sql]
SELECT AVG(score) AS average_score
FROM Application
WHERE intake = '2026';
```

It is syntactically perfect. It runs. It returns `72.4`. And it is wrong in three
separate ways that the model has no way to know about.

::code-trace{caption="Three questions the machine cannot answer, because the answers live in a registrar's office."}
```sql [generated.sql]
SELECT AVG(score) AS average_score
FROM Application
WHERE intake = '2026';
```

  :::code-trace-step{lines="1" caption="AVG silently ignores rows where score is NULL. Two hundred applicants have not been marked yet. They are not in the number, and nothing on the screen says so."}
  :::
  :::code-trace-step{lines="2" caption="Application, not Applicant. One person applying to three programmes is counted three times, so the average leans toward people who applied a lot. Which of the two the dean means is a question, not a fact."}
  :::
  :::code-trace-step{lines="3" caption="Nothing here excludes withdrawn applications. Whether a March withdrawal still counts is a policy decision somebody in the university has to make."}
  :::
::

Now the version that is defensible, which is what you are actually paid for.

```sql [checked.sql]
SELECT COUNT(*)                                   AS applications_counted,
       COUNT(DISTINCT a.applicant_id)             AS distinct_applicants,
       ROUND(AVG(a.score), 1)                     AS average_score
FROM Application AS a
WHERE a.intake = '2026'
  AND a.score IS NOT NULL
  AND a.status <> 'WITHDRAWN';
```

```text [Result]
 applications_counted | distinct_applicants | average_score
----------------------+---------------------+---------------
                 1840 |                1602 |          72.4
```

Same average. Completely different claim, because now the screen can say "72.4,
from 1,840 marked applications by 1,602 applicants, excluding withdrawals".

::real-life{title="What the review actually catches" source="Any team using these tools daily"}
The generated code is usually fine. The problem is that "usually" is not a
standard anybody can run a university on, and the failures are not random: they
cluster exactly where the business rules live, because that is the part that was
never in the training data. So the review is not proofreading. It is the only
place the domain enters the system.
::

## What confuses people here

**"So I should not use it."** No. Use it constantly. I would have saved a year
with it. The rule is narrow and non-negotiable: *never accept output you could
not have written yourself, in a language you cannot read.* The moment you do,
you have stopped being able to tell right from wrong, which is the one thing you
are for.

**"So I should skip the fundamentals and learn to prompt."** This is the
expensive version of the same error. Prompting is a small skill on top of
judgement. Without the layer underneath, you are a person who can request code
and not evaluate it, and that person is now worth very little, because requesting
is the free part.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
The honest risk, stated plainly: if you learn this way, you can reach month six
feeling productive and be unable to answer a basic question about why your own
code works. Interviews find this immediately, because they ask you to explain,
not to produce. Every lesson in this course is written to protect you from it.
::

## What you can do now

You can state what the machine is good at, what it is bad at, and why the second
list is the job. That is a genuinely strong answer to the question every fresher
is now asked.

## Your turn

Ask any model to write a Java method that decides whether an applicant is
eligible for a scholarship. Give it no rules at all.

Then write down every assumption it invented.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What you will find, roughly" icon="i-lucide-list-checks"}
  It will invent a cutoff, usually 75 or 80. It will invent an attendance
  threshold. It will probably assume income is a field it can read and that
  higher score always wins. All of those are policy, set by a university, written
  in a document, and it has made them up fluently.

  That is the exercise. Not "the model is bad". It did what you asked. The point
  is that the thing you did not specify is precisely the thing you are paid to
  know, and it will fill the gap without telling you it has.
  :::
::

::ai-prompt{title="The loop that turns reading into knowing" model="Any"}
I am learning [topic] for entry-level interviews. Here is my explanation of it in
my own words: [your explanation]. Do not rewrite it. Tell me which parts are
wrong, which parts are vague, and ask me the three follow-up questions an
interviewer would ask after this answer.

  #why
  It asks for a critique, not an answer. A critique tells you what your
  understanding was missing; an answer gives you somebody else's words to repeat,
  which is what the interviewer is testing for and will find.
::

## Check yourself

1. Name three things wrong with the generated query, and say why the model could
   not have known about any of them.
2. **An interviewer asks: "do you use AI, and how?"** Answer in a way that shows
   judgement rather than either enthusiasm or denial.

## The interview question this answers

**"How do you use AI in your work?"**

Both easy answers fail. "I do not use it" reads as either dishonest or
inefficient. "I use it for everything" reads as somebody who cannot be trusted
with a review. The answer that works describes a boundary: what you delegate
(boilerplate, a first draft, an unfamiliar syntax, an explanation of somebody
else's code) and what you never delegate (anything you could not have written
yourself, anything encoding a business rule, anything touching data you cannot
afford to be wrong about). Then give one concrete example, and the example is the
part they remember.
