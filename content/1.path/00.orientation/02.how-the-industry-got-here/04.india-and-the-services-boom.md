Now the part that is about you. The market you are about to enter has a specific
history, and once you see it the strange parts of the hiring process stop being
strange.

## Why this matters in a job

Because you are going to be filtered by a process, and it is far easier to pass a
process you understand the purpose of. I failed every first round for four years
without ever asking why the first round was what it was.

## How the market got here

::timeline{label="The short version"}
  :::timeline-item{title="1991 onward" date="Liberalisation" state="done"}
  The economy opens. Satellite links make it possible to do work here for a
  client there.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="1999" date="Y2K" state="done"}
  The world needs an enormous number of programmers to fix date handling in old
  systems, quickly, cheaply. India supplies them. This is the founding moment of
  the services industry, and it was built on maintenance work nobody else wanted.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="2000s" date="Offshoring at scale" state="done"}
  The model generalises: large teams, well-defined work, a rate card. Campus
  hiring in thousands begins, and engineering colleges multiply to supply it.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="2010s" date="Product companies arrive" state="done"}
  Global product companies open real engineering centres here, and Indian
  product companies grow. A second, much smaller, much better-paid market
  appears beside the first.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Now" date="Both, and a squeeze" state="current"}
  Mass hiring has slowed and the bar for the entry-level has risen, because the
  work that used to be a fresher's first year is the work a machine now does
  fastest.
  :::
::

## Why the first round is aptitude and not code

This is the thing I most wish somebody had told me in 2018.

A company visiting a campus may receive 4,000 applications for 60 seats. It has
one day. It cannot read 4,000 pieces of code, and it cannot interview 4,000
people. So the first round is not trying to find good engineers. **It is trying
to remove 3,400 people quickly and defensibly.**

The highlighted row is where I died, every time, for four years. Not the coding
round. The one before it.

::real-life{title="What that round is actually measuring" source="Any mass campus drive"}
Quantitative aptitude, logical reasoning, and written English, under time
pressure. Almost none of it resembles the job. It persists because it is cheap to
mark, hard to challenge, and correlates well enough with "can be trained". It is
a gate, not an assessment, and you pass gates by practising the gate.
::

## What confuses people here

That because round one is not about the job, it is not worth preparing for. That
is exactly backwards, and it is the mistake I made. A gate you do not pass is a
job you do not get, regardless of how good your code is.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
I was rejected in the first written round of every company that visited my
campus, across four years, and I concluded that I was not good enough to be a
developer. What was actually true was narrower and completely fixable: I had
never practised timed arithmetic or written English, and nobody had told me that
those were the wall.
::

This is why the track immediately after this one is English, and why the
interview track at the end treats aptitude as a subject rather than as an
insult.

## The other route, and why this course exists

The funnel above is the campus route. There is a second route, which is the one I
actually took: apply off-campus, to smaller companies, continuously, while
visibly building something. It is slower, lonelier and has no fixed date, and it
does not care what your college was.

::compare{caption="Both are real. Only one of them has a deadline you have already missed."}
  :::compare-side{label="Campus" verdict="neutral"}
  One shot, on a date, against everybody in your year. Filtered on aptitude
  first. If you are reading this before final year, prepare for the gate.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Off-campus" verdict="right"}
  Unlimited attempts, no date, filtered mostly on what you can show and how you
  talk about it. Slower and entirely within your control. This is what the rest
  of this course prepares you for.
  :::
::

## What you can do now

You can name the filter you are standing in front of, which is the first step to
passing it. And you know which two subjects the first filter tests that no
programming course teaches.

## Your turn

Find the actual pattern of one company's first round. Search for the name plus
"aptitude pattern" or "online assessment", and write down the sections, the
number of questions, and the time allowed.

Then work out the seconds per question. That number is the exercise.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Why the seconds-per-question number matters" icon="i-lucide-timer"}
  It is usually between 45 and 90 seconds. At that speed you are not solving
  problems, you are recognising shapes you have seen before. That is a
  practisable skill and it is nothing like intelligence, which is the most
  useful thing I can tell you about the round that beat me eleven times.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why does the first round test aptitude rather than programming?
2. **An interviewer asks: "why should we hire you when you have no experience?"**
   Use the funnel to work out what they are actually worried about.

## The interview question this answers

**"Why should we hire a fresher?"**

What they are worried about is the cost of training somebody who then cannot do
the work or leaves. So the answer is evidence of being trainable and of finishing
things: something you built, alone, that works, that you can explain end to end.
That is precisely why this course builds one system for its whole length rather
than twenty tutorials.
