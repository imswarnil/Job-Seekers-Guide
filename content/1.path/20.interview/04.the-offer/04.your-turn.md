Thirty-five terms and four rounds you could describe. This is the only exercise
in the track that changes outcomes, and it is the one people skip because it is
uncomfortable.

::pull-quote
Everything you have read about interviews is knowledge. An interview is a
performance, and nobody has ever improved at a performance by reading about one.
::

## Set it up

::steps
### Block ninety minutes
Four rounds back to back, as a real loop would be. The fatigue is part of what
you are practising for.

### Record every round
Audio is enough. You will not want to listen, and listening is where the value
is.

### Do it alone if you have to
A friend is better. Somebody in the industry is better still. Alone is far
better than not at all, and "I could not find anybody" is the reason most people
never do this.

### No pausing, no restarting
When it goes badly, keep going. Recovering from a bad answer is the specific
skill being practised.
::

## Round one: the screen (10 minutes)

Answer out loud, in order, with no notes.

1. Tell me about yourself.
2. Why do you want to work in software?
3. Why are you applying here?
4. What are you looking for in a first role?
5. What is your notice period and expected salary?

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="Listen back for these five" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  1. **Was question 1 under ninety seconds?** Most first attempts run to three
     minutes and lose the room.
  2. **Did a technology appear before a person did?** It should not.
  3. **Did you say "basically" or "kind of" more than twice?** Those appear
     exactly where you are describing something you do not fully own, which
     makes them a map of what to go and understand.
  4. **On question 5, did you name a number first?** You should have asked for
     their band first.
  5. **Did you say "I am a quick learner"?** Cut it. It carries no information
     and every candidate says it.
  :::
::

## Round two: coding (30 minutes)

Pick a problem you have not seen. Set a timer. **Narrate continuously**, even
when stuck, especially when stuck.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What is actually being scored" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  Not whether you finished. Listen back for:

  - **Did you ask a clarifying question before writing?** Are duplicates
    possible, can the input be empty, how large does this get. Silence here is
    the most common failure in the round.
  - **Did you state a brute-force approach before optimising?** "The obvious
    answer is nested loops at O(n²). Let me see whether a set makes it O(n)."
    That sentence alone passes a lot of interviews.
  - **Did you go quiet for more than fifteen seconds?** Anywhere you did, an
    interviewer learned nothing. Say what you are considering, even if it is
    "I am trying to work out whether I need to sort this first".
  - **Did you test it?** Out loud, with a real input, including the empty case
    and the boundary. Almost nobody does this and it is free marks.
  :::
::

## Round three: your project (20 minutes)

Two-minute walkthrough, then answer these six, out loud.

1. Why did you choose that architecture?
2. How is it secured?
3. What was the hardest part?
4. How would you scale it?
5. What would you do differently?
6. Show me the code.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="The three things to check" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **Did you argue exactly one decision, or list five features?** One with its
  rejected alternative is evidence. Five is a brochure.

  **Did you name a cost anywhere?** If every answer was about benefits, it
  sounds like a tutorial rather than a system you built.

  **Where did you get vague?** There will be a spot. That is the part you
  copied rather than understood, and it is where a real interviewer will push,
  because vagueness is audible. Write it down and go and learn it properly.
  :::
::

## Round four: HR (15 minutes)

1. Tell me about a time you failed.
2. Tell me about a disagreement with somebody.
3. Where do you see yourself in five years?
4. Why should we hire you over somebody with more experience?
5. Do you have any questions for us?

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What to listen for" icon="i-lucide-check"}
  **Question 1:** was it a real failure with a consequence, or a disguised
  strength? "I work too hard" ends the round badly.

  **Question 2:** did you blame the other person at any point? Listen carefully.
  It creeps in through tone rather than words.

  **Question 4:** did you apologise for your experience? Do not. Point at the
  thing you built and what you can explain about it.

  **Question 5:** did you have three questions ready? If you said "no, I think
  you covered everything", that is a wasted opportunity and it reads as not
  caring.
  :::
::

## The scoring that matters

Not out of ten. Three honest answers, written down:

::feature-list{columns="3"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-volume-2" title="Where did I go quiet?"}
  Silence is the only universal failure across all four rounds. Every gap is
  a place to practise saying something rather than knowing something.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-help-circle" title="Where did I get vague?"}
  That is what you do not actually understand. It is a reading list, and it is
  usually short.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-repeat" title="What did I say twice?"}
  A phrase you lean on is a crutch. "Basically", "at the end of the day",
  "kind of". Cut them and the same content sounds twice as confident.
  :::
::

## Then do it again

::note
The second run is always better and the third is the one you will actually give.
This is not because you learned anything new between them. It is because the gap
between knowing something and saying it out loud is enormous, entirely invisible
until you try, and closes with repetition rather than study.
::

## The honest note

I did not do this before my first interviews. I read about interviews, felt
prepared, and then discovered in the room that I could not say out loud things I
definitely knew. That happened several times before I worked out what the
problem was.

::pull-quote
Nobody is rejected for not knowing what a HashMap is. They are rejected for
going quiet for twenty seconds while they work out how to explain it.
::
