A number arrives with six components and two days to respond. This lesson is the
one I most needed and least had, and the mistake it prevents followed me for
three years.

## Why this matters in a job

Because your next salary is very often calculated from this one, and because the
component breakdown is where a large offer becomes a modest one.

::real-life{title="The number that followed me" source="2019 to 2022"}
I accepted 1.8 LPA because it was the only offer and I was delighted with it,
and I was right to take it. What I did not know was that the next three
conversations would all start from it. Accenture's 3.6 was calculated as roughly
double, and the one after that from there. It took a deliberate reset in 2022 to
break out of a ladder whose first rung had been set by somebody with no
alternatives.
::

## What a CTC actually contains

"Cost to company" is what you cost them, not what you receive. The gap is
frequently 25 to 35 per cent.

```text [A 12 LPA offer, taken apart]
Basic salary                      480,000     40%, everything else is a % of this
House rent allowance              240,000     50% of basic, partly tax-free with rent
Special allowance                 264,000     the balancing figure
Provident fund, employer          57,600      12% of basic. Yours, at retirement.
Gratuity                          23,088      only after 5 years. Often never received.
Performance bonus                 120,000     "up to". Ask what was actually paid.
Insurance premium                 15,312      a real benefit, and not money
                                ---------
CTC                             1,200,000

Monthly in hand, roughly:          72,000     ← after PF, professional tax and income tax
                                               NOT 100,000
```

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-wallet" title="Fixed against variable"}
  The performance bonus is the line to interrogate. "What percentage of that was
  actually paid out last year, across the team?" A straight answer is a good
  sign; a vague one is the answer.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-lock" title="Money you cannot spend yet"}
  Provident fund is yours and is not this month. Gratuity needs five years and
  most people leave before then, so treating it as part of your salary is
  optimistic arithmetic.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-gift" title="A joining bonus is counted once"}
  It inflates year one's CTC and vanishes in year two, so the same job appears
  to pay less next year. Ask whether it is clawed back if you leave within a
  year, because it usually is.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-trending-up" title="Stock is not money until it is"}
  RSUs at a listed company are close to cash on a schedule. Options at a private
  startup are a lottery ticket with a tax event attached. They are quoted as
  though they are the same instrument.
  :::
::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
**Never accept a verbal number.** Ask for the offer letter with the components
listed. I have seen a "9 LPA" offer where 1.5 of it was a bonus nobody in the
team had received in full, and the candidate found out in month fourteen.
::

## The one question to ask, always

> *"Could you share the component-wise breakup of the CTC, and what the actual
> payout on the variable component was last year?"*

Entirely normal, entirely professional, and it takes the conversation from a
headline to a fact. Nobody has ever withdrawn an offer over it.

## Negotiating when you have no leverage

The honest situation for most first jobs: one offer, no alternative, and rent due.

::pros-cons
---
title: Negotiating a first offer with no competing offer
pros:
  - The worst realistic outcome is "no, this is the band", offers are not withdrawn for asking politely
  - Even a small increase compounds, because the next offer is often calculated from this one
  - Asking well signals that you value yourself, which is noted rather than penalised
cons:
  - Your actual leverage is low and pretending otherwise is transparent
  - A large ask with nothing behind it can read as unrealistic
  - In a structured campus band, the number genuinely cannot move
---
::

### What to say

```text [If there is any flexibility]
"Thank you, I am glad to have the offer and I want to accept.

Before I do: based on what I have seen for backend roles at this level in
Bangalore, I was hoping for something closer to X. Is there any flexibility on
the fixed component?"
```

Three things are doing work there. You said you want to accept, so there is no
threat. You anchored on the **market and the role**, not on your current salary
or your needs. And you named **fixed**, because the variable is where a
negotiation gets quietly settled with money you may not receive.

```text [If the number genuinely cannot move]
"Understood, thank you. Two other things then: is the joining date flexible by
two weeks, and would you be open to a formal review at six months rather than
twelve?"
```

Almost everything except the number is negotiable and nobody asks. A six-month
review, the start date, the team you join, a training budget, equipment,
remote days. Several of those are worth more than the ₹40,000 you did not get.

::tip
Never negotiate on need. "I need more because my rent is high" invites sympathy
and not money, and it tells them your floor. Negotiate on **market and role**:
"for this role at this level in this city." That is a fact about the job rather
than a fact about you.
::

## The question you will be asked first

"What are your current or expected salary expectations?"

::compare{caption="Two answers, and the first one sets your ceiling."}
  :::compare-side{label="A number, too early" verdict="wrong"}
  "I am expecting around 5 LPA."

  If their band was 8 to 12, you have just been offered 5.5 and everybody is
  pleased. This exact mistake is why my first three salaries were what they
  were.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Deferred, once" verdict="right"}
  "I would rather understand the role properly first. Could you tell me the band
  you have budgeted for this position, and I can say whether that works?"

  If pushed, give a **range**, anchored to market rather than to your current
  pay: "for this role in Bangalore I have seen 8 to 12, and I would be
  comfortable in that range."
  :::
::

::note
Deferring works once, politely. If they insist a second time, give the range
rather than appearing evasive. And as a fresher with genuinely no market data,
*"I am flexible, and I would like to understand the component-wise breakup"* is
an honest answer that does not set a low anchor.
::

## Judging the offer itself

The number is one of five things, and at the start of a career it is not the most
important one.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-users" title="Who will you learn from"}
  Ask directly: "who would review my code, and how long have they been doing
  this?" A first job with nobody senior nearby is expensive in a way no salary
  compensates for.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-code" title="What will you actually do"}
  "What would my first three months look like?" A vague answer usually means
  nobody has thought about it, and you will be given whatever needs a body.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-building" title="Which of the three models"}
  Service, product or startup, from the orientation track. It decides how fast
  your salary can move afterwards, which matters more than the starting number.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-door-open" title="What does it lead to"}
  A service-company role that gets you in the door is worth taking. Just take it
  knowing that the move is the raise, not the appraisal.
  :::
::

::pull-quote
Take the offer you have. Then get good, and move deliberately rather than
waiting. The first job is a door, and the mistake is not taking a small one, it
is standing in it for three years.
::

## What you can do now

You can read a CTC breakdown, ask the one question that turns a headline into a
fact, and negotiate without leverage in a way that costs nothing and occasionally
works.

## Your turn

Take this offer apart. What do you receive monthly, what would you ask about, and
what would you negotiate?

```text [Offer]
Congratulations. We are pleased to offer you the position of Software Engineer
at a CTC of ₹9,00,000 per annum.

  Basic                        3,00,000
  HRA                          1,50,000
  Special allowance            1,80,000
  Provident fund (employer)      36,000
  Gratuity                       14,400
  Performance bonus            1,50,000
  Joining bonus                  50,000
  Insurance                      19,600
```

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="What this offer actually is" icon="i-lucide-calculator"}
  **The guaranteed cash is ₹6,30,000**, basic plus HRA plus special allowance.
  Everything else is conditional, deferred, one-off or not money.

  **In hand, roughly ₹45,000 a month** after employee PF, professional tax and
  income tax. Not ₹75,000.

  **Year two is a pay cut on paper.** The ₹50,000 joining bonus does not recur,
  so the same job is an 8.5 LPA offer next year unless something increases.

  **The performance bonus is 17% of the CTC**, which is high. That is the number
  to ask about: what percentage was actually paid last year, across the team?
  If the answer is 60%, this is really a 7.4 LPA offer.

  **Gratuity needs five years.** Statistically you will not be there, so treat it
  as zero.

  **What to ask:** the actual variable payout last year; whether the joining
  bonus is clawed back if you leave within twelve months; and when the first
  review is.

  **What to negotiate:** move money from the variable into the fixed component,
  even at the same total CTC. That is often possible when raising the total is
  not, it is worth real money, and almost nobody asks for it.
  :::
::

## Check yourself

1. Why is the fixed component the one to negotiate rather than the total CTC?
2. **An interviewer asks: "what are your salary expectations?"** What do you say
   first, and what do you say if they insist?

## The interview question this answers

**"What are your salary expectations?"**

Defer once, politely, and ask for their band. If pushed, give a range anchored to
the market for the role and the city rather than to your current pay. As a
fresher with no data, "I am flexible, and I would like to understand the
component-wise breakup" is honest and sets no anchor.

The mistake to avoid is naming a low number early, because the next employer
frequently calculates from it. That is not a theory; it is what happened to me
for three years.
