You have written code on a shared screen and talked through it. The next
conversation is about the thing you built, and it is the one candidates
under-prepare because it feels like the easy part.

## Why this matters in a job

It is not the easy part. It is the round where the offer is usually decided,
because it is the only one where the interviewer can find out whether you
understand what you did or assembled it from answers.

And you are the worst possible person to judge your own explanation. You know
the system too well, which means you skip the sentence that would have made the
rest make sense.

## What is actually being tested

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-anchor" title="Depth on one thing"}
  Anybody can list five features. The person who made a decision can explain
  what they rejected and why. They will pick one thing and push on it until you
  either get more precise or get vague, and which of those happens is the
  result.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-scale" title="Whether you know the cost"}
  Every choice traded something away. A candidate who names the cost unprompted
  is describing engineering. One who only names benefits is describing a
  tutorial.
  :::
::

## The shape of every answer

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="Four parts. It works for any technical decision, and it is worth practising until it is automatic."}
  :::flow-step{title="The constraint"}
  What was true that made this a decision at all. "One developer, a few thousand
  users, every question is a join."
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="The choice" highlight}
  What you did, in one sentence, with no hedging.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="The rejected alternative"}
  What else you considered and the specific reason it lost. This is the part
  that cannot be faked.
  :::
  :::flow-step{title="The expiry"}
  When your decision stops being right. Almost nobody says this, and it is the
  most senior-sounding sentence available.
  :::
::

## The eight that come

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. Walk me through the architecture." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Start with the constraint, not the diagram. One developer, a few thousand
  users, every registrar question is a join, and the data is confidential per
  row. Then the four decisions fall out of that constraint rather than out of
  fashion.

  Do not list your dependencies. Nobody has ever been hired for the contents of
  a `package.json`.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. Why did you use X instead of Y?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  The four-part shape, every time. And if the honest answer is "it was what the
  course used", say a version of that and then show you went and found out:
  "that was the default I started from, and when I looked at the alternative
  later the thing that would have changed my mind was..."

  Pretending a default was a decision is transparent and it is worse than
  admitting it.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. What was the hardest part?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Never "learning the framework". Pick something with a name.

  Two admins accepting the last seat at the same moment. It is a **lost update**,
  every major database allows it at its default isolation level, and I used a
  conditional update with the row count checked because clashes are rare, with a
  `CHECK` constraint underneath so a script cannot bypass it.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="4. How is it secured?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Authorisation as row-level policies on the tables, not checks in route
  handlers, so a forgotten check returns nothing rather than everything. Then
  the failure mode: it **fails closed**, and too little data gets reported as a
  bug while too much gets reported as a breach.

  Then prepared statements, server-side validation of everything including ids
  that came from a list I supplied, and rate limiting on the public form.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="5. How would you scale it?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Refuse first, then plan. At this size there is no scaling problem and most of
  what I could add is complexity with nothing measured behind it. If it grew, I
  would measure first, and my guess is the reporting queries hurt before
  anything else.

  "I would measure first", said with conviction, beats any architecture.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="6. What would you do differently?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  A decision, not a bug. I designed the schema before writing the access matrix,
  and I would do it the other way round now, because working out who is allowed
  to read what would have changed two tables.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="7. Did anyone use it?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  If somebody did, this is your best material: a lecturer told me the attendance
  register was slower than paper and she had gone back to paper.

  If nobody did, say so and say what you would learn from putting it in front of
  one person. Inventing users is the worst possible risk to take, because the
  follow-up questions are endless and specific.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="8. Show me the code." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Have the repository open before the call starts, on the file you want to
  discuss. Pick the file with a real decision in it: the transaction, the
  policies, the validation.

  Never open the file you are least proud of to get ahead of it. They were not
  going to find it.
  :::
::

## The answer to what you do not know

This comes up in every technical round and it is worth rehearsing, because the
instinct is to guess and the guess is always detected.

::compare{caption="The same gap, two responses."}
  :::compare-side{label="Guessing" verdict="wrong"}
  "I think it probably uses some kind of caching layer, and that would help with
  the performance, because, basically, it depends on..."

  Three hedges in one sentence. The interviewer now doubts the things you did
  know, which is the real cost.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Bounding it" verdict="right"}
  "I do not know. What I do know is the adjacent thing: I know how the index
  makes this fast, and I have never had to make it faster than that. If I needed
  to, I would start with the slow query log rather than guessing."

  Admits the gap, proves the neighbouring knowledge, names a method. This
  reliably scores better than a correct guess.
  :::
::

::pull-quote
"I do not know" followed by "here is how I would find out" is a complete answer.
It is also the only answer that cannot be caught out by the next question.
::

## Preparing properly

::steps
### Reread your own decision record
`docs/decisions.md` from the build track. It is four entries and it is the whole
round.

### Pick the one decision you will steer toward
You get to influence what gets discussed. Have one you can go five questions
deep on.

### Find the part you could not rebuild
There is always one. That is where your answers go vague, and it is where they
will push, because vagueness is audible.

### Say it all out loud, once
Alone, timed. The gap between knowing and saying is enormous and invisible until
you try.
::

## What you can do now

You can defend a system rather than describe it: the constraint, the choice, the
rejected alternative, the expiry, and an honest answer when the question goes
past what you know.

## Check yourself

1. Why does naming when your decision stops being right make it sound stronger
   rather than weaker?
2. **An interviewer asks something you genuinely do not know.** Say your answer
   out loud, now.

## The interview question this answers

All eight above, and the meta-question underneath them: **do you understand what
you built?**

The only reliable preparation is having actually decided things, which is why
the build track made you write the decision record. A candidate who opens a file
and says "I wrote this down at the time, here is what I was worried about" has
ended the question.
