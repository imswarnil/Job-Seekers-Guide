The code was right and the decisions held. This room contains somebody who will
not ask a technical question at all, and candidates who sail through the coding
round lose the offer here more often than anybody admits.

## Why this matters in a job

Because the HR round is not a formality, and treating it as one is visible. It
is deciding two things: whether you are a person the team can work beside, and
whether you will still be here in a year.

I have been rejected after a technical round I know I did well in. Nobody tells
you which round lost it, which is why people conclude they were not good enough
at programming and prepare for the wrong thing. I did that for two years.

## The rule for all of it

::pull-quote
Adjectives are free and nobody believes them. "I am a good team player" costs
nothing to say and carries no information. A specific thing that happened, with
a person and an outcome in it, cannot be invented on the spot and is therefore
the only thing that counts.
::

::compare{caption="The same claim, twice."}
  :::compare-side{label="Adjectives" verdict="wrong"}
  "I am hardworking, a quick learner, and I work well in a team."

  Every candidate says this. It is not disbelieved so much as not heard.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Evidence" verdict="right"}
  "I built the attendance screen the obvious way, a lecturer told me it was
  slower than paper and she had gone back to paper, and I rebuilt it so
  everybody is present by default and you only tap the absentees."

  Nobody else has this story, because it happened to you.
  :::
::

## The six

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. Tell me about yourself." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Ninety seconds, three parts, and it is **not** your biography.

  Where you are now and what you can do. One thing you built, with the problem
  in front of it. Why this role specifically.

  *"I am a backend developer working in Java and Postgres. The thing I would
  point at is a university admissions system I built end to end, replacing a
  shared spreadsheet four people were editing at once. I am applying here
  because the role is backend on a real data model rather than gluing services
  together, and that is the part I am actually good at."*

  What to leave out: school, your hometown, your family, and the phrase "I am a
  quick learner".
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. Why do you want to work here?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  The question is testing whether you have read anything about them.

  Name something specific: a product, a problem they have publicly, the shape of
  the engineering. "You are hiring backend engineers to work on the payments
  data model, and the thing I spent eight weeks on was a schema where the
  constraints carried the business rules."

  "It is a great company with good growth opportunities" is a wrong answer, and
  it is the most common one.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. Tell me about a time you failed." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  The trap is a disguised strength. "I work too hard" ends the conversation
  badly.

  Real failure, real consequence, real change, and **do not spend long on the
  redemption**.

  *"I built a fee report that assumed a fee was paid once. About 40% were paid
  in two instalments, so it showed roughly half the money collected, and it was
  used in a meeting before anybody noticed. I had never asked how fees were
  actually paid. Now I ask what the exceptions are before I write anything that
  produces a number."*

  One sentence of what changed is enough. Three sentences of it sounds
  rehearsed.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="4. Tell me about a conflict." icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  They are checking whether you make it about the person. Never make it about
  the person.

  Disagreement about a decision, what each side wanted, how it resolved, and
  what you would do differently. Best version: one where you were wrong, or
  where you did not get your way and it was fine.

  *"I wanted to put the validation in the database and somebody more senior
  wanted it in the application, for testability. We tried both on one table. His
  tests were genuinely faster to write. We kept the database constraints for the
  rules that must never be violated and put the rest where he wanted it."*
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="5. Where do you see yourself in five years?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Nobody knows, and everybody knows nobody knows. What is being tested is
  whether your answer is compatible with the job.

  A direction is enough: deeper on backend and data, eventually the person
  people ask about the schema. Say the next step, not a title.

  Do not say you want to start your own company. It is a fine thing to want and
  it answers their real question, "will you be here in a year", with a no.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="6. Do you have any questions for us?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Never say no. It reads as not caring, and it is the easiest question on the
  list.

  Three that are always good, because the answers actually matter to you:

  - "What does the first three months look like for whoever takes this role?"
  - "What is the thing about the codebase that surprises new people?"
  - "How does work get decided? Is it a roadmap, tickets, or something else?"

  That second one gets an honest answer more often than any other question I
  have asked, and it tells you a great deal about what you would be walking
  into.
  :::
::

## The salary question

Asked early, often first, and answered badly by almost everybody.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Whoever names a number first has given away information. You want them to name
it, and you will often not get that, so have both answers ready.
::

::tabs
  :::tabs-item{label="If you can deflect" icon="i-lucide-shield"}
  *"I would rather understand the role properly first. Do you have a band for
  this position?"*

  Most companies do have one and many will tell you. Asked politely and once,
  this is normal and nobody is offended.
  :::
  :::tabs-item{label="If they insist" icon="i-lucide-hash"}
  Give a **range**, informed by what you have found out, with the top of it
  slightly above your real target.

  *"Based on what I have seen for backend roles at this level, I am looking in
  the range of X to Y, and it depends on the rest of the package."*

  Never say "whatever you think is fair" or "as per company standards". Both
  reliably produce the bottom of the band.
  :::
  :::tabs-item{label="If you have no leverage" icon="i-lucide-scale"}
  This is the honest case for most first jobs, and pretending otherwise is
  worse.

  You can still ask for the band, still give a range, and still not accept
  immediately. What you cannot do is bluff about a competing offer you do not
  have, because it is checked more often than people think and it ends
  everything.
  :::
::

::note
Research the band before the call. Ask people a year ahead of you, look at
levels for that company and city, and have a number in your head. Walking in
without one is how you agree to something in the moment and regret it for two
years, because the next raise is a percentage of it.
::

## Preparing without sounding rehearsed

::steps
### Write four stories, not six answers
The build that worked, the thing that failed, the disagreement, the time you
were told you were wrong. Most behavioural questions are one of these four
wearing a different hat.

### Say each one out loud, timed
Ninety seconds. If it runs to three minutes, cut the setup, not the outcome.

### Never memorise wording
Memorised wording is audible and it collapses the moment they ask a follow-up.
Memorise the **events**; the words can be different every time.

### Prepare your three questions
And write them down. You will forget them when asked.
::

## What you can do now

You can answer six questions with evidence instead of adjectives, handle the
salary question without naming a number first, and ask three questions whose
answers you actually want.

## Check yourself

1. Why does "I am a quick learner" carry no information, and what would you say
   instead?
2. **Say "tell me about yourself" out loud, timed, right now.** Ninety seconds.

## The interview question this answers

All six, and the one underneath: **is this somebody the team will be glad to
have?**

You cannot answer that directly. What you can do is be specific, be honest about
a failure, avoid blaming anybody, and ask a question that shows you were
listening. Those four things together are what a good answer to this round looks
like, and none of them requires you to be a different person than you are.
