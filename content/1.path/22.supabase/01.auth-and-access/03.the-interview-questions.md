description: Five questions on authentication and access, including the vulnerability that is the most common serious flaw in a student portal.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. Authentication or authorisation?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Who you are, against what you may do. 401 against 403, and 401 is badly named
  because it means unauthenticated.

  Then the sentence that matters more than the definitions: authentication
  happens once per session, authorisation happens on **every request, for every
  row**. Most junior systems authenticate carefully and then authorise once, in
  the interface.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="2. How would you store passwords?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  A hash from bcrypt, scrypt or argon2, deliberately **slow**, which is the
  point, with a salt, which bcrypt includes in its output. Compare the hash;
  never the password.

  And say the negative: never SHA-256 or MD5, because they are designed to be
  fast and can be brute-forced at billions of guesses a second. A leaked
  database of bcrypt hashes is an incident; a leaked database of SHA-256 hashes
  is a list of passwords, because people reuse them.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="3. A student changes the id in the URL and sees another student's marks. What happened?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Insecure direct object reference. The screen only ever links to your own
  record, so nobody tested changing the number, and the endpoint checks that you
  are logged in without checking that the record is yours.

  The fix is not another `if` at that endpoint, because there will be six more
  endpoints. It is a row-level policy in the database, so a forgotten `WHERE`
  leaks nothing.

  And the detail that marks the answer out: return **404**, not 403, so the
  response does not confirm that a record with that id exists.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="4. What is row-level security and why bother?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  A policy in the database that filters rows by who is connected, so
  `SELECT * FROM ExamResult` returns different rows for different users.

  The reason is scope: a permission in your code protects you from your code. It
  does nothing about the import script, the analytics query, or the second
  application written against the same database in two years. A permission in
  the database protects you from all three.
  :::

  :::accordion-item{label="5. What would you not build yourself?" icon="i-lucide-message-circle"}
  Password hashing, session management and OAuth. There is no version of those
  where your own is better, and every subtlety in them is a vulnerability you
  would have to rediscover.

  Then what **is** yours, which is the point of the question: the authorisation
  rules. Who may shortlist for which programme, who may see which student's
  marks. Nobody can write those for you because they are your domain, and that
  is where your attention belongs.
  :::
::
