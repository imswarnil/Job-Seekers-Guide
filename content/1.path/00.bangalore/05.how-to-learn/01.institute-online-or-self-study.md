You came to learn. The first decision is how: pay a training institute, learn
from online courses, or teach yourself from free material. This page compares
all three honestly and tells you which one I chose and why.

## Why it matters

This decision is the biggest money decision after rent, and the biggest time
decision of the whole year. Choose badly and you lose months: either paying for
classes that do not suit you, or drifting through free videos that never add up
to a job.

I tried self-study first. It did not work, and I know exactly why, which is the
most useful thing I can tell you on this page.

## The three options

::tabs
:::tabs-item{label="Training institute"}
A classroom course in the city, usually Java or testing plus SQL and web basics,
taught by trainers in batches, with a placement cell that shares walk-in and
interview openings. Bangalore has many, clustered in BTM, Marathahalli,
Rajajinagar and a few other areas.

**Cost:** a one-time fee, typically in the tens of thousands of rupees for a
full course when I went. Check each one.

**Time:** about three months for the core course. Mine was about three months.
:::
:::tabs-item{label="Online courses"}
Recorded or live courses on platforms like Udemy, Coursera, NPTEL or a paid
bootcamp. You study from your room at your own pace.

**Cost:** from free (NPTEL, YouTube) to a few hundred rupees in a Udemy sale, to
tens of thousands for a live online bootcamp.

**Time:** as long as you take. That is the good news and the problem.
:::
:::tabs-item{label="Self-study"}
Books, free documentation, YouTube and practice sites, in an order you decide
yourself.

**Cost:** close to nothing.

**Time:** usually the longest, because nobody is sequencing it for you.
:::
::

## The honest trade-offs

::pros-cons
---
title: A training institute
pros:
  - Somebody has already decided the order, and that is most of the value
  - A fixed timetable forces you to turn up every day
  - Doubts get answered by a person, in the room, the same day
  - Placement cells share walk-in drives and interview calls
  - You study alongside people doing the same thing
cons:
  - The fee is real money, often from a family that does not have it
  - Batches can be large, so you get less personal attention
  - Placement support varies a lot, and "placement assistance" is not a job
  - You have to live near it, which means living in the city
---
::

::pros-cons
---
title: Online courses
pros:
  - Cheap or free, and some are excellent
  - Pause, rewind and rewatch the part you did not follow
  - Study from anywhere, including home, which saves rent
cons:
  - Nobody notices if you stop, and most people stop
  - No one to ask when you are stuck on something small for three days
  - No placement cell, no walk-in network
  - Easy to buy five courses and finish none
---
::

::pros-cons
---
title: Self-study
pros:
  - Almost free
  - Builds the habit of finding answers, which you need in any job
  - Complete freedom over order and pace
cons:
  - You cannot tell what you do not know
  - Easy to spend weeks on the wrong thing
  - Needs a foundation to build on, and many of us do not have one
  - Lonely, and slow
---
::

## Why self-study failed for me

::real-life{title="Why my self-study went nowhere" source="Bangalore, 2018"}
After those first interviews showed me I knew nothing, I tried to fix it on my
own. Every tutorial made sense while I was watching it and was gone the moment I
closed the tab. I had never really understood programming in college, so there
was nothing for the self-study to sit on. I could not tell the difference
between "I do not know this yet" and "I misunderstood something three topics
ago", and I had no way to find out which of the twenty things I half-knew was
the one breaking everything else.
::

That is the real test for self-study. If you understood programming in college
and only need revision and interview practice, self-study can work very well. If
you were like me, and the degree did not go in, you need somebody to sequence it
for you, at least at the start.

## Who each one suits

| You are... | Consider | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting close to zero, like me, and able to afford it | An institute | You need the sequence and the daily discipline more than anything else |
| Starting close to zero, with very little money | NPTEL, free YouTube playlists in a fixed order, and a study partner | Borrow the structure from a syllabus; borrow the discipline from a friend |
| Comfortable with basic programming already | Self-study plus an online course for gaps | You need revision and interview practice, not teaching |
| Still at home, not ready to move | Online courses, and move later | Learn the basics for free before paying Bangalore rent |
| Working in a non-IT job, switching | Online, evenings and weekends | You cannot attend a day batch |

## What to do

- [ ] Be honest about your foundation. Could you write a program to find the
      average of five marks, without looking it up, today?
- [ ] If no: plan on an institute, or a very disciplined online route with a
      partner.
- [ ] If yes: consider self-study plus targeted online courses, and save the fee.
- [ ] Whichever you choose, do aptitude and English yourself. No institute
      teaches enough of either.
- [ ] Do not pay any fee until you have sat demo classes. The next page is how.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing by what your friend chose.** Choose by your own foundation.
- **Thinking an institute is a job guarantee.** It is a sequence and a
  timetable. The job is still yours to get.
- **Buying courses instead of finishing them.** One course, finished, beats
  five started.
- **Skipping the evenings.** Institute or not, the CS subjects, aptitude and
  English happen in your own time.

## How to practise

Write one line in your notebook: "My foundation is ___, so I will learn by ___,
and I will know it is working when ___." Fill in the last blank with something
you can check in four weeks, like "I can write a Java program with a loop and a
method without looking anything up".
