You have decided on an institute. This page is how to choose one without
trusting the advert: sit three days of demo classes at several, judge them on
the same list, and only then pay.

## Why it matters

Institutes in Bangalore sell hard. Every board promises placements, every
counsellor has a story about a student now at a big company, and every one has a
discount that ends today. The fee is often the largest single amount your family
will send you all year.

Most institutes let you attend **demo classes** before you pay: real lectures
with a real batch, free, for a few days. Almost nobody uses them properly. They
sit one, like the trainer's voice, and pay. I sat three days at each one I was
considering, and it was the best decision I made that year.

## The three-day demo method

::steps
### Make a shortlist of three or four
Ask seniors, people at your PG, people outside the institutes. Look for names
that keep coming up. Keep the list to institutes you could walk or bus to every
day.

### Ask each for three days of demos
Ask at the front desk: "Can I attend the demo classes for three days before I
decide?" Most will say yes. If one pressures you to pay on day one, write that
down; it is information.

### Sit in the actual batch you would join
Not a special demo for new people if you can avoid it, but the running batch at
the time you would attend. That is the class you are buying.

### Score every day, the same evening
Use the scorecard below. Score while you remember, not at the end of the week.

### Talk to students on day three
Stand outside at the break and ask two or three students in the batch: "Is it
worth it? What is the placement cell really like?" They will tell you.

### Decide, then negotiate
Pick the one with the best score, not the best offer. Then ask about the fee,
instalments and what is included.
::

## What to judge

| What | Why it matters | How to judge it in a demo |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The trainer** | You will spend three months with this person. It matters more than the brand | Do you understand them? Do they explain why, or only what? Do they take questions? |
| **Batch size** | A very large batch means fewer doubts answered | Count the students. Can the trainer see the back row? |
| **Doubt clearing** | Everyone gets stuck; what happens then decides your progress | Ask a question. Is there a lab session, a doubt hour, someone to help after class? |
| **Placement support, really** | This is where claims and reality differ most | Ask students, not the counsellor: how many drives were shared last month, and how many people from the last batch got through? |
| **Mock interviews** | Interview practice is part of what you are paying for | Are there mock interviews? How often? Given by whom? |
| **Fees and what they include** | Hidden extras add up | Ask for the full fee in writing: course, tests, certificates, any "placement fee" |
| **Timings** | You need evenings for CS, aptitude and English | Does the batch time leave you the evening? |
| **Distance** | Every day for three months | Time the walk or bus from your PG |

## What I chose, and why

I looked at several institutes, including **JSpiders** and **QSpiders** (the
same group: JSpiders for development, QSpiders for testing) and others in and
around BTM. I sat three days of demo classes at each, and I chose **JSpiders**
for Java development, where I studied Java, SQL and web technologies for about
three months.

What decided it for me was the teaching in the demo classes: I understood the
Java trainer, and for the first time the language made sense rather than being a
shape to memorise. That is a judgement about one batch in one year. Trainers
move and batches change, so do the demo yourself rather than taking my choice as
a recommendation.

::note
I name the institutes I looked at because you will see their boards and will
wonder. This is not a ranking and not an advertisement. Plenty of people did well
at other institutes, and plenty did badly at mine. The method is the point, not
the name.
::

## What an institute does not do

::caution
- It does not give you a job. "Placement assistance" means they share openings
  and prepare you. You still have to clear the rounds.
- It does not teach enough aptitude or English for most written rounds. Do
  those yourself, every day.
- It does not make you study. Attending is not learning.
- Any institute that promises a **guaranteed** job, or asks for part of your
  future salary, deserves very careful questions. The *Scams and traps* page
  covers this.
::

## A worked example

After three days at three institutes, the totals look like this:

| Institute | Total score (out of 105) | Fee | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 78 | Higher | Trainer excellent, big batch, students positive about drives |
| B | 61 | Lower | Friendly counsellor, trainer read from slides, no mock interviews |
| C | 70 | Medium | Good trainer, but the only batch time eats your evening |

A wins, even at a higher fee. B is the trap: it felt good at the front desk and
was the weakest in the classroom. C is worth it only if you can do your other
study in the morning.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing at the front desk.** The counsellor is not your teacher.
- **One demo, not three.** Day one is often the best lecture of the week.
- **Paying the "today only" discount.** It will be on again next week.
- **Believing placement numbers on the board.** Ask the students.
- **Choosing by distance alone.** Ten more minutes of walking for a much better
  trainer is worth it.

## How to practise

Before your first demo, write your three questions for students on a card: "Is
the trainer good? How many drives did you hear about last month? Would you join
again?" Ask the same three at every institute, so the answers are comparable.
