And then one day the offer is real. This page is about mine: what it was, why I
took it when it was less than I had hoped for, how to read the bond before you
sign, and what I kept doing after I joined.

## Why it matters

The first offer is a strange moment. After months of rejections, anything
feels like rescue, and it is tempting to sign whatever is put in front of you.
At the same time, a friend will tell you it is too low and you should wait for
something better.

Both of those instincts can cost you. Signing blindly can lock you into a bond
you cannot afford to break. Waiting for something better can burn your last two
months of money. The right answer is usually in between: take a reasonable first
offer, read it properly, and never stop preparing for the next one.

## My offer

At the 34th walk-in, out of somewhere between 700 and 1000 candidates, I was
selected. A startup. Here is what they offered.

| What | My first offer, 2019 |
| --- | --- |
| Package | **1.8 LPA** (lakh per annum) |
| In hand | **₹13,000 a month** |
| Working days | **Six days a week**. No Saturday off |
| Bond | **Yes** |
| Orientation | None |
| Training | None. "Watch these Udemy courses" |

::pull-quote
I was happy. I was not satisfied. Both of those were true at once, and I think
that is the right way to feel about a first offer.
::

Why was I happy? Because ₹13,000 a month meant I could live on my own. My sister
had been sending me part of her salary every month for a year, and that could
stop. I had a job title, a company name for my resume, and an answer when people
at home asked.

Why was I not satisfied? Because I knew it was low, the hours were long, and
there was no training at all. I was being paid to learn on my own, six days a
week.

## Why I took it

::pros-cons
---
title: Taking a low first offer
pros:
  - Money coming in, and the pressure on my family gone
  - Real experience on a resume, which changes how every later interview goes
  - A reason to stay in Bangalore, near the walk-ins and the institutes
  - Proof, to myself more than anyone, that I could get selected
cons:
  - Six days a week left only Sunday to study
  - No training, so I learned only what the work needed
  - The bond made leaving early expensive or complicated
  - A low starting salary sets the base the next company negotiates from
---
::

For me the first column won clearly. I would take the same offer again. What I
would not do again is stop there, and I did not.

## How to read a bond before you sign

A **bond** is a clause that says you must pay the company an amount, or face
some penalty, if you leave before a fixed date. Ask for the full offer and the
bond document, take them home overnight, and read these lines.

::steps
### The period
How long does the bond last, and from when: the offer date, the joining date, or
the end of training?

### The amount
How much do you pay if you leave early? Is it a fixed sum, or does it reduce
month by month as you serve the bond? A reducing amount is fairer.

### What it is for
Does it say it covers training? Is there actually any training? A bond for
"training" at a company that gives you Udemy links deserves questions.

### What triggers it
Does it apply if **they** end your job, or only if you resign? Does it apply if
the company does not pay your salary on time?

### Security
Does the company want to keep your original certificates, or signed blank
cheques? Both are serious red flags. Do not agree to either.

### Notice period
How many days' notice must you give to leave, and can it be bought out? This is
separate from the bond and matters as much.
::

::note
I am not a lawyer. Whether a particular bond can actually be enforced in Indian
courts depends on its terms and the situation, and it is a question for somebody
qualified. What I can tell you is to know the numbers before you sign, not after.
::

::caution
Never sign a bond in the room on the day of the offer. Say: "Thank you, I would
like to read this at home and sign tomorrow." A real company will agree. If they
say the offer disappears unless you sign now, you have learned something about
the company.
::

## What I kept doing

This is the part that mattered most.

::timeline{label="The first months of the first job"}
:::timeline-item{title="Joined the startup" date="2019" state="done"}
Six days a week, no orientation, no training. Learning on the job from Udemy.
:::
:::timeline-item{title="Studied every Sunday" date="Every week" state="done"}
The one day off went on revision: Java, SQL, the CS subjects, aptitude.
:::
:::timeline-item{title="Kept interviewing" date="Whenever I could" state="done"}
I did not treat the first job as the end of the search. It was a better
position to search from.
:::
:::timeline-item{title="Selected at Accenture" date="Two months later" state="current"}
Proper orientation, friends, training, and a project. The next part of the
story is in *My story*.
:::
::

Two months after joining the startup, I was selected at Accenture. That did not
happen because the startup job made me better overnight. It happened because I
kept the Sunday routine from the institute months, and kept going to interviews
with a job title on my resume.

Two freshers get the same low offer.

## Common mistakes

- **Turning down a reasonable first offer** while the money runs out, waiting
  for a perfect one.
- **Signing without reading.** You will read it eventually. Read it before.
- **Stopping the routine.** The first job is a door, not the destination.
- **Staying too long out of comfort.** Years later, I found a friend from my
  job-seeker days earning four times my salary, because he had switched and I
  had not. That story is in *My story*, and it is why I say: keep interviewing.
- **Forgetting to say thank you.** Call the person who backed you. Mine was my
  sister.

## How to practise

When you get your first offer, write three numbers on one page before you sign:
the monthly in-hand salary, the bond amount and period, and the notice period.
If you cannot fill in all three from the documents, ask the company to put them
in writing.
