The salary was **1.8 LPA**. That is **₹13,000 a month**. The bond, the six-day
week and the training came after.

**2019. A startup in Bangalore.**

## The numbers

Here is the offer, in full, because I promised the numbers would stay in.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-indian-rupee" title="₹13,000 a month"}
  1.8 LPA (lakh per annum, the yearly figure every offer in India is quoted in).
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-lock" title="A bond"}
  A signed promise to stay a fixed period, with a penalty if I left early.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-calendar-days" title="Six days a week"}
  No Saturday off. Sunday was the only day that was mine.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-graduation-cap" title="No training"}
  No orientation. No onboarding. A list of Udemy courses and a desk.
  :::
::

::note
**LPA** means lakh per annum: the total yearly amount, before deductions. What
reaches your bank account each month (the **in-hand** figure) is lower than LPA
divided by twelve, because of tax, provident fund and whatever else the company
takes out. Always ask for the in-hand number.
::

## Why I was happy anyway

I want to be honest about why that number made me happy, because I think a lot
of advice sneers at a first salary like mine.

My sister was earning ₹15,000 a month and sending me ₹8,000 of it. Thirteen
thousand meant I could live on my own. It meant she could stop. It meant the
month-by-month cost of my job search, which she had been carrying for all that
time, was over.

::pull-quote
Thirteen thousand rupees meant my sister could keep her own salary. That was
worth more than any number on the offer.
::

My sister was not happy. She always thought I deserved better, and she said so.
I took it anyway, and I would take it again.

## The bond

A **bond** is a clause in the offer saying that if you leave before a fixed date,
you pay the company a sum of money, or they keep your original certificates until
you do. Startups and some service companies use them with freshers because they
expect to train you and do not want you to leave the day you become useful.

I signed it, because the alternative was walk-in number thirty-five.

::warning
Read the bond before you sign, every word. How long is it? What exactly do you
pay if you leave, and is it a fixed amount or "training costs"? Are they asking
for your **original** certificates? Keeping originals is the clause that traps
people. Photocopies are one thing; originals are another. The
[interview track](/interview) covers reading an offer line by line.
::

## Six days, and no training

The job was six days a week. Saturday was a working day, every week. There was
no orientation: nobody explained how the company worked, who did what, or what
was expected of me in my first month.

And there was no training. On the first days I was handed a list of Udemy courses
and told to watch them. That was the onboarding.

::compare{caption="What I had imagined a first job was, and what this one was."}
  :::compare-side{label="What I imagined" verdict="neutral"}
  An induction, a trainer, a batch of other freshers, a few weeks of learning
  before real work.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What I got" verdict="neutral"}
  A desk, a list of videos, real work straight away, and Saturday in the office.
  :::
::

It was hard. It was also where I learned that a job teaches you things no course
can, because the problems are real and somebody is waiting for the answer. I
learned more web development in those weeks than in any classroom, because I had
no choice.

## What I would tell you about your first offer

If it is small, it may still be the right one to take. A first job turns "fresher"
into "one year of experience", and that single line changes every conversation
after it.

But take it with your eyes open: know the in-hand figure, read the bond, and do
not mistake the door for the room.

## What this taught me

- A small first salary can be the right offer. Know the in-hand figure, read the
  bond, and take it if it gets you in.
- Getting in is the first step, not the last. [The interview](/interview) track
  shows how to read an offer before you sign it.
