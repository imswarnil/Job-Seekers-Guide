The first thing Accenture gave me was something the startup never had: somebody
explaining how any of it worked.

**2019. Accenture, Bangalore.**

## Selected, again

Two months after the thirty-fourth walk-in, I was selected at **Accenture**. The
salary was better than the startup's; as I remember it, about **3.6 LPA**, with
around **₹24,000 a month in hand**. Nearly double the ₹13,000. Check my memory on
the exact figure; the direction is the part that matters.

::story-media{kind="image" label="Accenture, 2019: the joining day, the ID card, the building"}
The day the second job became real.
::

For a boy from Mahroni who had failed every written round in college, the name
mattered too. Everybody at home had heard of Accenture. Nobody had heard of the
startup.

## Orientation

At the startup I had been given a desk and a list of Udemy courses. At Accenture
there was an **orientation**: days of sessions on how the company works, what a
project is, who you report to, how you log your hours, what the policies are, and
what is expected of you.

::compare{caption="The same job title, two very different first weeks."}
  :::compare-side{label="The startup" verdict="neutral"}
  A desk on day one. "Watch these Udemy courses." Real work straight away, six
  days a week, and nobody to ask.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Accenture" verdict="neutral"}
  Orientation. A batch. Trainers. A structured few weeks before any client
  project.
  :::
::

Neither is better in every way. The startup threw me into real work, and I learned
fast because I had to. Accenture taught me how a large company runs: process,
hierarchy, projects, clients. Both were an education, and I needed both.

::note
A **service company** (Accenture, TCS, Infosys, Wipro, Cognizant and others)
builds and runs software for other companies, called clients. You are hired into
a large pool, trained, and then placed on a client's project. A **product
company** builds its own software and sells it. Freshers in Bangalore mostly
start in services, and that is fine.
::

## A batch, and friends

Here is the part I did not expect to matter as much as it did.

At Accenture I joined with a **batch**: a large group of freshers, all joining in
the same weeks, sitting through the same orientation and training. For the first
time since college I had friends at work. People my age, from towns like mine,
who had also come through walk-ins and PGs and institutes.

::pull-quote
At the startup I had a job. At Accenture I had a batch, and a batch is how you
find out you were never the only one.
::

Those friendships did something practical, too. We compared notes on everything:
who was learning what, which projects were good, who got which rating, and later,
who was switching and for how much. That last part would change my life three
years later.

::story-media{kind="image" label="The Accenture batch, 2019"}
A room of people who had all come the long way.
::

## Being trained properly

After orientation came training in a specific technology stream, with trainers,
assessments and a schedule. After a year of teaching myself around a six-day job,
being taught again felt like a luxury.

I took it seriously for the same reasons I had at JSpiders. Never miss a
session. My own notes. My own examples. The habits did not change because the
company's name got bigger.

## What this taught me

- A large company's orientation and training are worth a lot early on. If you can
  get into one, the structure teaches you how work works.
- The people you join with matter. Stay close to your batch; they are how you
  will hear about the next opportunity. The [interview](/interview) track covers
  the first ninety days.
