Two companies said yes: **PwC** and **Twilio**. I joined **Twilio**, at **30+
LPA**.

**2023. Twilio, Bangalore.**

## Two offers

I cracked **PwC** and **Twilio**. PwC is a large professional-services firm.
Twilio is a **product company**: it builds its own software (tools that let other
companies send messages, make calls and talk to their customers) and sells it.

I chose Twilio, at **30+ LPA**.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-trending-up" color="success"}
₹13,000 a month in 2019. 30+ LPA in 2023. Written down, it looks unreal. It
looked unreal to me while it was happening.
::

::story-media{kind="image" label="2023: the first day at Twilio, the badge or the office"}
The first product company, and the first time the software on the screen was the company's own.
::

Every number in that callout is true, and I want to put the other true number
next to it: it took four years, thirty-three rejected walk-ins before that, three
years sitting still at about 5 LPA, and nine months in a job I had to leave.
None of it was quick. Only the end of it looks quick.

## The role: Salesforce analytics in GTM

I joined as a **Salesforce analytics** engineer in the **GTM** team.

::note
**GTM** means **go-to-market**: everything a company does to find customers and
sell to them. Sales, marketing, customer success, the pipelines and forecasts
that tell leadership how much will be sold next quarter. A **GTM team** builds and
runs the systems behind all of that, and at many companies those systems live in
Salesforce.
::

My job was analytics in Salesforce: turning the data the sales and marketing
teams generated into answers. Which deals were likely to close. Where the
pipeline was leaking. Which campaigns were bringing in customers. The questions
leadership asked on Monday morning.

And every one of those answers was, underneath, a query. Rows in, rows out, and
the question of whether the numbers were right. S. N. Dixit sir had taught me that
in a classroom in 2018. I was using it every week.

::pull-quote
The foundation was the thing. Tools change every few years. The layer underneath
them does not.
::

## Learning GTM engineering

At Twilio I learned a whole field I had not known existed: **GTM engineering**.
It is the engineering side of go-to-market. Building the data flows, automations
and tools that sales and marketing teams run on, so that the numbers are right and
the people selling do not have to fight their own systems.

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-database" title="Data"}
  Where each number comes from, and whether it can be trusted. SQL thinking,
  every day.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-workflow" title="Automation"}
  The processes that move a lead to a deal to a customer without someone copying
  it by hand.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-chart-column" title="Analytics"}
  Dashboards and reports leadership makes decisions from, which means they had
  better be correct.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-users" title="The business"}
  Understanding what sales and marketing actually need, which is not always what
  they ask for.
  :::
::

## What a product company taught me

Twilio is where I learned what a product-company culture actually is, which is
different from what people tell you it is. It was not free food or beanbags. It
was being trusted with a problem and left to solve it. It was being asked "why?"
by people who wanted to know, not to catch me out. After Cognizant, that trust
felt like air.

I stayed at Twilio for **three years and more**. It was the longest I had stayed
anywhere, and the reason was simple: I was learning, I was trusted, and I was
paid fairly. Those three things together are rarer than any single one of them.

## What this taught me

- The big numbers come late and are built on everything before them. If you are
  at the ₹13,000 stage, that is not a sign of where you end.
- The fundamentals outlast every tool. Learn [SQL](/sql) properly; years later it
  may be the thing you use most.
