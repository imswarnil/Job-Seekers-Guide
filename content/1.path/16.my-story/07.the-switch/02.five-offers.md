**2022. Bangalore, evenings and weekends.**

## Preparing while working

I was working full time. So preparation had to fit around the job, and I built
it the way I had built the routine in 2018: small, daily, written down.

::timeline{label="How I prepared, while working"}
  :::timeline-item{title="Weekday evenings" state="done"}
  One topic at a time: Salesforce concepts I used but could not explain cleanly,
  and the Java and SQL underneath them.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="Weekends" state="done"}
  Mock answers out loud, and interviews. Many companies interviewed online by then,
  which made fitting them around work possible.
  :::
  :::timeline-item{title="After every interview" state="done"}
  The notebook. Every question, where it went wrong, fixed before the next one.
  Same method as the walk-ins.
  :::
::

The same notebook habit, four years later, on a better desk.

## What actually changed

Here is the most useful thing in this chapter. My technical knowledge moved very
little in those months. What changed completely was **how I presented it**.

For three years, when somebody asked me about my work, I answered the question
and stopped. Now I went into every interview with a story:

::feature-list{columns="3"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-box" title="One system"}
  One piece of work I had built, which I knew end to end.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-scale" title="One decision"}
  One choice in it I could defend for ten minutes: what I chose, what I rejected,
  and why.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-mic" title="Ninety seconds"}
  "Tell me about yourself", said out loud so many times I had stopped hearing it.
  :::
::

::pull-quote
I had been going into interviews and answering questions. I started going in with
a story, and the offers started arriving.
::

"Tell me about yourself" had been the thing that decided my walk-ins in 2019, and
it was still the thing deciding interviews in 2022. The first four words, and the
two minutes after them.

## Five offers

I cracked **five companies**. The best offer was **15.5 LPA**, three times what I
was earning, and more than a lakh rupees a month.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-trending-up" color="success"}
₹13,000 a month in 2019. About 5 LPA by 2022. Then 15.5 LPA on one switch. More
than one lakh a month, from the boy who had not cleared a written round.
::

The 15.5 LPA offer was from **Cognizant**, and I took it.

I want to be honest about one thing here. Five offers sounds like I had become
brilliant. I had not. I had become prepared: the same person, the same knowledge,
presented clearly and practised out loud. That is available to you too, and it
does not need talent.

## Choosing between offers

With more than one offer, the question changes from "will anybody hire me?" to
"which one?". I chose on the number, mostly. It was the highest, and after three
years of being underpaid, the number mattered.

I would add something now that I did not check carefully enough then: how the
team treats people. Ask to talk to someone on the team. Ask how work is assigned
and reviewed. Read what current employees say. I learned why that matters over
the next nine months.

::tip
When you have two or more offers, ask each company the same three questions
before you choose: who will I report to, how is work assigned and reviewed, and
can I talk to someone already on the team? The answers tell you more than the
CTC.
::

## What this taught me

- Prepare for a switch the way you prepared for your first job: a small daily
  routine, a notebook, and answers said out loud. The knowledge often matters less
  than how you present it.
- Walk in with one system, one decision you can defend, and ninety seconds about
  yourself. [The interview](/interview) track teaches all three.
