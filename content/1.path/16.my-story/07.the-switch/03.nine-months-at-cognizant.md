The rule everybody gives you is "stay at least two years, or it looks bad". I left
Cognizant after **nine months**.

**2022–2023. Cognizant, Bangalore.**

## The number, and the rest

On paper, Cognizant was the best job I had ever had. **15.5 LPA**. More than a
lakh a month. Three times my Accenture salary. My family was proud. I was proud.

::story-media{kind="image" label="2022: joining Cognizant"}
The best number I had ever been paid, and the start of the shortest job on my CV.
::

It did not take long to see what else came with it.

## Micromanagement

**Micromanagement** is when a manager controls every small detail of your work:
how you do each task, when you update them, what you say in every message,
instead of agreeing the goal and trusting you to reach it.

I had three years of real project experience by then. I knew how to do my work. I
did not need to be watched while I did it. But I was, constantly, and so were the
people around me.

::compare{caption="Two ways a manager can run the same piece of work."}
  :::compare-side{label="What I had at Cognizant" verdict="wrong"}
  Every step checked. Every update demanded. Every decision second-guessed. The
  work got slower and everyone got quieter.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What good management looked like later" verdict="right"}
  Agree what done looks like. Let the person do it. Help when they are stuck.
  Review the result, not every keystroke.
  :::
::

And beyond the micromanagement there was **how people were treated**, which is
harder to put in a sentence and was the bigger reason. I will not name anybody or
tell other people's stories. I will say that I watched how people around me were
spoken to, and I decided I did not want to become someone who thought that was
normal.

::pull-quote
The salary was the best I had ever had. I still left, because I could see what
staying would turn me into.
::

## Leaving anyway

I want the nine months on the record, because the advice everybody gives is to
stay two years whatever happens, and it is not always right.

::pros-cons
---
pros:
  - Leaving protected my health and how I thought about work
  - I had kept my interview habits, so I was ready when I needed them
  - The next role was far better, in money and in culture
cons:
  - A nine-month stint needs explaining in every interview afterwards
  - It is a risk if you leave before you have the next offer
  - Doing it repeatedly would make a CV look unreliable
---
::

What made it work was the same thing that had got me out of the startup: I kept
interviewing. The notebook, the answers out loud, the one system and the one
decision. The decision to leave came first; the preparation is what made it safe.

::tip
If you are in a job that is hurting you, you do not have to stay two years. But
look while you are employed, leave to something rather than away from something,
and prepare one honest, calm sentence about why you left. "The way the team was
managed did not suit how I work, and I wanted a culture where I could own my
work" is enough. Never criticise your old employer in detail in an interview.
::

## Explaining it later

Interviewers ask about a short stint, and you should expect them to. I answered
it the same way each time: briefly, honestly, without blaming anybody, and then
turned to what I wanted next. What they are really checking is whether you will
say something bitter about the last place. Do not.

## What this taught me

- "Stay two years" is a default, not a law. If a job is harming you, leave, but
  leave to an offer, not into nothing.
- Prepare one calm, honest sentence about any short stint. [The interview](/interview)
  track covers answering "why are you leaving?".
