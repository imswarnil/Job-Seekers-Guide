Not long after that dinner, the bag was packed. The train was the first thing in
this story I filmed on purpose.

**2018. Mahroni to Bangalore, by train.**

## Leaving

There is no direct, quick way from Mahroni to Bangalore. It is a long journey
across most of the country, the kind you measure in nights rather than hours.

::story-media{kind="video" label="The train from Mahroni to Bangalore, 2018"}
The window, the platforms going past, and me pointing a phone at all of it because I had a feeling it mattered.
::

I filmed a lot of that journey. Partly habit, from the channel. Partly because
some part of me knew that this was the start of something, whichever way it
went, and I wanted a record of what the start looked like.

::story-media{kind="image" label="Photos from the journey: the station, the berth, the bag"}
Everything I owned for the next year fitted under one berth.
::

## What was in the bag, and what was not

Here is what I took with me:

::feature-list{columns="2"}
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-graduation-cap" title="A degree"}
  Computer science, LNCT Bhopal, 2018. Passed. Not understood.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-file-text" title="A CV"}
  One page, and most of it was the degree. No projects I could explain.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-indian-rupee" title="₹8,000 a month"}
  From my sister's ₹15,000 salary. Rent, food and travel had to fit inside it.
  :::
  :::feature{icon="i-lucide-map" title="Directions"}
  The name of an area where job seekers stay, and not much more.
  :::
::

And here is what I did not have: a skill anybody would pay for. I did not know
that yet. I thought the degree was the skill and that Bangalore would recognise
it. That belief lasted about a week.

## What I thought would happen

I want to write down what I expected, because I suspect you are expecting
something similar, and it is worth seeing it next to what happened.

I thought I would arrive, find the walk-ins, attend a few, and within a month or
two one of them would work out. I had a degree in the right subject. The city
was full of companies hiring freshers. The arithmetic seemed simple.

::compare
  :::compare-side{label="What I expected" verdict="wrong"}
  A month or two of walk-ins, then an offer. The degree would carry me.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What happened" verdict="right"}
  A few interviews, all of them showing me I knew nothing. Three months of
  learning from zero. Then 33 walk-ins. The offer came at the 34th, in 2019.
  :::
::

The difference between those two columns is not bad luck. It is that the degree
had never been the thing companies were testing. They were testing whether I
could write Java, write a query, and clear an aptitude round against a clock. I
could do none of the three.

## Arriving

Bangalore station is loud, large and completely indifferent to you. Nobody is
waiting. The city does not know you arrived, and it will not notice if you
leave. I found that frightening for about a day and then oddly freeing: nobody
here knew about the four years of written rounds. Nobody here knew I was
supposed to be a teacher.

::pull-quote
Nobody in Bangalore knew I had failed. That was the first thing the city gave me.
::

::tip
If you are about to make this journey, book the ticket early (sleeper and 3AC go
fast on the long routes), keep your documents and a few printed CVs in the bag
you sleep with, and arrive with a place to sleep for the first week already
arranged, even if it is a friend's floor.
::

## What this taught me

- Expect the first month to show you the gap, not close it. That is normal, and
  it is information rather than failure.
- Plan the journey and the first week before you leave. The
  [Moving to Bangalore](/bangalore) track covers tickets, the first week and
  money, with what it cost when I went.
