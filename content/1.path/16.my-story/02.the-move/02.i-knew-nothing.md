What I actually knew, it turned out, was almost nothing. The interviewers found
that out in minutes. It took me a little longer to admit it.

**2018. Bangalore, my first weeks.**

## The first few interviews

I gave a few interviews in those first weeks, two or three walk-ins. They went
badly, and this time the reason was not mysterious.

The questions were not tricks. Write a program to reverse a string. What is the
difference between an abstract class and an interface? Write a query to find the
second-highest salary. What happens when you type a URL into a browser? These
are the ordinary questions of a fresher interview, and every page of this guide
now teaches the answers. Then, I could not answer them.

::story-media{kind="image" label="The queue outside one of the first walk-ins, 2018"}
A few hundred people with the same folder and the same CV, and me somewhere in the middle of the line.
::

The worst moment was being asked to write a small Java program and realising
that I did not know how to start it. Not the tricky part. The start.

::pull-quote
I had a degree in this, and I could not do it.
::

## Understanding what was wrong

It would have been easy to tell myself the story I had told in college: that I
was not smart enough, that this was not for me. I had four years of evidence for
it.

But sitting in those rooms I understood something more specific, and more
useful. I was not stupid. I was **unskilled**. Those are different problems with
different fixes. Stupid is permanent. Unskilled is a list of things you have not
learned yet, and a list can be worked through.

::compare
  :::compare-side{label="The story I had told myself in college" verdict="wrong"}
  I am not good enough for this. The rejections prove it.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What was actually true" verdict="right"}
  I had never learned Java, SQL or aptitude properly. Nobody gets selected
  without them. That is a gap, and gaps close.
  :::
::

I tried self-study first, from videos and free tutorials, because it cost
nothing. It did not work, and I know exactly why. I had never understood
programming in college, so there was no foundation for the self-study to sit on.
Every tutorial made sense while I watched it and disappeared the moment I closed
the tab. I could not tell the difference between "I do not know this yet" and "I
have misunderstood something three chapters back", and so I could not find which
of the twenty things I half-knew was breaking everything else.

That was when I decided to upskill properly, with a teacher and an order. The
next chapter is how I chose where.

## The decision to write it down

It was in those same weeks that I made the decision this whole guide rests on.

I thought: there are thousands of people in this city in exactly my position.
Small town, a degree they cannot use, no guidance, no money for mistakes. Nobody
had written down for us what to learn, in what order, where to live, how the
walk-ins actually work, what the written round tests. Everybody was working it
out alone, repeating the same mistakes, a year at a time.

So I started documenting. What I studied, what the interviews asked, what the
walk-ins were like, what things cost, what I would do differently. I filmed what
I could and wrote down the rest.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-notebook-pen" color="primary"}
The promise I made myself: if I ever got a job, this record would not stay in a
notebook. It would become a path for somebody like me. You are standing on it.
::

I did not know it would take years. I did not know the job, when it came, would
be at ₹13,000 a month. I only knew that if I got through, somebody behind me
should have it easier.

::tip
Start your own record today. A notebook or a notes app: every interview, the
questions asked, what you answered, what you should have answered. After ten
interviews it becomes the most useful study material you own, because it is the
exact list of what Bangalore asks you in particular.
::

## What this taught me

- "I am not smart enough" and "I have not learned this yet" feel the same from
  inside. Only the second one is true, and it has a fix: [Java](/java),
  [SQL](/sql) and [the interview](/interview), in that order.
- Write down every interview question you are asked. It is the syllabus the city
  is actually setting you.
