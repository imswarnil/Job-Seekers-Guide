I did not miss a single day. That sounds like a boast. It was a strategy, and it
was the most important one I had.

**2018. JSpiders, Bangalore.**

## Three months, three subjects

The course ran for about **three months**, and it covered three things in a fixed
order:

::flow{direction="vertical" numbered}
  :::flow-step{label="Core Java" highlight}
  Variables, loops, methods, then objects, inheritance, interfaces, exceptions,
  collections. The language that almost every fresher interview in Bangalore
  asked about.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="SQL"}
  Tables, queries, joins, grouping, subqueries. The second-highest-salary question
  I had failed, and fifty like it.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Web technologies"}
  HTML, CSS, JavaScript and how a page reaches a browser. Enough to build
  something and explain it.
  :::
::

For the first time, I started to actually understand the language, rather than
memorise the shape of it.

::story-media{kind="image" label="The JSpiders classroom, and my notebooks from the course"}
A classroom that was always full, and the notebooks I still have.
::

## The four habits

Here is what I did in those three months, and it matters more than the institute
did. You can do all four of these wherever you learn, including from this guide.

::steps
### I never missed a class
Not one. A missed class in week two is a gap in week six that you cannot see, and
I had already spent four years building on gaps.

### I made my own notes
Not the handouts. Mine, in my own words, after every class. If I could not write
it in my own words, I had not understood it, and I found out that night instead
of in an interview.

### I wrote my own answers to interview questions
I collected the questions from every interview I had failed, and from everyone in
the PG, and wrote my answer to each one, then said it out loud until it sounded
like me and not like the notes.

### I wrote my own example programs
So that when an interviewer asked me about inheritance, I had a small program I
had written and could explain line by line, rather than the textbook example
everybody else recited.
::

::tip
The last habit is the one that changed interviews for me. An interviewer has
heard the textbook example a thousand times. They have never heard yours, and
you can defend yours when they ask "why did you do it that way?".
::

## Why it worked this time

Self-study had failed me. This did not, and I have thought a lot about why,
because "join an institute" is not the real lesson.

It worked because somebody had sequenced it. Java before SQL. Objects before
collections. Nobody let me skip the part I found boring to reach the part that
felt like progress. And every day I had to explain, in my own notes, what I had
learned, so the gaps showed up in my notebook instead of in front of an
interviewer.

That is what every track in this guide is built to do. The order is decided. The
explanations assume nothing. The questions at the end of each chapter are the
ones I was asked.

## What this taught me

- Attendance and your own notes beat talent. Never skip a day, and rewrite each
  lesson in your own words the same night.
- Write your own small example for every concept you might be asked about. Start
  with [Java](/java) and [SQL](/sql), in that order.
