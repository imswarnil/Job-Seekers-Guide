My father's answer was a government job. It was a reasonable answer, and it
nearly became my life.

**2018. Home, in Mahroni.**

## The conversation

You know this conversation. Everybody from a town like mine knows it. You are
home, the degree is finished, there is no offer letter, and someone at dinner
asks what next.

My father had his answer ready. **BTC**, the teacher-training course, and then a
job as a **government teacher** in Uttar Pradesh. My marks were good enough to get
in. A government job is secure. It is respectable. Nobody in Mahroni would ask a
second question about it.

::story-media{kind="image" label="The house in Mahroni, and the kitchen where this was decided"}
The room where my next ten years were argued over, in about twenty minutes.
::

It was not a bad plan. It was the only plan on the table, and that is a
different thing.

::callout{icon="i-lucide-wallet" color="warning"}
I need to say this plainly, because most career advice is written by people who
never had to think about it. My father could not spend money on a gamble. We did
not have it. That was not a detail of my options. It **was** my options.
::

I said nothing about YouTube. I said nothing about software either, because what
could I say? That I wanted to try again at the thing I had failed at for four
years?

## My sister

Then my sister spoke.

::pull-quote{attribution="My sister, 2018"}
My brother is not going to live here in Mahroni. He is going to Bangalore. He
will study there and attend walk-ins.
::

That sentence is where this whole guide comes from. Somebody in the room refused
to accept the default on my behalf, at a moment when I did not have the standing
to refuse it myself.

She was not speaking from a comfortable position. She was earning **₹15,000 a
month**, and she backed the decision with the only thing that makes a decision
like that real: she sent me **₹8,000 of it** every month while I was in
Bangalore. More than half her salary, for a brother who had not cleared a single
written round.

::note
A **walk-in** is a recruitment drive where a company announces a date and an
address, and anybody who meets the criteria turns up with a CV. No application,
no referral. Bangalore runs them constantly, which is a large part of why a
fresher with no network goes there.
::

## Why Bangalore

It was not a romantic choice. It was a practical one.

- **The companies are there.** More software jobs for freshers than anywhere else
  we could reach.
- **The walk-ins are there.** A fresher with no contacts can get into a room
  with a company without knowing anybody.
- **The institutes are there.** If I had to learn it all again properly, that is
  where the training was.
- **Other people like me are there.** Thousands of graduates from small towns,
  sharing rooms, doing the same thing. You are not the only one in the queue.

My father agreed. My sister, I think, trusted me more than I trusted myself.

## The decision I made privately

I made a second decision at that table, one I did not say out loud. The channel
was not cancelled. It was **queued**. Everything would go into getting a job
first, and once there was a salary, the weekends would be for the camera.

Before I left, I shot my first proper vlog, without the mask.

::story-media{kind="video" label="The first vlog, shot the night before the train to Bangalore"}
A borrowed phone and a haircut I would not defend: the only record of how I sounded before any of this.
::

## What this taught me

- If nobody in your room will refuse the safe default for you, that is a fact
  about your room, not about you. Find the one person who will back you, and
  then make their bet worth it.
- Bangalore is where the walk-ins, the institutes and the people like you are.
  If you are deciding whether to go, [Moving to Bangalore](/bangalore) is what I
  wish I had read at that table.
