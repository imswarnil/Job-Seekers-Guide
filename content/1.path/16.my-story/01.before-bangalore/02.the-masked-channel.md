The thing I cared about in those years was a camera, not a compiler. Here it is.

**2017. A hostel room in Bhopal.**

## The thing I actually wanted

In my final year I found out what I wanted to do with my life. I wanted to be a
YouTuber. A creator. I started a channel, and for the first time since school
there was something I would stay up late for without anybody making me.

And I told nobody.

I made the videos wearing a mask. Not as a style. I did it because I did not have
the courage to say out loud, in my family and in my town, that I wanted to work
in a creative field. Where I come from, a son with an engineering degree becomes
an engineer, or a government employee, or a disappointment. "YouTuber" was not on
the list.

::story-media{kind="video" label="A clip from the masked channel, 2017"}
About a thousand people watched these, and not one person I ate dinner with knew they existed.
::

The channel got to about a thousand subscribers. That is small. It felt enormous,
because every one of them had chosen to watch something I made, and nobody had
ever chosen anything of mine before.

::pull-quote
You can want something very badly and still not be able to say its name at
dinner.
::

## After graduation: the dream and the rent

Then college ended, and the question changed. It stopped being "what do I want
to do?" and became "how will I pay for anything?".

Here is the honest arithmetic I was facing in 2018. A thousand subscribers pays
nothing. YouTube needed years of consistent uploads, equipment I did not own,
and time I would have to fund from somewhere. My family could not fund it. We did
not have that money, and I was not going to ask.

So I had two things in my head that did not fit together:

::compare
  :::compare-side{label="What I wanted" verdict="neutral"}
  To be a creator. To make videos full time. To stop hiding behind a mask.
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What I needed" verdict="right"}
  A salary. Anything that paid rent and let me stop being a cost to my family.
  :::
::

For a long time I thought I had to pick one and kill the other. I did not see
yet that they could be a sequence rather than a choice: the job first, and the
dream on the weekends, once there was money to pay for the weekends.

## What the channel actually gave me

I want to be careful here, because "follow your passion" is the most common and
least useful advice anybody gives a person in my position. The channel did not
pay rent. It did not become my career.

What it did was prove to me, privately, that I could be consistent about
something for a year when nobody was checking. College had never shown me that.
I had passed exams because exams were scheduled. The channel had no schedule
except mine, and I kept it.

That consistency turned out to be the only real skill I carried into the next
four years. Sitting through three months of classes without missing one. Going
back for a walk-in the morning after a rejection. Studying on Sundays after a
six-day week. None of that was talent. It was the same muscle the channel had
built.

::tip
If you have a thing you do consistently, with nobody making you (a channel, a
sport, a notebook, a game you got good at), you already have the habit that a job
search runs on. Point it at the search for a few months. It transfers.
::

## The part I did not say

I did not bring the channel up at home when college ended. I did not bring it up
when the conversation about my future started either. That conversation happened
at the kitchen table in Mahroni, and I went into it with a plan I was too
embarrassed to name.

## What this taught me

- A dream you cannot fund yet is not cancelled. It is queued behind a salary.
  Get the job first; the [guide starts here](/bangalore).
- Consistency without supervision is the real skill a job search needs. If you
  have it anywhere in your life, you have it for this.
