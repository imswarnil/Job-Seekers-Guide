The cloze test was decided by the words that join ideas: but, therefore, as a
result. A **para jumble** takes a paragraph apart at the sentences and asks you
to put it back, and the same joining words are what tell you the order.

## Why it matters

Para jumbles (also called sentence rearrangement) appear in the verbal sections
of Infosys, Capgemini, Accenture, Cognizant, AMCAT-style tests and, at times,
TCS NQT. Usually two to four questions; check the current pattern. They look
slow and they are the question type people skip most, which is a waste: with
the method below most take 45–60 seconds.

## What to study

| # | Type | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Four or five sentences, choose the right order from options | High |
| 2 | First (and sometimes last) sentence fixed, order the middle | High |
| 3 | Find the sentence that does not belong (odd sentence out) | Medium |
| 4 | Six sentences, no options (type in the order) | Low, some online tests |

## The clues, in order of strength

| Clue | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| A sentence with no back-reference | Probably the opening. It introduces the topic by name |
| Pronouns: he, she, it, they, this, these, such | Must come after the noun they refer to |
| "However", "but", "yet" | Follows the claim it turns against |
| "Therefore", "as a result", "so" | Follows the cause; often near the end |
| "Also", "moreover", "furthermore" | Adds to a point already made |
| "First", "then", "finally", years and times | Chronological order |
| A full name, then a surname or "he" | The full name comes first |

## The method

::steps
### Find the opener
Look for the sentence that makes sense on its own: a name, a general
statement, no "this" or "however". Usually only one qualifies.

### Find one fixed pair
A pronoun and the noun it points to, or "however" and what it contradicts. Two
sentences that must sit together.

### Use the options
Check which options start with your opener and contain your pair in the right
order. Often only one survives, and you never need to order the rest.

### Read the survivor once
If it reads as a paragraph, mark it and move on.
::

::tip
Step three is the whole speed trick. You are not solving the jumble. You are
eliminating options, and one opener plus one pair usually removes three of
four.
::

## A worked example

Arrange these into a paragraph.

> **A.** However, the aptitude component proved a better predictor of
> first-year results.
> **B.** The college shortlisted applicants on qualifying marks alone until
> 2019.
> **C.** It therefore now carries thirty per cent of the composite score.
> **D.** This was simple to administer and easy to defend.
> **E.** But school boards varied by as much as fourteen percentage points, so
> the marks were not comparable.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="The order, and how to find it"}
  **B D E A C.**

  - **B** has no back-reference, so it opens.
  - **D** starts with "This", which refers to the marks-only system in B. **BD**
    is the fixed pair.
  - **E** starts with "But" and turns against D's "easy to defend".
  - **A** starts with "However" and introduces the alternative (aptitude) after
    the problem is stated.
  - **C** starts with "It therefore", a conclusion, and "it" is the aptitude
    component from A. It goes last.

  You never needed to understand admissions policy. The connectors did it.
  :::
::

## Common mistakes

- **Reading all the sentences carefully first.** Scan for the opener and the
  connectors. Meaning comes last.
- **Choosing a pronoun sentence as the opener.** "It", "this" and "they" almost
  never start a paragraph.
- **Solving without the options.** When options exist, use them from step
  three.
- **Ignoring the fixed first sentence** when one is given. It tells you the
  topic and often the tense.

## How to practise

- **Daily:** 8 jumbles from IndiaBix "Ordering of Sentences" or PrepInsta.
- **Target:** 60 seconds each in the first week, 45 by the third.
- **Habit:** before choosing, write the opener and one fixed pair in the
  margin, for example "B, then BD". It takes five seconds and stops you
  guessing.
- **Real reading helps:** when you read a newspaper paragraph, notice how each
  sentence points back to the one before. That is the skill.

## Practice questions

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. P: He took a train to Bangalore and found a room in a PG. Q: After graduating, Ravi had no job and no plan. R: There he joined a training institute and studied every day. S: Six months later, he cleared his first written round."}
  **Q P R S.** Q names Ravi and has no back-reference. P's "He" needs Ravi. R's
  "There" needs Bangalore from P. S's "Six months later" closes the sequence.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. A: As a result, the number of candidates who clear it stays low. B: The written round is the first filter at most mass-hiring drives. C: Yet most freshers spend almost no time preparing for it. D: It tests aptitude, reasoning and English in about an hour."}
  **B D C A.** B opens. D's "It" is the written round. C's "Yet" turns against
  how important and broad the test is. A's "As a result" is the consequence of
  not preparing.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. Which sentence opens this paragraph? (a) This made them far easier to compare. (b) Many companies now conduct the written round online. (c) However, some candidates find the screens tiring. (d) They also mark the answers automatically."}
  **(b).** It is the only one without a back-reference; "this", "however" and
  "they" all point to something earlier.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="4. Odd one out. A: Walk-ins are announced on job portals. B: Candidates bring printed résumés. C: The Taj Mahal is in Agra. D: Most walk-ins begin with a written round."}
  **C.** The other three are about walk-in interviews.
  :::
::
