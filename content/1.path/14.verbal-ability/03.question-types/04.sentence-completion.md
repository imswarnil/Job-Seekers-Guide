Fill in the blanks asked for one word, and grammar usually decided it. Sentence
completion widens the gap: a phrase, a clause or two words at once, and the
answer is decided by **logic**, what the sentence is trying to say, more than by
grammar.

## Why it matters

Sentence completion appears in the verbal sections of Infosys, Capgemini,
Cognizant, Accenture and AMCAT-style papers, and TCS NQT uses closely related
formats. Roughly two to four questions when it appears; check the current
pattern. Each should take 30–40 seconds, which makes it one of the cheaper
sections to score in once the method is a habit.

## What to study

| # | Type | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | One blank, a word that fits the meaning | High |
| 2 | Two blanks, a pair of words | High |
| 3 | Complete the sentence with a clause (four endings) | Medium |
| 4 | Contrast and cause-effect signals | High, it underlies all three |

## The signal words

The sentence tells you the direction of the missing part. These words are the
signposts.

| Signal | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contrast | The blank goes against what came before | but, although, however, despite, yet, whereas |
| Continuation | The blank agrees with what came before | and, also, moreover, similarly, in fact |
| Cause and effect | The blank follows from the reason | because, since, so, therefore, as a result |
| Definition | The blank is explained by the rest | that is, which means, a colon or a comma phrase |

::tip
Circle the signal word before you read the options. One word like "although"
removes half the options on its own.
::

## The method

::steps
### Cover the options
Read the sentence only.

### Find the signal
Contrast, continuation, cause or definition.

### Predict in your own words
"Something like *careful*." It does not have to be elegant, it only has to
point the right way: positive or negative, strong or mild.

### Match, then eliminate
Pick the option closest to your prediction. If two are close, put each one into
the sentence and read it whole.
::

## A worked example

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="One blank, contrast"}
  **Although the written round was only forty minutes long, most candidates
  found it ______.**

  (a) brief (b) exhausting (c) simple (d) optional

  "Although" signals contrast with "only forty minutes long". Short, yet it
  felt the opposite of short. Prediction: *tiring* or *long*.

  **Answer: (b) exhausting.** (a) and (c) agree with the first half, which the
  signal rules out. (d) makes no sense.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="Two blanks, cause and effect"}
  **Because the instructions were ______, many candidates ______ the second
  section entirely.**

  (a) clear ... attempted (b) vague ... missed (c) printed ... enjoyed
  (d) detailed ... skipped

  "Because" means the second blank is the result of the first. Bad
  instructions lead to a bad outcome.

  **Answer: (b) vague ... missed.** Test one blank at a time: for the first
  blank alone, (b) fits and so might others, but only (b) gives a cause that
  produces its result. (d) looks possible until you ask why detailed
  instructions would make people skip a section.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="Complete with a clause"}
  **She had cleared the written round at three companies, ______.**

  (a) so she never studied aptitude (b) but she had not yet cleared an interview
  (c) because the tests were cancelled (d) and therefore failed all three

  The first half is an achievement. (a) and (c) contradict it, (d) is
  illogical ("cleared... therefore failed").

  **Answer: (b).** Contrast with "but", and it is consistent.
  :::
::

## Common mistakes

- **Reading the options first.** Every option sounds possible once you have
  read it into the sentence. Predict first.
- **Ignoring the signal word.** It is the answer key, and people skim past it.
- **Solving both blanks at once.** Do the easier blank, eliminate, then check
  the other blank only in the survivors.
- **Choosing the hardest-sounding word.** The setter knows you think the long
  word is right. Meaning decides, not impressiveness.

## How to practise

- **Daily:** 10 questions from IndiaBix "Sentence Completion" or PrepInsta's
  verbal section.
- **Target:** 35 seconds a question.
- **Habit:** for the first week, write your prediction in the margin before
  looking at the options. It feels slow and it makes you fast.
- **Book:** R.S. Aggarwal's *Objective General English* has a long exercise;
  do the first 50.

## Practice questions

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. The interviewer was friendly, ______ her questions were difficult. (a) and (b) so (c) but (d) because"}
  **(c) but.** Friendly versus difficult is a contrast.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. He practised every evening; ______, his speed improved within a month. (a) however (b) as a result (c) although (d) instead"}
  **(b) as a result.** Practice causes improvement.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. The offer letter was ______: every clause was explained in plain language. (a) confusing (b) transparent (c) brief (d) late"}
  **(b) transparent.** The colon introduces the definition of the blank.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="4. Far from being ______, the new trainee asked questions in every session. (a) curious (b) confident (c) reticent (d) talkative"}
  **(c) reticent** (unwilling to speak). "Far from being" signals the opposite
  of asking questions in every session.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="5. The company's growth was ______, so it ______ hundreds of freshers that year. (a) rapid ... hired (b) slow ... hired (c) rapid ... fired (d) steady ... avoided"}
  **(a) rapid ... hired.** Cause and effect: fast growth leads to hiring.
  :::
::
