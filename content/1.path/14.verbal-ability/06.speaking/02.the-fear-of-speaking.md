The last page gave you a daily routine. This page is about the thing that stops
people doing it: the fear of opening your mouth in English in front of someone
and getting it wrong. I had that fear, and I am writing this page for the
person who has it now.

## Why it matters

In the written round, fear costs nothing: you answer on a screen, alone. In
every round after it, fear costs a lot. It makes you speak too fast, or too
little, or not at all. It makes you give a one-line answer to a question you
actually know, because stopping felt safer than continuing.

Interviewers cannot tell the difference between "does not know" and "knows but
is too scared to say it". They mark what they hear.

::note
The fear is extremely common among freshers from small towns and from schools
where English was a subject rather than the language of the classroom. It is not
a sign that you are bad at English. Mostly it is a sign that you have not spoken
it enough times for it to feel ordinary.
::

## What the fear actually is

When I came to Bangalore, my English on paper was passable. Speaking it in
front of people was another matter. The fear was not really about grammar. It
was three smaller fears stacked together:

| The fear | What it sounds like in your head | What is actually true |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Being judged | "They will think I am not smart." | Interviewers meet dozens of nervous freshers. They are listening for content. |
| Making a mistake | "If I get one tense wrong, it is over." | A small mistake you correct and move past is barely noticed. |
| Going blank | "I will stop in the middle and not be able to continue." | The phrases on the last page exist for exactly this. |

Naming which one is loudest for you helps, because each has a different fix.

## What to do

The fix is exposure in small steps: speak English in situations slightly
scarier than the last one, often enough that each step becomes boring before you
move to the next.

::steps
### Week one: alone
Read aloud every day. Record one 60-second answer a day and listen back. Nobody
hears it but you. The goal is to get used to your own voice in English.

### Week two: one safe person
One friend, batchmate or PG roommate. Ten minutes of English a day, about
anything: what you studied, what you ate. Agree that mistakes are allowed and
corrections are kind.

### Week three: small real situations
Ask a question in English at the institute. Order at a café in English. Ask
for directions in English. Each one lasts thirty seconds and nothing depends on
it.

### Week four: pressure, practised
Mock interviews with friends or a mentor, five questions, recorded, with
someone watching. Then real walk-ins, which become the practice themselves.
::

::tip
Treat early walk-ins partly as speaking practice. You will sit many interviews
before you get a job (I went to more than thirty walk-ins before the one that
selected me), and every one of them makes the next room less frightening.
::

## A worked example

**The moment:** the interviewer asks "What is polymorphism?" You know the
answer. Your mind goes blank, and your heart is racing.

::compare
  :::compare-side{label="What fear does" verdict="wrong"}
  "Sir... polymorphism is... sorry sir, I know it but I am not able to
  explain."

  You gave up on a question you knew, and it now reads as "does not know".
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="What the phrases do" verdict="right"}
  "Let me think for a second." (Breathe. Three seconds of silence is fine.)

  "Polymorphism means one method name behaving differently depending on the
  object. For example, a `calculateFee()` method can work one way for a hostel
  student and another way for a day scholar."

  Two sentences, slowly, and you have answered it.
  :::
::

The phrase buys you three seconds. The three seconds are usually enough for the
answer to come back, because the knowledge never left: only the calm did.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting until you feel ready.** The confidence comes after the speaking, not
  before it.
- **Practising only alone.** Recording helps, but the fear lives in front of
  other people, so that is where the practice has to go eventually.
- **Treating one bad interview as proof.** One bad room is one bad room.
- **Memorising to avoid thinking.** It feels safe until the first interruption,
  and then it is worse.
- **Laughing at other people's English** in your PG or batch. It makes
  everyone, including you, less willing to try.

## How to practise

- **Every day:** the routine from the last page, plus the step of the ladder
  you are on.
- **Every week:** move up one step, or stay if the current step is not boring
  yet.
- **Keep a short log:** date, what you did, how scary it felt from 1 to 10. The
  numbers going down is the evidence your brain needs.

## Check yourself

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. Which of the three fears is loudest for you?"}
  Write it down. Judgement: focus on content and slow down. Mistakes: practise
  "sorry, let me correct that" until it is automatic. Going blank: learn the
  phrases for thinking time.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. What step of the ladder are you on today?"}
  If you have never recorded yourself, you are on week one, whatever your
  written English is like. Start there tonight.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. What do you say when your mind goes blank?"}
  "Let me think about that for a second." Then breathe, and give the one-sentence
  answer first.
  :::
::
