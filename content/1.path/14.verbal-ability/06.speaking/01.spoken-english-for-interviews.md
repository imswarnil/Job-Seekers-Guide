The essay was the last thing you write. Everything after the written round is
spoken: the technical round, the HR round, sometimes a separate communication
round. This page is how to be understood in those rooms, and how to practise it
every day when nobody around you speaks English.

## Why it matters

Once you clear the written round, English stops being a section and becomes the
medium of everything. Some companies (Accenture and several other service
companies, for example) have used a separate **communication or versant-style
round**: listening, repeating sentences, reading aloud, answering a question in
your own words. Check the current process for the company. Every company tests
spoken English indirectly in every interview.

Here is what I did not know in 2018: interviewers are not marking your accent or
your vocabulary. They are marking whether they can follow you, and whether you
can keep going when you are unsure. A fresher who speaks simple, slow, correct
sentences does better than one who speaks fast, borrowed phrases.

## What to study

- [ ] **Answer first**: the one-sentence answer before any build-up.
- [ ] **Simple sentences**: subject, verb, object. Short.
- [ ] **Speed**: slower than feels natural. Nerves make everyone fast.
- [ ] **The useful phrases** for not knowing, and for thinking time.
- [ ] **Your prepared answers**: tell me about yourself, your projects, why this
      company. Those live in [the interview track](/interview); this page is
      about how you say them.
- [ ] **A daily routine**, below, because speaking only improves by speaking.

## The shape of a spoken answer

Any question, technical or HR, fits this shape.

::flow{numbered direction="vertical" caption="About 60 to 90 seconds. Answer first, then support it."}
  :::flow-step{label="The answer, in one sentence" icon="i-lucide-target" highlight}
  "An index makes reads faster and writes slower." If they stop you there, they
  have the answer.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="One small, concrete example" icon="i-lucide-minimize-2"}
  From a world they know. "Say the college has one attendance register for
  every student..."
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="The cost or the catch" icon="i-lucide-scale"}
  Everything has one, and naming it shows you have used the thing, not only read
  about it.
  :::
  :::flow-step{label="Stop" icon="i-lucide-square"}
  The hardest part. Silence after a complete answer is fine. Let them ask the
  next question.
  :::
::

::compare
  :::compare-side{label="A memorised definition" verdict="wrong"}
  "A join combines rows from two or more tables based on a related column
  between them."

  Correct, over in six seconds, and it invites "have you actually used one?"
  :::
  :::compare-side{label="Answer, example, catch" verdict="right"}
  "It is how you answer a question that needs two tables at once. Students are
  in one table and exam results in another, linked by a student id. To list
  each student's name with their marks, I join the two on that id.

  The catch is choosing the right kind: an inner join quietly drops students
  who have no result yet, so a report can be missing people and nobody
  notices."
  :::
::

## The phrases worth having

| Situation | Say |
| --- | --- |
| You need a moment | "Let me think about that for a second." |
| You did not understand | "Could you repeat the question, please?" / "Do you mean X or Y?" |
| You are not sure | "I think it is X, but I am not certain. I would check before relying on it." |
| You do not know | "I do not know that yet. Here is how I would find out." |
| You made a mistake | "Sorry, let me correct that." |
| You finished | Stop. No "that's it" or "so, yeah". |

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
Presenting a guess as a fact is worse than saying "I am not sure". The
interviewer will test it, and the collapse costs more than the admission.
::

## The daily routine

Thirty minutes a day. It feels slow for two weeks and then it stops feeling
like practice.

::steps
### Read aloud, 10 minutes
A newspaper editorial or a technical article, out loud, slowly. It trains your
mouth to make English sentences without your brain having to build them.

### Shadow, 5 minutes
Play a short clip of a clear speaker (a news bulletin, a tech talk), pause
after each sentence and repeat it with the same rhythm. You are copying the
pace and the pauses, not the accent.

### Record one answer, 10 minutes
Pick one interview question. Answer it into your phone for 90 seconds. Listen
back once, note one thing to fix (too fast, "basically" six times, the answer
came last), and record it again.

### Speak with a person, 5 minutes or more
Find one other job seeker in your PG or your institute batch and agree on
English-only time: at dinner, or on the walk to class. Correct each other
kindly. Two people practising together improve faster than either alone.
::

::tip
Keep your first recordings. Listening to day 1 on day 30 is the most convincing
proof of progress you will get, and you will need that proof on the days it
feels like nothing is changing.
::

## Common mistakes

- **Memorising paragraphs.** Interviewers hear it in the first sentence, and one
  interruption breaks it. Memorise the points, not the words.
- **Speaking fast to sound fluent.** Fluency is not speed. It is not stopping.
- **Filler words**: "basically", "actually", "like", "so". One recording will
  show you yours.
- **Translating from your first language in your head**, word by word. Reading
  aloud and shadowing are what slowly remove this.
- **Only practising in your head.** Silent rehearsal does not train speaking.

## How to practise

- **Every day:** the 30-minute routine above.
- **Twice a week:** a mock interview with a friend, a batchmate or a mentor,
  five questions, all in English, recorded.
- **Where:** news clips for shadowing, any English newspaper editorial for
  reading aloud, and the questions in [the interview track](/interview) for
  recordings.

## Practice

Answer each in the four-step shape, out loud, recorded, 60–90 seconds.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. What is the difference between a primary key and a foreign key?"}
  **Answer first:** "A primary key identifies a row in its own table; a foreign
  key points to a row in another table." **Example:** student id in the
  Students table, and the same id in ExamResult. **Catch:** a foreign key stops
  you adding a result for a student who does not exist. **Stop.**
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. Why do you want to work in IT?"}
  **Answer first:** one honest reason. **Example:** something you built or
  learned that you enjoyed. **Catch:** what you are still learning.
  **Stop.** No speech about the country's growth.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. What did you do yesterday? (a warm-up used in some communication rounds)"}
  **Past tense throughout.** Three or four simple sentences in time order, and a
  final line about why it mattered. Listen back for tense slips.
  :::
::
