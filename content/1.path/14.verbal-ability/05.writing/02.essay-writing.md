The email was short and had a clear reader. An essay is longer and has no
reader except the marker, which makes it feel harder than it is. It is not a
test of ideas. It is a test of whether you can organise a few ideas in clear,
correct English inside a time limit.

## Why it matters

Some papers include an essay or a longer written-communication task: it has
appeared in AMCAT-style tests (the written English module), in some Infosys and
TCS hiring tracks, and in plenty of smaller companies' own papers. Typically it
is **one essay, 20–30 minutes, around 200–300 words**, on a general topic.
Check the current pattern for the company; many papers have no essay at all.

Where it exists, it is often scored by software plus a rubric, and both reward
the same things: staying on the topic, a clear structure, correct grammar,
reasonable vocabulary and the right length.

## What to study

- [ ] **The five-paragraph structure** below, until you can write it without
      thinking.
- [ ] **A 5-minute plan** before you write a sentence.
- [ ] **Linking words**: firstly, moreover, however, for example, as a result,
      in conclusion.
- [ ] **The common topics** (below), with two or three points ready for each.
- [ ] **Grammar in longer sentences**: subject-verb agreement and tenses break
      most often in paragraph three, when you are tired.

## The structure

| Paragraph | Job | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Introduction | Say what the topic is and give your position in one sentence | 2–3 sentences |
| 2. First point | One reason or idea, with an example | 3–4 sentences |
| 3. Second point | Another reason or idea, with an example | 3–4 sentences |
| 4. The other side | A fair counterpoint, and why your position still holds | 2–3 sentences |
| 5. Conclusion | Restate the position in new words, end with one forward-looking line | 2 sentences |

::tip
One idea per paragraph, and the first sentence of each paragraph says what the
idea is. A marker skimming only first sentences should be able to follow your
argument.
::

## The timing plan

For a 25-minute essay:

::steps
### 0–5 minutes: plan
Write your position in one line. List three points, pick the best two, and one
counterpoint. Note one example for each.

### 5–20 minutes: write
Introduction, two body paragraphs, the other side, conclusion. Do not go back
and edit while you are writing.

### 20–25 minutes: check
Read it once for grammar: every verb agrees with its subject, tenses are
consistent, every singular countable noun has an article. Count roughly to
check the length.
::

## A worked example

**The prompt:** *"Online learning can replace classroom training." Do you
agree? (250 words, 25 minutes)*

**The 5-minute plan:**

```text [plan.txt]
Position: partly. Online learning is a strong addition, not a full replacement.
Point 1: cost and access. A student in a small town can learn from the best
         teachers for little money.
Point 2: flexibility. People who work or look for jobs can study at night.
Other side: classrooms give discipline, doubts cleared on the spot, and peers.
Conclusion: the best results come from combining the two.
```

**The introduction, written from the plan:**

> Over the last ten years, online courses have made good teaching available to
> anyone with a phone and an internet connection. Many people now ask whether
> classroom training is still necessary. In my view, online learning is a
> powerful addition to classroom teaching, but it cannot fully replace it.

Three sentences: topic, the question, the position. Every body paragraph then
supports that last sentence.

::note
You do not need rare words. "Powerful addition" and "cannot fully replace" are
plain and precise. A long word used slightly wrongly costs more than a short
word used right.
::

## Common mistakes

- **No plan.** The essay drifts, and the conclusion contradicts the
  introduction.
- **Writing off topic** because you prepared a different essay. Software checks
  relevance.
- **One huge paragraph.** Structure is part of the mark.
- **Chasing big words.** "Plethora", "myriad" and "in this contemporary era"
  often appear in wrong or clumsy sentences.
- **No position.** "There are advantages and disadvantages" is not an answer.
- **Far under the word count.** Being 30% short usually costs more than a few
  grammar slips.

## How to practise

::steps
### Weeks one and two
Two essays a week, 25 minutes each, typed. Use the common topics below.
After each one, check four things: a clear position, one idea per paragraph,
the length, and every verb against its subject.

### Weeks three and four
One essay a week, plus a 5-minute plan (no writing) for three other topics.
Planning is the part that saves you on the day.
::

**Common topics** in written rounds: online versus classroom learning,
technology and jobs, social media for young people, work from home, a skill
every graduate should have, the role of AI at work, cities versus small towns,
the importance of reading.

## Practice prompts

For each, write the 5-minute plan. Compare with the suggested one.

::accordion
  :::accordion-item{label="1. Should graduates take the first job they are offered?"}
  **Position:** usually yes, with open eyes. **Point 1:** experience on a CV
  opens doors that a gap does not. **Point 2:** you learn how a workplace runs.
  **Other side:** a long bond or no learning can trap you. **Conclusion:** take
  it, keep learning, and keep interviewing.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="2. Is social media good for students?"}
  **Position:** useful if controlled. **Point 1:** free learning and
  communities. **Point 2:** networking for jobs. **Other side:** distraction
  and comparison. **Conclusion:** set limits and use it for a purpose.
  :::
  :::accordion-item{label="3. Work from home is better than working in an office."}
  **Position:** a mix is best. **Point 1:** no commute, more focus. **Point 2:**
  wider hiring beyond big cities. **Other side:** freshers learn faster sitting
  near experienced people. **Conclusion:** hybrid, with more office time early
  in a career.
  :::
::
